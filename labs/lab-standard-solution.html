Standard solutions and calibration curves

Guiding questionHow do we make a solution whose concentration we know exactly, and then use it to measure a concentration we do not know?

Structure 1.4.5

The chemistry behind it

A standard solution is a solution whose concentration is known accurately. You make it by dissolving a weighed mass of solid and making the solution up to an exact volume. Two relationships do all the work:

n = mM    n = CV
n
amount of solute mol
m
mass of solute g
M
molar mass g mol-1
C
concentration mol dm-3
V
volume of solution dm3

Diluting a solution does not change the amount of solute, so C1V1 = C2V2. The full explanations are on the topic page: Concentration.

Why copper(II) sulfate?

Copper(II) sulfate solution is blue. The more concentrated it is, the deeper the blue and the more light it absorbs. A colorimeter measures this as a number called absorbance. Over a sensible range, a graph of absorbance against concentration is a straight line. That graph is the calibration curve.

The solid you weigh is the hydrate, CuSO4.5H2O, M = 249.72 g mol−1. One mole of it gives one mole of CuSO4 in solution, so [CuSO4] = n(hydrate) ÷ V.

You do not need the Beer–Lambert law or any equation for absorbance. You only need the method: standards of known concentration, a graph, a line of best fit, and reading the unknown from the line. The colorimeter here is simulated with a straight-line response.

Aim, variables and safety

Aim. To prepare a 0.250 mol dm−3 standard solution of copper(II) sulfate, and to use it to find the concentration of an unknown copper(II) sulfate solution with a calibration curve.

Equipment

  • Hydrated copper(II) sulfate, weighing boat, spatula, balance reading to 0.01 g
  • Beaker, glass stirring rod, filter funnel, wash bottle of distilled water, dropping pipette
  • 250.0 cm3 volumetric flask (±0.2 cm3) with stopper
  • Two 50 cm3 burettes (±0.05 cm3 per reading), six test tubes in a rack
  • Colorimeter with a red filter, cuvettes, tissue

Wear eye protection. Copper(II) sulfate, solid and solution, is harmful if swallowed and causes eye and skin irritation. Wipe up spills at once and wash your hands after the practical. It is very toxic to aquatic life, so collect all leftover solutions in the labelled waste container and do not pour them down the sink. Fill burettes below eye level, with a funnel, and remove the funnel before you take a reading. Glassware such as volumetric flasks breaks easily: hold the flask by the neck and the stopper together when you invert it.

Method

Part A: the standard solution

  1. Calculate the mass of CuSO4.5H2O needed (15.61 g for 250.0 cm3 of 0.250 mol dm−3).
  2. Weigh a weighing boat with about that mass of solid. Record the mass exactly; it does not need to be exactly 15.61 g.
  3. Tip the solid into a beaker, then reweigh the empty boat. This is weighing by difference: the mass in the beaker is what you use, even if a few crystals stay in the boat.
  4. Add about 100 cm3 of distilled water and stir until all the solid has dissolved. Solid that has not dissolved would not reach the flask evenly.
  5. Pour the solution through a funnel into the volumetric flask. Rinse the beaker, rod and funnel with distilled water and add the rinsings to the flask. This makes sure all the weighed solute ends up in the flask.
  6. Add distilled water until the bottom of the meniscus sits on the mark, with your eye level with the mark. Add the last few drops with a dropping pipette. Overshooting cannot be undone.
  7. Stopper the flask and invert it ten or more times. Otherwise the solution near the bottom is more concentrated than near the top.

Part B: standards and the calibration curve

  1. Fill one burette with the standard solution and one with distilled water.
  2. Run 2.00, 4.00, 6.00, 8.00 and 10.00 cm3 of standard into five test tubes, and add water to make each up to 10.00 cm3. Mix each tube.
  3. Fit the red filter. Put the blank (distilled water) in the colorimeter and set the reading to zero.
  4. Measure the absorbance of each standard. Rinse the cuvette with the next solution before you fill it, and wipe the clear sides with tissue.
  5. Measure the unknown three times and take the mean.
  6. Plot absorbance against concentration, draw the line of best fit, and read the unknown’s concentration from the line.

For “describe how to prepare a standard solution”, markschemes look for: dissolve in distilled (or deionized) water; transfer with rinsings to a volumetric flask; make up to the mark; stopper and invert to mix. “Add 250 cm3 of water” loses the mark.

Run the experiment

Every run gives slightly different readings, as a real one would. Try the good technique first, then change one choice at a time to see what each error does.

Structure 1.4.5

Processing the data

The concentration of the standard solution

A student wants 250.0 cm3 of 0.250 mol dm−3 copper(II) sulfate. They weigh by difference: boat and solid 17.43 g, empty boat afterwards 1.84 g. Calculate the mass they should aim for, and the actual concentration they made. M(CuSO4.5H2O) = 249.72 g mol−1.

  1. Convert the volume to dm3 first, then use n = CV.

    V = 250.0 cm3 ÷ 1000 = 0.2500 dm3
    n = 0.250 mol dm−3 × 0.2500 dm3 = 0.0625 mol

  2. Use the molar mass of the hydrate, because that is the solid on the balance.

    m = 0.0625 mol × 249.72 g mol−1 = 15.61 g

  3. The mass in the beaker is the difference between the two readings.

    m = 17.43 g − 1.84 g = 15.59 g

  4. Report the concentration you really made, not the one you aimed for.

    n = 15.59 g ÷ 249.72 g mol−1 = 0.062430 mol
    [CuSO4] = 0.062430 mol ÷ 0.2500 dm3 = 0.2497 mol dm−3

Aim for 15.61 g. The solution made is 0.2497 mol dm−3 (4 s.f., matching 15.59 g and 250.0 cm3).

Another student uses a 100.0 cm3 volumetric flask. Boat and solid: 7.95 g. Empty boat afterwards: 1.69 g. Calculate the concentration of copper(II) sulfate in the flask.

  1. Mass transferred = 7.95 − 1.69. Then n = m ÷ 249.72.
  2. [CuSO4] = n ÷ 0.1000 dm3.
m = 7.95 g − 1.69 g = 6.26 g
n = 6.26 ÷ 249.72 = 0.025068 mol
[CuSO4] = 0.025068 ÷ 0.1000 = 0.251 mol dm−3

Diluting to make the standards

Each standard is a dilution of the stock, so use C2 = C1V1 ÷ V2, where V2 is the total volume (stock + water).

A test tube holds 7.00 cm3 of 0.250 mol dm−3 copper(II) sulfate and 3.00 cm3 of distilled water. Calculate the concentration of the mixture.

  1. The total volume is 7.00 + 3.00 = 10.00 cm3.
  2. C2 = 0.250 × 7.00 ÷ 10.00.
C2 = 0.250 mol dm−3 × 7.00 cm3 ÷ 10.00 cm3 = 0.175 mol dm−3

Find the concentration of the unknown in mol dm−3 and in g dm−3. M(CuSO4) = 159.62 g mol−1.

  1. Repeat readings reduce the effect of random error.

    mean absorbance = (0.41 + 0.42 + 0.41) ÷ 3 = 0.413

  2. The points lie close to a straight line through the origin. Use two points on the line, far apart.

    gradient = (0.740 − 0.000) ÷ (0.2500 − 0.0000) = 2.96 dm3 mol−1

  3. On the graph you read across from the absorbance to the line and down to the axis. The gradient does the same job.

    [CuSO4] = 0.413 ÷ 2.96 = 0.140 mol dm−3

  4. Multiply by the molar mass of the dissolved substance, CuSO4.

    0.140 mol dm−3 × 159.62 g mol−1 = 22.3 g dm−3

[CuSO4] = 0.140 mol dm−3 = 22.3 g dm−3. The unknown lies inside the range of the standards, so reading it from the line is reliable.

Reading an unknown from a part of the line you have extended beyond the last standard is not reliable: the line may bend at high absorbance. If the unknown is above the top standard, dilute it by a known factor, measure it again, and multiply the result by the factor.

In another run the line of best fit passes through the origin with a gradient of 2.95 dm3 mol−1. The mean absorbance of the unknown is 0.52. Calculate [CuSO4].

  1. Absorbance = gradient × concentration, for a line through the origin.
  2. [CuSO4] = 0.52 ÷ 2.95.
[CuSO4] = 0.52 ÷ 2.95 dm3 mol−1 = 0.18 mol dm−3

The absorbance has 2 significant figures, so 0.18 (or 0.176) mol dm−3.

Uncertainties

Every piece of glassware has an uncertainty, usually printed on it. For a reading on a scale, it is half the smallest division or the precision of the display.

Uncertainty in the stock and in one standard

Use the worked example above: 15.59 g of solid in a 250.0 cm3 flask gives 0.2497 mol dm−3. Standard 2 is 4.00 cm3 of this stock and 6.00 cm3 of water, each from a burette. Find the percentage uncertainty in each concentration.

  1. Weighing by difference uses two readings, ±0.01 g each.

    0.02 ÷ 15.59 × 100 = 0.13 %
  2. 0.2 ÷ 250.0 × 100 = 0.08 %

    Concentration = n ÷ V is a division, so the percentages add.

    stock: 0.13 % + 0.08 % = 0.21 %, so 0.2497 ± 0.0005 mol dm−3

  3. Each burette volume is a difference of two readings: ±0.10 cm3. The total volume is a sum of two volumes: ±0.20 cm3.

    stock taken: 0.10 ÷ 4.00 × 100 = 2.5 %
    total volume: 0.20 ÷ 10.00 × 100 = 2.0 %

  4. C2 = C1 × V1 ÷ V2: multiply and divide, so add all three percentages.

    0.21 % + 2.5 % + 2.0 % = 4.7 %
    C2 = 0.0999 mol dm−3 ± 4.7 % = 0.100 ± 0.005 mol dm−3

Stock: ±0.21 %. Standard 2: about ±5 %. The small volumes in the dilution, not the balance or the flask, control the uncertainty of the standards.

The same absolute uncertainty is a bigger percentage of a smaller quantity. That is why the most dilute standard is the least certain, and why bigger volumes (a pipette into a 100.0 cm3 flask, say) give better standards than a few cm3 from a burette.

Comparing with the true value

Suppose the unknown was made by the teacher as 0.150 mol dm−3, and you found 0.140 mol dm−3.

percentage error = |0.140 − 0.150| ÷ 0.150 × 100 = 6.7 %

The uncertainty of the result is at least that of the standards (about 5 %), plus the reading of the absorbance (0.01 in 0.41, about 2.4 %), so around 7 %. The percentage error is about the same size, so the result is consistent with the true value within its uncertainty. If the error had been 20 %, you should look for a systematic error.

A student makes a standard by running 10.00 cm3 of stock from a volumetric pipette (±0.04 cm3) into a 50.00 cm3 volumetric flask (±0.08 cm3) and making it up to the mark. Ignoring the uncertainty of the stock, calculate the percentage uncertainty in the new concentration.

  1. Find the percentage uncertainty of each volume.
  2. 0.04 ÷ 10.00 × 100 and 0.08 ÷ 50.00 × 100, then add.
pipette: 0.04 ÷ 10.00 × 100 = 0.4 %
flask: 0.08 ÷ 50.00 × 100 = 0.16 %
total = 0.4 % + 0.16 % = 0.56 %

About 0.6 %, much better than about 5 % with burettes into 10 cm3.

Evaluation: errors and improvements

Random errors make the points scatter about the line; more precise glassware and repeats reduce them. Systematic errors move every result the same way; only a better method removes them.

Common errors, their effect, and how to fix them
Source of errorEffect on the resultRealistic improvement
Rinsings of the beaker and funnel not added to the flaskSome solute is lost: the true concentration is lower than the one calculatedRinse beaker, rod and funnel with distilled water and add every rinsing to the flask
Water added past the markVolume too large: concentration lower than calculatedAdd the last few cm3 drop by drop with a dropping pipette, eye level with the mark
Stopped below the mark, or meniscus read from aboveVolume too small: concentration higher than calculatedRead the bottom of the meniscus with your eye level with the mark
Flask not inverted to mixThe solution is not uniform: samples taken from it are too dilute or too concentrated, at randomStopper and invert at least ten times
Mass of solid taken from the first reading only (no reweighing of the boat)Crystals left in the boat are counted: calculated concentration higher than the true oneWeigh by difference: reweigh the boat after tipping
Standards measured with measuring cylindersLarge random error: points scatter about the line, so the unknown is less certainUse burettes, or pipettes and volumetric flasks
Colorimeter not zeroed with the blankEvery absorbance is too high by the same amount: the line does not pass through the origin. A line through the origin would then give an unknown that is too highZero on a cuvette of distilled water before measuring, and check the zero again between readings
Fingerprints, scratches or bubbles on the cuvetteRandom extra absorbance: readings too high and scatteredHold cuvettes by the ridged sides, wipe the clear sides, tap out bubbles
Wrong filter (blue light for a blue solution)Very small absorbances and a shallow line: a large percentage uncertaintyUse the filter of the complementary colour (red or orange for a blue solution)

For each error, give the direction and the reason: “volume too large, so the concentration is lower than calculated”. Then give an improvement that fixes that exact error. The colour wheel in the data booklet tells you which filter is complementary to the colour of the solution.
