Lab 9 · Structure 2.1–2.4

Identifying the type of bonding from properties

Guiding questionWhich simple laboratory tests can show how the particles in an unknown solid are bonded and arranged?

What you need to be able to do

This lab links the four bonding models of Structure 2 to evidence you can collect. All of it is SL and HL core. Tick each item when you can do it without help.

  • Plan a fair set of tests: the same mass of solid, the same volume of solvent, the same circuit, and a distilled-water control.
  • Give real precautions for heating solids, using hexane and using an electrical circuit.
  • Record qualitative observations and ammeter readings in a clear results table.
  • Classify an unknown solid as ionic, covalent molecular, covalent network or metallic from its melting, solubility and conductivity.
  • Explain each result with particles: ions fixed or free to move, delocalized electrons, weak forces between molecules, strong covalent bonds throughout.
  • Calculate the electronegativity difference and average for a binary compound, and link its position in the bonding triangle to its properties.
  • Use repeat readings to give a mean with an uncertainty, and decide whether two readings really differ.
  • Identify the limits of the tests and suggest realistic improvements.

The chemistry behind it

Each bonding model predicts a pattern of properties. You can test three of them with simple apparatus: how easily the solid melts, what it dissolves in, and when it conducts electricity.

The pattern each model predicts

Ionic latticehigh: strong attraction between oppositely charged ionsoften soluble in water; insoluble in hexanenot as a solid (ions fixed); yes when molten or dissolved (ions free to move)
Covalent molecularlow: weak forces between moleculesnon-polar ones dissolve in hexane; polar ones that can hydrogen bond may dissolve in waterno, as a solid or liquid: no charged particles that can move (a few, such as HCl, react with water to form ions, so their solutions conduct)
Covalent networkvery high: strong covalent bonds throughoutinsoluble in bothno, except graphite (and graphene): delocalized electrons
Metallicusually high: attraction between cations and delocalized electronsinsoluble in bothyes, as a solid and as a liquid: delocalized electrons

Metals are also malleable, while ionic crystals and network solids are hard and brittle. A hammer test is a quick extra clue.

For the full explanations, see ionic compounds, covalent substances and covalent network structures, metals, and the bonding triangle.

Bonding is a continuum, not four boxes. The four labels are models. A real substance fits one of them well enough to predict its properties, and the bonding triangle shows where it sits between them.

Equipment

  • Unknown solids A–F, spatula, balance, white tile, small hammer and paper
  • Test tubes and rack, distilled water, hexane (in a fume cupboard), 10 cm3 measuring cylinder
  • Beaker of hot water (water bath), crucible, pipe-clay triangle, tripod, Bunsen burner, heat-resistant mat, tongs
  • Low-voltage d.c. supply (6 V), 100 Ω resistor, digital milliammeter, two carbon electrodes in a holder, leads and crocodile clips

Wear eye protection throughout. Hexane is highly flammable and its vapour is harmful: use it in a fume cupboard, with no flames anywhere nearby, and pour the waste into the labelled organic waste container, not the sink. Heat solids gently at first and point tubes away from people. Sucrose chars and wax can catch fire if overheated: heat wax only in a water bath, and turn off the Bunsen if anything ignites. Crucibles stay hot long after they stop glowing: handle them with tongs and leave them on the mat. Use only a low-voltage supply (6 V), and switch it off before you move the electrodes. Molten metals are a teacher demonstration only. Wrap a solid in paper before you tap it, so that no fragments fly.

Method

  1. Record the appearance of each solid. Why: a metallic shine is a first clue, though graphite shines too.
  2. Wrap a small piece in paper and tap it once with a hammer on a tile. Record whether it flattens, shatters, crumbles or squashes.
  3. Put a spatula of the solid in a dry test tube in a beaker of boiling water for 2 minutes. If it has not melted, put a little in a crucible and heat it with a Bunsen flame, gently and then strongly, for up to 3 minutes. Why: the water bath picks out solids that melt below 100 °C without the risk of fire.
  4. Shake one spatula of the solid with 5 cm3 of distilled water. Repeat with 5 cm3 of hexane in the fume cupboard. Record whether it dissolves.
  5. Set up the circuit. Touch both electrodes onto the solid and record the current. Why: a current in the solid shows electrons that can move.
  6. Rinse and dry the electrodes. Dip them in 10.0 cm3 of distilled water and record the current: this is the control. Add 0.50 g of the solid, stir and record the current again. Why: only a reading clearly above the control shows ions in solution. Rinsing stops ions carrying over from one test to the next.
  7. Where the solid melted in the flame, your teacher may test the conductivity of the melt.
  8. Repeat each conductivity reading three times, rinsing the electrodes between readings.

Run the experiment

Choose a solid and a test, then press “Do the test”. Results go into the table. Repeat a conductivity test to collect more readings. When you are ready, classify each solid and check your answers. “New set of unknowns” gives a different set.

“It dissolves in water, so it is ionic” is a common wrong conclusion. Sucrose dissolves in water too, but the solution does not conduct. Only a solution that conducts more than distilled water shows ions.

Classifying from a results table

Use the table above. Classify P, Q, R and S, and give the evidence for each.

  1. Only metals and graphite conduct as solids, because only they have delocalized electrons.

    R conducts as a solid (59.6 mA); P, Q and S do not

  2. A metal is malleable: layers of cations slide over each other without breaking the non-directional metallic bonding.

    R is malleable → R is metallic

  3. Compare with the control (0.1 mA). Only P’s mixture conducts much more, so only P releases ions that can move.

    P: 23.8 mA ≫ 0.1 mA, high melting point, brittle → P is ionic

  4. A low melting point means weak forces between particles; a solid that does not melt in a flame has strong bonds throughout.

    Q melts below 100 °C, no current → Q is covalent molecular
    S does not melt, insoluble, no current → S is covalent network

  5. Q dissolves in hexane but not in water, so its molecules are non-polar (London forces). P dissolves in polar water, as many ionic solids do.

    every result fits the chosen model

P ionic, Q covalent molecular (non-polar), R metallic, S covalent network.

Link to the bonding triangle

For a binary compound you can check the classification with electronegativity values from the data booklet. Calculate Δχ = |χa − χb| (the vertical axis) and the average (χa + χb) / 2 (the horizontal axis), then read the region from the triangle in the booklet.

Two of the unknowns on the bonding triangle (data booklet electronegativities)
CompoundΔχaverage χRegionFits the tests?
NaCl3.2 − 0.9 = 2.3(0.9 + 3.2) / 2 = 2.05ionicyes: conducts only when dissolved
SiO23.4 − 1.9 = 1.5(1.9 + 3.4) / 2 = 2.65polar covalentyes: covalent bonds, no ions

The triangle tells you the type of bonding, not the structure. Silicon dioxide and a molecular compound such as hydrogen chloride (Δχ = 1.0, average 2.7) are both polar covalent. Only the melting test tells you that silicon dioxide is a giant network.

Potassium iodide was one of the unknowns. Use χ(K) = 0.8 and χ(I) = 2.7 from the data booklet. Calculate the electronegativity difference, Δχ, for KI.

  1. Δχ is the size of the difference: subtract the smaller value from the larger.
  2. 2.7 − 0.8.
Δχ = 2.7 − 0.8 = 1.9
average χ = (0.8 + 2.7) / 2 = 1.75

A large difference with a low average puts KI in the upper left part of the triangle, towards the ionic corner. That fits the tests: it conducts when dissolved but not as a solid.

Tool 3

Uncertainties

Most results in this lab are qualitative. The ammeter readings are numbers, so they carry an uncertainty. The digital ammeter reads to 0.1 mA, so one reading is ±0.1 mA. Repeat readings usually vary by more than this, because the electrodes are never in exactly the same place.

When repeats vary by more than the instrument uncertainty, use half the range of the repeats as the uncertainty of the mean.

Three readings with sodium chloride and water
StepWorking
Readings24.3, 24.9, 24.6 mA
Mean(24.3 + 24.9 + 24.6) ÷ 3 = 24.6 mA
Uncertainty (half the range)(24.9 − 24.3) ÷ 2 = ±0.3 mA
Result24.6 ± 0.3 mA
Percentage uncertainty0.3 ÷ 24.6 × 100 = 1.2 %

Is a reading really different from the control?

Subtract the control. For a difference, add the absolute uncertainties.

  • Sucrose solution 0.1 ± 0.1 mA, distilled water 0.1 ± 0.1 mA. Difference 0.0 ± 0.2 mA. The difference is smaller than its uncertainty, so there is no evidence of ions.
  • Sodium chloride solution 24.6 ± 0.3 mA, distilled water 0.1 ± 0.1 mA. Difference 24.5 ± 0.4 mA, far larger than its uncertainty: there are ions free to move.

There is no literature value to compare with, because the current depends on this circuit, the electrodes and the concentration. Compare currents only within one set-up. A melting point measured with a thermometer can be compared with a data value as a percentage error, as in Lab 3.

A “conducts / does not conduct” conclusion needs a comparison. Say what the control read, and say that the difference is (or is not) larger than the uncertainty of the readings.

Evaluation: errors and improvements

Random errors, such as small changes in where the electrodes sit, scatter repeat readings. Systematic errors, such as ions left on the electrodes, push results one way every time.

Errors, limitations and improvements
Source of error or limitationEffect on the resultRealistic improvement
Ions left on the electrodes from the previous solutionSystematic. The next mixture reads too high, so a molecular or network solid can look as if it releases ions.Rinse the electrodes with distilled water and dry them between tests; check against the control each time.
Tap water used instead of distilled waterSystematic. Dissolved ions make every mixture conduct.Use distilled or deionized water and measure a control.
Electrode distance and depth change between readingsRandom. Repeat currents for the same solution vary.Fix the electrodes in a holder at a set depth; repeat three times and take the mean.
Different masses of solid, or a solid that dissolves slowlyThe concentration of ions differs, so currents cannot be compared.Weigh 0.50 g each time, crush to a powder and stir for the same time before reading.
Flakes of an insoluble conductor (graphite) touching both electrodes in the waterThe mixture seems to conduct, so graphite might be taken for an ionic solid.Let the solid settle or filter it, and test the clear liquid.
A Bunsen flame cannot melt most ionic, network and metallic solidsLimitation: “does not melt” only shows a high melting point. It cannot tell sodium chloride from sand.Rely on conductivity in water; use data booklet melting points; test melts only as a teacher demonstration.
Sucrose chars as it meltsThe melting behaviour is hard to judge.Heat gently in an oil bath with a thermometer, or use a melting point apparatus.

An insoluble ionic solid, such as magnesium oxide, gives the same results as sand in these school tests, because it does not dissolve and does not melt in a flame. You do not need further tests for this in the course: just say that the tests cannot tell them apart. Percentage ionic character is not calculated.
