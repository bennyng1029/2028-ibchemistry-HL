Finding an empirical formula by experiment

Guiding questionHow can the change in mass when a substance is burned or heated tell us the formula of a compound?

What you need to be able to do

Structure 1.4.3 – 1.4.4

The chemistry behind it

An empirical formula gives the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound. To find it, you need the mass of each element in a sample. Then n = m ÷ M turns each mass into an amount in moles, and the ratio of the amounts is the ratio of the atoms. The full method is on the topic page: Empirical and molecular formulas.

Part A: magnesium burning in air

When magnesium burns, it combines with oxygen from the air. The solid gets heavier, because the oxygen atoms now sit in the solid product.

2Mg(s) + O2(g) -> 2MgO(s)

So you weigh the magnesium before, and the oxide after. The increase in mass is the mass of oxygen that combined.

Part B: driving off water of crystallization

Many salts form crystals with water molecules built into the lattice. This is water of crystallization. Blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate loses this water on gentle heating and turns into a white powder.

CuSO4.xH2O(s) -> CuSO4(s) + xH2O(g)

This time the solid gets lighter. The loss in mass is the mass of water. The mass left is the anhydrous salt.

In both parts, the balance cannot see atoms. It sees mass. The whole method rests on knowing which change in mass belongs to which substance: a gain of oxygen in part A, a loss of water in part B.

Linking question: combustion data

The same idea works for compounds of carbon and hydrogen. Burn a known mass completely in excess oxygen and collect the products. All the carbon ends up in CO2 and all the hydrogen in H2O. So n(C) = n(CO2) and n(H) = 2 × n(H2O). If the compound also contains oxygen, its mass is found by difference. You will meet one of these in the exam practice.

Aim, variables and safety

Aim. To find the empirical formula of magnesium oxide, and the value of x in CuSO4.xH2O, from measured masses.

These are measurements, not investigations of a cause and effect, so there is no independent variable to change. What matters is what you measure and what you keep the same.

Equipment

  • Porcelain crucible with lid, pipeclay triangle, tripod, heatproof mat, Bunsen burner, crucible tongs
  • Balance reading to 0.01 g (or 0.001 g if available)
  • About 25 cm of magnesium ribbon, cleaned with emery paper to remove the dull oxide layer
  • About 2.5 g of hydrated copper(II) sulfate crystals; a desiccator for cooling if available

Wear eye protection throughout. Burning magnesium gives out a very bright white light that includes ultraviolet: do not look straight at it, and lift the lid only a little, with tongs. The crucible, lid, triangle and tripod stay hot long after they stop glowing, so move them only with tongs and let them cool on the heatproof mat. Keep the rest of the magnesium ribbon away from the flame. Copper(II) sulfate is harmful if swallowed and irritates the eyes and skin; wash your hands after use and put the residue in the waste container, not the sink, because it is toxic to aquatic life. Do not heat copper(II) sulfate strongly: above about 600 °C it decomposes and gives off sulfur trioxide, which is corrosive.

Method

Part A: magnesium oxide

  1. Heat the empty crucible and lid for a minute, let them cool, then weigh them together. This drives off any moisture, so the first reading is a true starting mass.
  2. Coil the cleaned magnesium ribbon loosely, put it in the crucible, and weigh crucible, lid and magnesium together.
  3. Put the crucible on the pipeclay triangle with the lid on. Heat gently at first, then strongly.
  4. Every minute or so, lift the lid slightly with tongs for a moment, then put it back. Lifting lets fresh air in, so there is oxygen for the reaction. Putting it back stops the white smoke of magnesium oxide escaping.
  5. When the magnesium no longer glows when the lid is lifted, take the lid off and heat strongly for two more minutes.
  6. Let everything cool, then weigh crucible, lid and contents.
  7. Heat again for two minutes, cool and reweigh. Repeat until two readings in a row agree to within 0.01 g. This is heating to constant mass. It shows that the reaction is complete.

Part B: hydrated copper(II) sulfate

  1. Weigh a clean, dry crucible.
  2. Add about 2.5 g of the blue crystals and weigh again.
  3. Heat gently, with the lid off, stirring now and then with a glass rod. Gentle heating stops the crystals spitting out, and stops the anhydrous salt decomposing.
  4. When the solid is white all through, let it cool in a desiccator, then weigh. Anhydrous copper(II) sulfate takes water back from the air, so it should not cool on the open bench.
  5. Heat, cool and weigh again until two readings agree to within 0.01 g.

“Heat to constant mass” earns its mark only with the detail: heat, cool, weigh, and repeat until two consecutive masses are the same (within the balance’s precision). Then say why: all the magnesium has reacted, or all the water has been removed.

Run the experiment

Each run uses a new piece of apparatus and a new sample, so your readings change a little from run to run, just as in a real lab. Work through the buttons from left to right. Write your readings down, or copy the raw data table, before you process them.

Processing the data

The route is always the same: masses of each part, then amounts in moles, then divide by the smallest amount, then a whole-number ratio. Use the data booklet Ar values: Mg = 24.31, O = 16.00, H = 1.01, S = 32.07, Cu = 63.55.

Determine the empirical formula of magnesium oxide.

  1. Use the last, constant reading. The gain in mass is the oxygen that combined.

    mass of Mg = 26.78 g − 26.42 g = 0.36 g
    mass of O = 27.00 g − 26.78 g = 0.22 g

  2. Atoms combine in a ratio of numbers, not of masses, so use n = m ÷ M for each element.

    n(Mg) = 0.36 g ÷ 24.31 g mol−1 = 0.01481 mol
    n(O) = 0.22 g ÷ 16.00 g mol−1 = 0.01375 mol

  3. This makes the smaller number exactly 1, so the ratio is easy to read.

    Mg : O = 0.01481 ÷ 0.01375 : 0.01375 ÷ 0.01375 = 1.08 : 1
    or, as O ÷ Mg: 0.01375 ÷ 0.01481 = 0.93

  4. 1.08 : 1 is close to 1 : 1. The gap is small enough to be experimental error (see the uncertainty section). It is not close to a real ratio such as 3 : 2 = 1.5 : 1.

    Mg : O ≈ 1 : 1, so the empirical formula is MgO

MgO (measured ratio Mg : O = 1.08 : 1)

Using the masses directly (0.36 : 0.22) instead of moles gives a ratio that means nothing. Another slip is to take the mass of oxygen from the first heating reading. Always use the reading where the mass has become constant.

Now you: the value of x in a hydrate

For a hydrate, the two “parts” are the anhydrous salt and the water. Treat CuSO4 as one unit with M = 159.62 g mol−1, and water with M = 18.02 g mol−1. Then x = n(H2O) ÷ n(CuSO4).

A student heats hydrated copper(II) sulfate. Crucible: 18.47 g. Crucible and hydrate: 21.03 g. After heating: 20.25 g, 20.14 g, 20.12 g, 20.12 g. Calculate the value of x in CuSO4.xH2O from these data, before rounding to a whole number.

  1. Mass of anhydrous salt = final mass − crucible. Mass of water = (crucible + hydrate) − final mass.
  2. Anhydrous: 1.65 g; water: 0.91 g. Now find n of each and divide n(water) by n(CuSO4).
mass of CuSO4 = 20.12 g − 18.47 g = 1.65 g
mass of water = 21.03 g − 20.12 g = 0.91 g
n(CuSO4) = 1.65 ÷ 159.62 = 0.010337 mol
n(H2O) = 0.91 ÷ 18.02 = 0.050499 mol
x = 0.050499 ÷ 0.010337 = 4.89

x = 4.9 to 2 significant figures (0.91 g has only 2). The nearest whole number is 5, so the formula is CuSO4.5H2O.

Approximation is a judgement, not a rule. Round 0.93 or 4.89 to the nearest whole number only when the difference is within your experimental uncertainty. A ratio such as 1 : 1.50 or 1 : 1.33 is real: multiply by 2 or 3 to get whole numbers (2 : 3, 3 : 4).

Uncertainties

A balance that reads to 0.01 g has an uncertainty of ±0.01 g in every reading. The masses you use are differences of two readings, so their uncertainties add.

Adding or subtractingabsolute uncertainties(26.78 ± 0.01) − (26.42 ± 0.01) = 0.36 ± 0.02 g
Multiplying or dividingpercentage uncertaintiesratio = n(O) ÷ n(Mg): add the % uncertainties of the two masses

The molar masses come from the data booklet. Their uncertainty is tiny compared with the balance, so you can ignore it.

How sure is the magnesium oxide ratio?

Use the part A data above (balance ±0.01 g). Find the uncertainty in the ratio n(O) ÷ n(Mg) = 0.93, and compare it with the expected value of 1.00 for MgO.

  1. Each mass is a difference of two readings, each ±0.01 g.

    mass of Mg = 0.36 ± 0.02 g
    mass of O = 0.22 ± 0.02 g

  2. Percentage uncertainty = absolute uncertainty ÷ value × 100.

    Mg: 0.02 ÷ 0.36 × 100 = 5.6 %
    O: 0.02 ÷ 0.22 × 100 = 9.1 %

  3. n = m ÷ M carries the same percentage as the mass, and a ratio is a division, so the percentages add.

    ratio: 5.6 % + 9.1 % = 14.7 % ≈ 15 %
    0.93 × 0.15 = 0.14, so ratio = 0.93 ± 0.14

  4. Percentage error = |experimental − expected| ÷ expected × 100.

    percentage error = |0.93 − 1.00| ÷ 1.00 × 100 = 7 %
    7 % < 15 %: the difference lies inside the uncertainty

Ratio = 0.93 ± 0.14 (about ±15 %). The result agrees with MgO within its uncertainty. Random error from the balance is large enough to explain the whole gap.

Quote an absolute uncertainty to one significant figure and the value to the same decimal place: 0.9 ± 0.1, not 0.93 ± 0.14, in a final answer. When the percentage error is bigger than the percentage uncertainty, random error alone cannot explain it: look for a systematic error.

The magnesium experiment uses small masses, so a 0.01 g reading is a large fraction of each. A balance reading to 0.001 g makes each mass about ten times more precise. A longer piece of ribbon helps too. Try both in the experiment above.

Use the hydrate data from the “Try it” in the processing section (anhydrous salt 1.65 g, water 0.91 g; balance ±0.01 g). Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the value of x.

  1. Each mass is a difference of two readings, so each has an absolute uncertainty of ±0.02 g.
  2. x is a ratio of the two amounts. Add the two percentage uncertainties: 0.02 ÷ 0.91 × 100 and 0.02 ÷ 1.65 × 100.
water: 0.02 ÷ 0.91 × 100 = 2.20 %
anhydrous salt: 0.02 ÷ 1.65 × 100 = 1.21 %
x: 2.20 % + 1.21 % = 3.4 %

So x = 4.89 ± 0.17, or 4.9 ± 0.2. The expected value 5 lies inside this range, and the percentage error (2.3 %) is smaller than the uncertainty.

Evaluation: errors and improvements

A random error scatters results both ways; repeats and a more precise balance reduce it. A systematic error pushes every result the same way; only a change of method removes it. In these experiments most of the important errors are systematic.

An evaluation point earns marks when it has three parts: the error, its direction (too high or too low, and why), and an improvement that fixes that error. “Human error” and “be more careful” earn nothing.

You do not need to learn the reaction of magnesium with nitrogen or the decomposition of copper(II) sulfate for the exam. They are here only to explain the errors. You are expected to reason about the direction of an error from the data, not to recall these equations.
