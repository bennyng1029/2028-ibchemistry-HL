The chemistry behind it

Heat or an electric discharge gives atoms energy. An electron moves up to a higher energy level. The atom is now in an excited state.

The electron soon falls back to a lower energy level. As it falls, the atom emits a photon. The energy of the photon equals the difference in energy between the two levels.

The energy levels are fixed, so only certain energy differences are possible. Each atom therefore emits light of only certain wavelengths. Through a spectroscope you see a line spectrum: separate bright lines on a dark background. A hot solid, such as a lamp filament, gives a continuous spectrum: every colour, with no gaps.

Every element has its own set of energy levels, so every element has its own pattern of lines. That pattern identifies the element.

  • Shorter wavelength means higher frequency and more energy per photon.
  • Across the visible spectrum, violet light (about 400 nm) has the most energy per photon and red light (about 700 nm) the least.

The full story is on the topic page: emission spectra and the hydrogen spectrum.

Turning a wavelength into an energy

At HL you also calculate the energy. Two equations from the data booklet link wavelength, frequency and energy.

c = λf    E = hf
c
speed of light, 3.00 × 108 m s-1
λ
wavelength m
f
frequency s-1 (Hz)
E
energy of one photon J
h
Planck constant, 6.63 × 10−34 J s

The lines of a series get closer together at higher frequency and finally merge. This limit of convergence corresponds to the electron leaving the atom. For the series that ends on the ground state, its energy per mole is the first ionization energy. See convergence and ionization.

The guide does not ask you to learn flame colours or the wavelengths of the lines of any element. In this lab they are practice data. In an exam the colours or the reference lines are given to you.

Equipment

  • Bunsen burner, heat-resistant mat
  • Nichrome wire loop in an insulated handle (one for each salt, if you can)
  • Dilute hydrochloric acid in a small beaker or watch glass, for cleaning the wire
  • Solid samples on watch glasses: lithium chloride, sodium chloride, potassium chloride, calcium chloride, strontium chloride, copper(II) chloride; three unknown salts
  • Hand spectroscope with a wavelength scale marked every 10 nm
  • Filament lamp; hydrogen, helium and mercury discharge tubes with their power supply (set up by your teacher)

Eye protection throughout. Hydrochloric acid is an irritant: use only a few cm3 of dilute acid and wipe up spills at once.

The wire and its holder get very hot. Hold it only by the insulated handle and rest it on the heat-resistant mat. Tie back long hair near the Bunsen flame.

Copper(II) chloride is harmful if swallowed, and lithium and strontium chlorides are irritants. Use tiny amounts and wash your hands afterwards.

Discharge tubes run at several thousand volts and get hot. Only your teacher switches them on. Do not touch the tube or its connections. Never point a spectroscope at the Sun.

Method

Part A: flame tests

  1. Light the Bunsen burner and open the air hole fully to get a blue, non-luminous flame. A yellow flame would hide the colours you are looking for.
  2. Dip the wire loop in dilute hydrochloric acid, then hold it in the hottest part of the flame. Repeat until the wire gives no colour to the flame. This blank shows the wire is clean.
  3. Dip the clean loop in the acid and then into the first salt, so a few grains stick to it.
  4. Hold the loop at the edge of the flame. Record the colour straight away, in words. Some colours last only a second.
  5. Clean the wire again (step 2) before every new salt. Traces of the last salt, or of sodium from your fingers, change the colour.
  6. Test each known salt, then each unknown. Compare each unknown with the known salts under the same conditions.

Part B: emission spectra

  1. Look at the filament lamp through the spectroscope. You should see a continuous spectrum: a check that the spectroscope works.
  2. Look at the hydrogen discharge tube. Line up each bright line with the cross-hair and read the scale. These lines have known wavelengths, so they show whether the scale reads correctly.
  3. Record the wavelength of every line you can see for each tube and each flame. Give each reading with its uncertainty.
  4. Correct your readings using the result of step 8. Then compare the pattern of lines with reference data to identify each element.

Processing the data

Correcting the readings and identifying the element

A cheap spectroscope often has a scale that is slightly out of place. Every reading is then wrong by the same amount. This is a systematic error. You find it with a source whose lines you know, and then correct every other reading.

Calibrate, correct, identify

Through one spectroscope, the hydrogen tube gave lines at 413, 437, 489 and 659 nm (each ±2 nm). An unknown tube gave lines at 408, 439, 549 and 581 nm. Identify the element in the unknown tube.

  1. Hydrogen’s lines are known, so any difference is an error in the scale.

    413 − 410 = 3; 437 − 434 = 3; 489 − 486 = 3; 659 − 656 = 3

  2. Every reading is too high by the same amount. That is a systematic error, not random scatter.

    correction = −3 nm

  3. Apply the same correction to every reading from this spectroscope.

    408 − 3 = 405; 439 − 3 = 436; 549 − 3 = 546; 581 − 3 = 578 nm

  4. One line can match more than one element (436 is close to hydrogen’s 434). Only mercury has all four lines, and hydrogen’s 486 and 656 nm lines are missing.

    405, 436, 546, 578 nm = mercury

The unknown tube contains mercury.

Another spectroscope shows the hydrogen lines at 653 and 483 nm. Through the same spectroscope, a lithium salt in a flame shows a bright red line at 668 nm. Calculate the corrected wavelength of the red line.

  1. Compare the hydrogen readings with the data: 656 and 486 nm. Is the scale reading too high or too low, and by how much?
  2. The scale reads 3 nm too low, so add 3 nm to every reading.
656 − 653 = 3; 486 − 483 = 3, so the scale reads 3 nm too low
corrected = 668 + 3 = 671 nm (lithium’s red line)

From a wavelength to an energy

Energy of the lithium red line

The corrected wavelength of lithium’s red line is 671 nm. Calculate the frequency, the energy of one photon, and the energy of one mole of these photons in kJ mol−1.

  1. The speed of light is in m s−1, so the wavelength must be in m.

    λ = 671 nm = 671 × 10−9 m = 6.71 × 10−7 m

  2. Rearrange c = λf.

    f = c / λ = 3.00 × 108 / 6.71 × 10−7 = 4.471 × 1014 s−1

  3. Use E = hf. Keep the unrounded frequency.

    E = 6.63 × 10−34 × 4.471 × 1014 = 2.964 × 10−19 J

  4. Multiply by the Avogadro constant, then divide by 1000 to change J into kJ.

    E = 2.964 × 10−19 × 6.02 × 1023 = 1.784 × 105 J mol−1

  5. The wavelength has 3 significant figures.

    f = 4.47 × 1014 s−1; E = 2.96 × 10−19 J; 178 kJ mol−1

4.47 × 1014 s−1; 2.96 × 10−19 J per photon; 178 kJ mol−1.

Sodium gives a yellow line at 589 nm. Calculate the energy, in kJ mol−1, of one mole of photons of this light.

  1. Change 589 nm into metres, then find the frequency with f = c / λ.
  2. Find E = hf for one photon, multiply by 6.02 × 1023 and divide by 1000.
f = 3.00 × 108 / 5.89 × 10−7 = 5.093 × 1014 s−1
E = 6.63 × 10−34 × 5.093 × 1014 = 3.377 × 10−19 J
3.377 × 10−19 × 6.02 × 1023 / 1000 = 203 kJ mol−1

Uncertainties

The spectroscope reading

The scale is marked every 10 nm and each line has some width. You can judge the centre of a line to about ±2 nm. Write every reading with this absolute uncertainty, for example 589 ± 2 nm.

The percentage uncertainty is the absolute uncertainty divided by the reading, × 100:

percentage uncertainty = 2 / 589 × 100 = 0.34 %

The uncertainty is small compared with the gaps between most lines. It is not small enough to separate lines that are closer than about 4 nm, such as helium’s 588 nm line and sodium’s 589 nm line. Then you must use the rest of the pattern.

Comparing with the literature value

Suppose you read sodium’s line as 593 ± 2 nm. The data value is 589 nm.

percentage error = (593 − 589) / 589 × 100 = 0.68 %

The difference, 4 nm, is bigger than the uncertainty of 2 nm. So random error alone cannot explain it. There is probably a systematic error: the scale needs calibrating. If the difference were 2 nm or less, the reading would agree with the data within its uncertainty.

Uncertainty in a calculated energy

The energy is E = hc / λ. The constants are treated as exact, so the percentage uncertainty in E is the same as in λ.

671 ± 2 nm: 2 / 671 × 100 = 0.30 %; 0.30 % of 178 kJ mol−1 = 0.5 kJ mol−1

So the energy is 178.4 ± 0.5 kJ mol−1. Give the absolute uncertainty to one significant figure and the value to the same decimal place.

Reliability of the flame colours

A flame colour is a qualitative observation, so it has no ± value. You make it reliable in other ways.

  • Blank: heat the clean wire on its own first. If the flame stays blue, any colour later comes from the salt.
  • Known samples: test the known salts under the same conditions, and compare each unknown with them, not with a colour from memory.
  • Repeats: test each salt at least twice, with the wire cleaned in between. A second observer should agree with your description.
  • Confirm with a spectrum: when two ions give similar colours, the pattern of lines settles it.

Evaluation: errors and improvements

Random errors make repeated readings scatter on both sides of the true value, such as judging the centre of a blurred line. Repeating and averaging reduces their effect. Systematic errors push every reading the same way, such as a scale that is out of place. Repeating does not help; calibration does.
