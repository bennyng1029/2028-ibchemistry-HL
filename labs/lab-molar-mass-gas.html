Molar volume and the molar mass of a gas

Guiding questionHow can a mass, a volume, a temperature and a pressure be combined, through the ideal gas equation, to give the molar mass of a gas?

What you need to be able to do

These are practical skills. Tick each one when you can do it on your own.

  • Convert cm3 to m3, kPa to Pa and °C to K before using PV = nRT.
  • Find the molar volume of hydrogen at room conditions from magnesium and excess acid.
  • Convert a measured molar volume to STP and compare it with 22.7 dm3 mol−1.
  • Calculate the molar mass of a volatile liquid with M = mRT / PV.
  • Find a molar volume or a molar mass from the gradient of a graph.
  • Propagate uncertainties through M = mRT / PV and compare them with the percentage error.
  • Explain whether a named error makes the result too high or too low, and suggest an improvement.
Structure 1.5.4 · 1.4.3

The chemistry behind it

The ideal gas equation links the amount of a gas to things you can measure: its pressure, volume and temperature. The amount also equals mass ÷ molar mass. Put the two together and you can find the molar mass of a gas from four measurements.

PV = nRT and n = mM, so M = mRTPV
M

mass of the gas g

The molar volume is the volume of one mole of gas, Vm = V ÷ n. For an ideal gas it is the same for every gas at the same temperature and pressure. At STP (273.15 K, 100 kPa) it is 22.7 dm3 mol−1. A laboratory is warmer than 0 °C, so the molar volume there is larger, about 24 to 25 dm3 mol−1. Work it out from RT / P; it is not in the data booklet.

In Part A, magnesium reacts with excess hydrochloric acid. One mole of magnesium gives one mole of hydrogen:

Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)

In Part B, a weighed sample of a liquid with a low boiling point is turned into vapour inside a heated gas syringe.

More on the topic pages: the ideal gas equation, molar mass of a gas, molar volume at STP and Avogadro’s law.

Aim, variables and safety

Aim. Part A: find the molar volume of hydrogen at room temperature and pressure, and compare it with the ideal gas value. Part B: find the molar mass of an unknown volatile liquid and identify it.

Equipment

  • Part A: magnesium ribbon and sandpaper, balance (±0.001 g), 50 cm3 of 1.0 mol dm−3 hydrochloric acid, conical flask, small test tube to stand inside it, bung and delivery tube, 100 cm3 gas syringe (±0.5 cm3), thermometer (±0.5 °C), barometer or digital pressure sensor (±0.1 kPa)
  • Part B: 100 cm3 gas syringe in an electric heating jacket (or a steam jacket), self-sealing rubber cap, 1 cm3 hypodermic syringe, balance (±0.001 g), thermometer, barometer, the unknown liquid

Hydrogen is extremely flammable and forms explosive mixtures with air: no flames anywhere near Part A, and release the gas only into a well-ventilated room. Hydrochloric acid at 1.0 mol dm−3 irritates the eyes and skin, so wear eye protection and rinse any splashes with plenty of water. The volatile liquids in Part B (such as propanone or hexane) are highly flammable and their vapours are harmful: use a fume cupboard, heat only with the electric or steam jacket, never a Bunsen burner. The heating jacket is hot enough to burn. Point the hypodermic needle away from people and put it in a sharps container after use.

Method

Part A: molar volume of hydrogen

  1. Rub a length of magnesium ribbon with sandpaper until it is shiny. Why: the dull coating is magnesium oxide. It adds to the mass but gives no hydrogen.
  2. Cut and weigh a piece of about 0.07 g. Why: at room conditions 0.1 g of magnesium gives about 100 cm3 of hydrogen, which would fill the syringe.
  3. Put 50 cm3 of 1.0 mol dm−3 hydrochloric acid in the flask. Stand the magnesium in a small dry test tube inside the flask. Why: the acid is in large excess, so all the magnesium reacts.
  4. Fit the bung joined to the gas syringe and check that the syringe reads zero. Then tip the flask so the acid reaches the magnesium. Why: no hydrogen escapes before the apparatus is sealed.
  5. When the fizzing stops, wait until the flask has cooled to room temperature, then read the volume. Why: the reaction gives out heat, and warm gas takes up more space.
  6. Record the room temperature and the atmospheric pressure. Repeat with different masses of magnesium.

Part B: molar mass of a volatile liquid

  1. Heat the gas syringe in its jacket to a steady temperature at least 20 °C above the boiling point of the liquid. Let a few cm3 of air warm up inside, and record this starting volume. Why: all the liquid must turn into vapour, and the vapour should be far from condensing.
  2. Draw about 0.15 cm3 of the liquid into the hypodermic syringe and weigh it. Why: the vapour must not overfill the gas syringe.
  3. Push the needle through the self-sealing cap, inject the liquid, withdraw the needle and reweigh the hypodermic syringe at once. Why: the mass injected is the difference between the two weighings.
  4. Wait until the plunger stops moving. Record the final volume and the jacket temperature. Why: the vapour must reach the jacket temperature.
  5. Record the atmospheric pressure. Repeat with different volumes of liquid at the same jacket temperature.

Another common version uses butane from a gas lighter. Weigh the lighter, release the gas under water into an upturned measuring cylinder full of water, dry the lighter and weigh it again. The data are processed in exactly the same way.

Processing the data

Molar volume of hydrogen

0.0742 g of magnesium reacts with excess hydrochloric acid. The hydrogen has a volume of 76.5 cm3 at 23.0 °C and 100.6 kPa. Calculate the molar volume of hydrogen under these conditions, and convert it to STP.

  1. Use Ar(Mg) = 24.31 from the data booklet.

    n(Mg) = 0.0742 g ÷ 24.31 g mol−1 = 3.052… × 10−3 mol

  2. The equation shows 1 mol of Mg gives 1 mol of H2.

    n(H2) = 3.052… × 10−3 mol

  3. Convert the volume to dm3 to get the usual unit.

    Vm = 0.0765 dm3 ÷ 3.052… × 10−3 mol = 25.06… dm3 mol−1 ≈ 25.1 dm3 mol−1

  4. Use the combined gas law, P1V1 / T1 = P2V2 / T2, with temperatures in kelvin. STP is 100 kPa and 273.15 K.

    Vm(STP) = 25.06 × (100.6 ÷ 100) × (273.15 ÷ 296.15) = 23.3 dm3 mol−1

  5. Percentage error = |experimental − literature| ÷ literature × 100.

    percentage error = (23.26 − 22.7) ÷ 22.7 × 100 = 2.4 %

25.1 dm3 mol−1 at 23.0 °C and 100.6 kPa, which is 23.3 dm3 mol−1 at STP: 2.4 % above 22.7 dm3 mol−1.

0.0585 g of magnesium gives 59.0 cm3 of hydrogen with excess acid. Calculate the molar volume of hydrogen under the conditions of the experiment, in dm3 mol−1.

  1. Find the amount of magnesium. It equals the amount of hydrogen.
  2. n = 0.0585 ÷ 24.31. Then divide the volume in dm3 by that amount.
n(H2) = n(Mg) = 0.0585 ÷ 24.31 = 2.406 × 10−3 mol
Vm = 0.0590 dm3 ÷ 2.406 × 10−3 mol = 24.5 dm3 mol−1

Molar mass of a volatile liquid

A hypodermic syringe weighs 12.483 g before and 12.331 g after injecting a liquid into a gas syringe at 99.0 °C. The gas syringe reading rises from 4.5 cm3 to 84.0 cm3. The atmospheric pressure is 101.0 kPa. Calculate the molar mass of the liquid.

  1. Both come from the difference between two readings.

    m = 12.483 − 12.331 = 0.152 g
    V = 84.0 − 4.5 = 79.5 cm3

  2. R = 8.31 J K−1 mol−1 needs Pa, m3 and K.

    V = 79.5 × 10−6 m3; P = 1.010 × 105 Pa; T = 99.0 + 273.15 = 372.15 K

  3. Keep the mass in grams, so the molar mass comes out in g mol−1.

    M = (0.152 × 8.31 × 372.15) ÷ (1.010 × 105 × 79.5 × 10−6)
    M = 470.07 ÷ 8.0295 = 58.54… g mol−1

  4. The mass, 0.152 g, has 3 significant figures.

    M = 58.5 g mol−1
    propanone, C3H6O: M = 58.09 g mol−1; percentage error = 0.8 %

58.5 g mol−1, which fits propanone (58.09 g mol−1).

0.103 g of a liquid is injected into a gas syringe at 98.5 °C. The volume of vapour is 36.5 cm3 and the pressure is 100.2 kPa. Calculate the molar mass of the liquid, in g mol−1.

  1. Convert first: cm3 to m3, kPa to Pa, °C to K.
  2. M = (0.103 × 8.31 × 371.65) ÷ (1.002 × 105 × 36.5 × 10−6).
T = 98.5 + 273.15 = 371.65 K; P = 1.002 × 105 Pa; V = 3.65 × 10−5 m3
M = 318.1 ÷ 3.657 = 87.0 g mol−1 (hexane, C6H14, is 86.20 g mol−1)

Using a graph instead of one run

Plot the volume of gas against the mass, for several runs, and draw a line of best fit through the origin. A graph averages out random errors and shows up an odd run. The gradient is the volume per gram.

  • Part A: molar volume = gradient (cm3 g−1) × 24.31 g mol−1 ÷ 1000.
  • Part B: V / m = RT / PM, so M = RT ÷ (P × gradient), with the gradient in m3 g−1.

If your best line clearly misses the origin, a similar amount of gas was lost (or gained) in every run. That is a sign of a systematic error.

Using P in kPa with V in cm3 makes PV 1000 times too big, so the molar mass comes out 1000 times too small. Using °C instead of K is just as serious. If a molar mass looks impossible (0.06 g mol−1, or 2000 g mol−1), check the units first.

Tool 3

Uncertainties

For a product or a quotient such as M = mRT / PV, add the percentage uncertainties of the measured quantities. R is a constant and adds nothing. A mass or a volume found by difference uses two readings, so add their absolute uncertainties first.

Propagating uncertainties for worked example 2
QuantityValueAbsolute uncertaintyPercentage uncertainty
mass, two weighings0.152 g±0.002 g1.3 %
volume, two readings79.5 cm3±1.0 cm31.3 %
temperature372.2 K±0.5 K0.13 %
pressure101.0 kPa±0.1 kPa0.10 %
molar mass58.5 g mol−1±1.6 g mol−12.8 %

So M = 58.5 ± 1.6 g mol−1, or 59 ± 2 g mol−1 with the uncertainty to one significant figure. The percentage error compared with propanone is 0.8 %, smaller than the 2.8 % uncertainty. The result agrees with propanone within experimental uncertainty. The mass and the volume dominate: a larger sample, within the range of the syringe, would reduce both.

Part A: is the difference bigger than the uncertainty?

In worked example 1, the mass (0.0742 ± 0.001 g) has an uncertainty of 1.3 % and the volume (76.5 ± 0.5 cm3, one reading from zero) 0.7 %. The molar volume therefore has an uncertainty of about 2.0 %: 25.1 ± 0.5 dm3 mol−1.

The ideal gas value at 23.0 °C and 100.6 kPa is RT / P = 8.31 × 296.15 ÷ 100.6 = 24.5 dm3 mol−1. The measured value is 2.5 % higher, which is more than the 2.0 % uncertainty. Random error alone does not explain it, so a systematic error is likely. The table in the next section suggests which.

Hydrogen collected over aqueous acid (or butane collected over water) also contains some water vapour, so the gas volume is a little larger than the volume of dry gas. Correcting for this uses the vapour pressure of water and partial pressures, which are not in the IB guide. You only need to recognize it as a source of error that makes the volume too large.

Evaluation: errors and improvements

A random error scatters the results; repeats and a graph reduce its effect. A systematic error pushes every result the same way; only a change of method fixes it. For each error below, follow the chain: what happens to the measured quantity, then what happens to the result.

Sources of error in finding molar volume and molar mass
Source of errorEffect on the resultRealistic improvement
Part A: oxide coating on the magnesium (systematic).Part of the mass is magnesium oxide, which gives no hydrogen. Less gas forms than the mass suggests, so the molar volume is too low.Clean the ribbon with sandpaper until it is shiny, just before weighing.
Part A: hydrogen escapes before the bung is fitted (systematic).The volume measured is too small, so the molar volume is too low.Keep the magnesium in a small tube (or on a thread) above the acid and tip the flask only after sealing it.
Part A: volume read while the gas is still warm (systematic).The reaction is exothermic. Warm gas takes up more space, so the molar volume is too high.Wait until the flask is back at room temperature, then read the volume.
Part A: the hydrogen contains water vapour (systematic).The volume includes some water vapour, so the molar volume is too high.Note it as a limitation. (The correction is beyond the guide; see the box above.)
Part A: some hydrogen dissolves in the acid (systematic, small).The volume is slightly too small, so the molar volume is slightly too low.Hydrogen is only slightly soluble, so this effect is very small. Use the smallest volume of acid that is still in excess.
Part B: the vapour is close to its boiling point (systematic).The attractions between the molecules are no longer negligible, so the vapour takes up less space than an ideal gas would. V is too small, so M is too high.Set the jacket at least 20–30 °C above the boiling point of the liquid.
Part B: not all of the liquid vaporizes (systematic).V is too small for the mass recorded, so M is too high.Use a higher jacket temperature or a smaller sample. Check that no drops remain.
Part B: liquid evaporates from the needle between weighing and injecting (systematic).The mass recorded is larger than the mass that reached the syringe, so M is too high.Inject straight after the first weighing and reweigh at once.
Both: friction in the gas syringe, and reading the scale (random).Volumes scatter, so the result is less precise.Turn the plunger gently before reading; do several runs and use a graph.
Both: small masses on a ±0.001 g balance (random).A large percentage uncertainty in m (1.3 % for 0.07 g).Use the largest sample the syringe allows, or a ±0.0001 g balance.

Markschemes want the direction and the reason. “The molar volume is too low because some hydrogen escaped before the bung was fitted, so the volume measured was too small” earns the mark. “Gas escaped” on its own usually does not.
