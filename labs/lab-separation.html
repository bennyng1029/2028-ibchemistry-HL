The chemistry behind it

A mixture can be separated by physical methods because its parts are not chemically bonded. Each technique uses one physical property that is different for the parts: solubility, particle size or boiling point. The full table of techniques is in Structure 1.1: separating mixtures.

Chemists also need to purify products. A solid made in a reaction usually contains small amounts of other substances. Recrystallization works when the product is much more soluble in a hot solvent than in the same solvent when cold.

Approximate solubility of benzoic acid in water
Temperature / °CMass that dissolves in 100 cm3 of water / g
5 (ice bath)0.2
20 (room temperature)0.3
95 (nearly boiling)4.6

So hot water can dissolve about 20 times as much benzoic acid as cold water. A small amount of a soluble impurity stays dissolved when the solution cools, because there is too little of it to saturate the cold water. An insoluble impurity, such as sand, never dissolves, so it can be filtered off while the solution is hot.

A pure solid melts at a fixed temperature, over a narrow range of about 1 °C. Impurities lower the melting point and make it melt over a wider range. So a melting point is a quick check of purity, as well as of identity.

Chromatography is the other technique in Structure 1.1.1. It has its own practical: Lab 2: Paper and thin-layer chromatography.

You do not need fractional distillation columns, separating funnels, sublimation or mixed melting points. You also do not need to explain why impurities lower a melting point in any depth. A percentage recovery is not a percentage yield: yields from reactions come later, in Reactivity 2.1.

Aim, variables and safety

Aim: to purify a sample of impure benzoic acid by recrystallization from water, find the percentage recovery, and use the melting range to judge the purity of the product.

The impure sample contains about 91 % benzoic acid, with some sand and a small amount of a soluble impurity.

Equipment

  • impure benzoic acid, about 2 g
  • distilled water
  • two 100 cm3 beakers and a glass rod
  • hot plate
  • 50 cm3 measuring cylinder
  • stemless filter funnel, fluted filter paper, conical flask
  • ice bath
  • second funnel and filter paper (or a Büchner funnel with suction)
  • watch glass and spatula
  • balance reading to 0.01 g
  • melting point apparatus with thermometer, capillary tubes
  • low-temperature oven or desiccator

Wear eye protection throughout. Benzoic acid causes serious eye irritation and its dust irritates the nose and throat: do not make dust, and wash your hands afterwards.

Nearly boiling water scalds. Move hot beakers with tongs or heat-resistant gloves, and remember the hot plate stays hot after it is switched off. The melting point block goes above 120 °C: do not touch it, and let it cool before you change capillaries. Capillary tubes are thin glass: put broken ones in the glass bin, not the ordinary bin.

If your teacher shows a distillation of ethanol and water, ethanol is highly flammable: heat it with an electric heater or water bath, never a naked flame, with anti-bumping granules in the flask.

Method

Part A: recrystallization

  1. Weigh about 2 g of impure benzoic acid into a 100 cm3 beaker. Record the mass to 0.01 g.
  2. Heat about 60 cm3 of distilled water on the hot plate until it nearly boils.
  3. Add the hot water to the solid in small portions, stirring and keeping it hot, until the benzoic acid has just dissolved. The sand will not dissolve. Record the volume used. Why: the less solvent you use, the less product stays dissolved when the solution cools.
  4. Add a few cm3 more hot water (about one tenth extra). Why: a solution that is exactly saturated starts to crystallize in the funnel as it cools slightly.
  5. Filter the hot solution through fluted filter paper in a warm, stemless funnel. Why: this removes insoluble impurities such as sand. A warm funnel with no stem gives the solution little chance to cool before it passes through.
  6. Cover the filtrate and let it cool slowly to room temperature, then stand it in an ice bath for 10 minutes. Why: slow cooling gives larger crystals that trap less impurity; the ice bath makes more of the product crystallize.
  7. Filter to collect the crystals. Rinse them with a small volume of ice-cold water. Why: the rinse washes away the solution that contains the soluble impurity. Warm water would dissolve some of the crystals.
  8. Dry the crystals on a weighed watch glass in a low-temperature oven or a desiccator. Weigh, dry again and reweigh until two masses agree (constant mass). Why: any water left in the product adds to its mass.

Part B: melting point

  1. Grind a little of the dry product. Press the open end of a capillary tube into it and tap the tube until there is 2–3 mm of tightly packed solid at the bottom.
  2. Heat the block quickly to about 110 °C, then slowly, at 1–2 °C per minute. Why: if you heat fast, the thermometer gets ahead of the sample.
  3. Record the temperature when liquid first appears and when the last solid disappears. This is the melting range.
  4. Repeat with the impure starting material and with pure benzoic acid, so you can compare all three.

Run the experiment

1. Choose a technique

Start by matching techniques to mixtures. For each mixture, think about which part you want to keep and which property differs, then try a technique. Wrong choices are useful: see where each part ends up.

2. Recrystallize the impure benzoic acid

Do a first run with the method as written. Then change one thing at a time and compare the recovery and what the crystals look like. Each run uses a new sample of about 2 g, so the masses change slightly from run to run.

3. Test the purity

Products from your runs appear in the sample list. Compare one of them with the impure sample and with pure benzoic acid. Press the buttons at the moment you see each change.

Processing the data

The percentage recovery tells you how much of the starting material you got back as product. Find the mass of product by difference, then compare it with the mass you started with.

Percentage recovery and purity from one run

A student recrystallized 2.04 g of impure benzoic acid. The empty watch glass had a mass of 24.36 g. The watch glass with the dry product had a mass of 25.87 g. The product melted at 120.0–121.5 °C. The literature melting point of benzoic acid is 122 °C. Calculate the percentage recovery and comment on the purity.

  1. The product was weighed on the watch glass, so subtract the mass of the glass.

    m(product) = 25.87 g − 24.36 g = 1.51 g

  2. Recovery compares what you got back with what you started with.

    recovery = (1.51 g / 2.04 g) × 100 = 74.0196… %

  3. Both masses have 3 significant figures.

    recovery = 74.0 %

  4. A pure solid melts sharply at its literature value; impurities lower and widen the range.

    The range is 1.5 °C wide and ends 0.5 °C below 122 °C. The product is much purer than the starting material, but it may still hold a trace of impurity.

Percentage recovery = 74.0 %. The narrow melting range close to 122 °C shows the product is nearly pure.

Another student starts with 3.12 g of impure benzoic acid. The empty watch glass has a mass of 25.08 g, and 27.66 g with the dry product. Calculate the percentage recovery.

  1. First find the mass of product by difference.
  2. The product has a mass of 2.58 g. Divide by 3.12 g and multiply by 100.
m(product) = 27.66 g − 25.08 g = 2.58 g
recovery = (2.58 g / 3.12 g) × 100 = 82.7 %

A recovery close to or above 100 % is not good news. Some product is always lost, so a very high value usually means the product was still wet, or contains an impurity such as sand.

Propagating the uncertainty

Use the worked example data. The mass of product comes from two readings subtracted, so add the absolute uncertainties.

Δm(product) = ±(0.01 + 0.01) g = ±0.02 g

The recovery is a division, so add the percentage uncertainties.

% uncertainty of m(product) = (0.02 / 1.51) × 100 = 1.32 %
% uncertainty of m(impure sample) = (0.01 / 2.04) × 100 = 0.49 %
% uncertainty of recovery = 1.32 % + 0.49 % = 1.81 %
absolute uncertainty = 74.0 × 1.81 / 100 = ±1.3, so recovery = 74 ± 1 %

The mass of impure sample is read once on a balance that was set to zero, so it carries one reading uncertainty. The absolute uncertainty is given to 1 significant figure, and the value is rounded to the same decimal place.

For the "Try it" data above (3.12 g of sample; watch glass 25.08 g, then 27.66 g), calculate the percentage uncertainty of the recovery. The balance reads to ±0.01 g.

  1. The product mass (2.58 g) comes from two readings, so its uncertainty is ±0.02 g.
  2. Add (0.02 / 2.58) × 100 and (0.01 / 3.12) × 100.
(0.02 / 2.58) × 100 = 0.775 %
(0.01 / 3.12) × 100 = 0.321 %
% uncertainty = 0.775 % + 0.321 % = 1.1 %

Comparing with the literature value

The impure starting material in the same experiment melted at 113.5–119.5 °C. Compare the temperature at which each sample is fully melted with the literature value of 122 °C.

percentage error = |experimental − literature| / literature × 100
product: |121.5 − 122| / 122 × 100 = 0.4 %
impure sample: |119.5 − 122| / 122 × 100 = 2.0 %

Then ask the more useful question: is the difference bigger than the uncertainty? For the product, the difference is 0.5 °C, the same as the thermometer uncertainty, so it agrees with the literature value. For the impure sample the difference is 2.5 °C, five times the uncertainty, so it is a real effect: the impurities lowered the melting point.

In Paper 1B, give an absolute uncertainty to 1 significant figure and the value to the same decimal place. When you compare with a literature value, say whether the difference is larger than the uncertainty. If it is, there must be a systematic error or a real effect such as an impurity.

Evaluation: errors and improvements

A random error makes repeated results scatter, for example judging by eye the moment the last crystal melts. Repeating and averaging reduces its effect. A systematic error pushes every result the same way, for example weighing crystals that are still wet. Repeats do not help: you must change the method.

For an error, always say which way it moves the result and why. "Human error" earns nothing. A good improvement names a specific change to the apparatus or method that removes that error.
