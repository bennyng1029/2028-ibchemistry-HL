Investigating the gas laws

Guiding questionWhat can measurements on a trapped sample of air tell us about how pressure, volume and temperature are linked, and is there a lowest possible temperature?

What you need to be able to do

These are practical skills. Tick each one when you can do it on your own.

  • Identify the independent, dependent and controlled variables in a gas experiment.
  • Record pressure, volume and temperature readings with their units and uncertainties.
  • Test for inverse proportion by calculating PV or by plotting P against 1/V.
  • Plot a graph against temperature and draw a line of best fit.
  • Extrapolate a straight line to estimate absolute zero.
  • Estimate the uncertainty of an extrapolated value and compare it with the percentage error.
  • Identify random and systematic errors and suggest realistic improvements.
Structure 1.5.3

The chemistry behind it

Gas particles are always moving. They hit the walls of their container, and those collisions cause the pressure. For a fixed amount of an ideal gas, three relationships follow.

Three relationships for a fixed amount of gas
Kept constantRelationshipStraight line through the origin
temperaturePressure is inversely proportional to volume.P against 1/V
volumePressure is proportional to the absolute temperature.P against T in K
pressureVolume is proportional to the absolute temperature.V against T in K

In the laboratory you measure temperature in °C. A graph of pressure (or volume) against temperature in °C is still a straight line, but it does not pass through the origin. If you extend the line backwards, it meets the temperature axis at about −273 °C. That is absolute zero, 0 K. Extending a line beyond your data like this is called extrapolation.

The full story is on the topic page: gas-law graphs and the ideal gas equation.

All three relationships come from one equation, PV = nRT. Keep one of P, V and T (and n) fixed and the other two are proportional or inversely proportional, as long as T is in kelvin.

Books call these Boyle’s law, Charles’s law and the pressure law. The guide says the names of the gas laws will not be assessed. You need the relationships and their graphs, not the names.

Aim, variables and safety

Equipment

  • 100 cm3 gas syringe (±0.5 cm3), with a cap to seal the nozzle
  • gas pressure sensor and data logger (±0.1 kPa, range up to about 210 kPa)
  • short, narrow plastic tube to join the syringe or flask to the sensor
  • 250 cm3 borosilicate round-bottomed flask with a one-hole bung and glass tube
  • large beaker or electric water bath, kettle, ice, stirring rod
  • thermometer, −10 °C to 110 °C (±0.5 °C)
  • clamp stands, bosses and clamps

Water near 90 °C scalds. Use an electric water bath or add kettle water slowly, never carry a full beaker of hot water, and clamp the flask so the bath cannot be knocked over. Wear eye protection: a sealed flask is above atmospheric pressure when hot, and a flask with a crack can break. Check the flask for cracks first, and never move a hot flask straight into ice water. Keep the data logger and any mains leads away from the water.

Method

Part A: pressure and volume

  1. Pull the plunger out to 50 cm3. Join the syringe to the pressure sensor with the shortest tube you have. Why: the air inside the tube is part of the trapped gas, but the syringe scale does not measure it.
  2. Test for leaks. Hold the plunger in at about 30 cm3 for one minute. The pressure reading should not fall. Why: the amount of gas must stay constant.
  3. Move the plunger to a new volume. Wait about 30 seconds, then record the volume and the pressure. Why: pushing the plunger in quickly warms the gas a little. Waiting lets it return to room temperature.
  4. Take readings from 25 cm3 to 100 cm3, about every 10 cm3. Then repeat the readings, moving the plunger the other way. Why: a wide range shows the shape of the graph, and repeats show up leaks and friction.
  5. Record the room temperature at the start and at the end. Why: temperature is a controlled variable, so you need to show it stayed the same.

Part B: temperature

  1. Clamp the dry flask so that as much of it as possible is under the water. Join it to the pressure sensor with a short tube. Why: all the gas should be at the bath temperature.
  2. Start with a mixture of ice and water at about 0 °C. Stir, wait until both the thermometer and the pressure reading are steady, then record both. Why: the gas inside the flask takes a few minutes to reach the temperature of the water.
  3. Raise the temperature by about 10 °C to 15 °C, by heating or by adding hot water. Stir, wait, and record again. Continue up to about 90 °C. Why: a wide range of temperatures makes the extrapolation more reliable.
  4. For the fixed-pressure version, seal about 70 cm3 of air in a gas syringe with a cap and hold it under the water. Record the volume instead of the pressure. Turn the plunger gently before each reading. Why: friction can stop the plunger moving freely, so the gas would not be at atmospheric pressure.
  5. Plot pressure (or volume) against temperature in °C. Draw the line of best fit and extend it to the temperature axis.

Run the experiment

These are simulated experiments. Every reading has a small random error, like a real one, so a new run gives slightly different data. The precision is that of real apparatus. Copy your table into a spreadsheet if you want to plot it yourself.

On a graph you plot yourself, label each axis with the quantity and its unit (P / kPa, θ / °C), choose scales that spread the points over most of the grid, and draw one smooth line of best fit. Do not join the points dot to dot.

Processing the data

Two calculations come out of this lab. From Part B, you find where the line meets the temperature axis. From Part A, you test whether PV stays constant and use the gradient to find the amount of gas.

Absolute zero from a pressure–temperature graph

A student heats a sealed flask of air and plots pressure against temperature in °C. The line of best fit passes through (0.0 °C, 92.4 kPa) and (80.0 °C, 119.6 kPa). Estimate absolute zero from this line.

  1. Use two points on the line, far apart, not two data points.

    gradient = (119.6 − 92.4) kPa ÷ (80.0 − 0.0) °C = 27.2 ÷ 80.0 = 0.340 kPa °C−1

  2. The line passes through θ = 0.0 °C at 92.4 kPa, so that is the y-intercept.

    P = 0.340θ + 92.4

  3. An ideal gas has zero pressure at absolute zero, so solve for θ when P = 0.

    0 = 0.340θ + 92.4
    θ = −92.4 ÷ 0.340 = −271.76… °C

  4. The data have 3 significant figures. The literature value is −273.15 °C.

    θ = −272 °C
    percentage error = |−271.8 − (−273.15)| ÷ 273.15 × 100 = 0.5 %

Absolute zero is about −272 °C, a 0.5 % difference from −273.15 °C.

In the fixed-pressure experiment, the line of best fit of volume against temperature passes through (10.0 °C, 52.6 cm3) and (90.0 °C, 67.4 cm3). Estimate absolute zero, in °C.

  1. Find the gradient first, in cm3 °C−1. Then work back from a known point to the temperature at which V = 0.
  2. gradient = (67.4 − 52.6) ÷ (90.0 − 10.0). The volume must fall by 52.6 cm3 from the point at 10.0 °C.
gradient = 14.8 cm3 ÷ 80.0 °C = 0.185 cm3 °C−1
temperature fall needed = 52.6 ÷ 0.185 = 284.3 °C
θ = 10.0 − 284.3 = −274 °C

Testing inverse proportion and finding the amount of gas

In Part A at 21.0 °C a student records: 30 cm3, 168.6 kPa; 50 cm3, 101.3 kPa; 80 cm3, 63.5 kPa; 100 cm3, 50.8 kPa. Show that the pressure is inversely proportional to the volume, and calculate the amount of air, in mol.

  1. If P is inversely proportional to V, the product PV is constant.

    30 × 168.6 = 5058; 50 × 101.3 = 5065; 80 × 63.5 = 5080; 100 × 50.8 = 5080 (kPa cm3)

  2. The values differ by less than 0.5 %, which is within the uncertainty of the volume readings (about 1.7 % at 30 cm3). So PV is constant within experimental error.

    mean PV = 5071 kPa cm3 ≈ 5.07 × 103 kPa cm3

  3. 1 kPa = 103 Pa and 1 cm3 = 10−6 m3, so 1 kPa cm3 = 10−3 Pa m3 = 10−3 J.

    PV = 5.07 × 103 × 10−3 = 5.07 J (Pa m3)

  4. Temperature must be in kelvin: 21.0 + 273.15 = 294.15 K.

    n = PV ÷ RT = 5.07 ÷ (8.31 × 294.15) = 2.074… × 10−3 mol
    n = 2.07 × 10−3 mol (3 significant figures)

PV is constant within error, so P ∝ 1/V. The syringe holds 2.07 × 10−3 mol of air.

The gradient of a student’s graph of P against 1/V is 3.86 × 103 kPa cm3. The room temperature was 18.5 °C. Calculate the amount of air in the syringe, in mol.

  1. The gradient of P against 1/V equals PV. Convert kPa cm3 to J (Pa m3) before you use R = 8.31.
  2. 3.86 × 103 kPa cm3 = 3.86 J. T = 18.5 + 273.15 = 291.65 K.
PV = 3.86 × 103 × 10−3 = 3.86 J
n = 3.86 ÷ (8.31 × 291.65) = 1.59 × 10−3 mol

Putting kPa and cm3 straight into PV = nRT with R = 8.31 gives an answer 1000 times too large. Use Pa and m3, or kPa and dm3. Always use kelvin for T: a temperature in °C gives nonsense.

Percentage uncertainty = absolute uncertainty ÷ value × 100. The same reading has a much smaller percentage uncertainty in kelvin than in °C, because the number is bigger. A temperature difference uses two readings, so its absolute uncertainty is ±0.5 + 0.5 = ±1.0 °C.

For a product or a quotient, add the percentage uncertainties. At 30 cm3, PV has an uncertainty of 1.7 % + 0.06 % ≈ 1.7 %. The volume dominates. That is why the smallest volumes give the least certain points.

The uncertainty of absolute zero

Your estimate comes from an extrapolation, so small errors in the line become large errors at −273 °C. One way to see how large is to draw two worst-fit lines: the steepest and the shallowest lines that still pass through the error bars of your lowest and highest points.

Take the worked example. The lowest and highest readings, (0.0 °C, 92.4 kPa) and (80.0 °C, 119.6 kPa), sit on the line, with error bars of ±0.5 °C and ±0.1 kPa.

  • Steepest line: through (0.5 °C, 92.3 kPa) and (79.5 °C, 119.7 kPa). It meets the axis at −266 °C.
  • Shallowest line: through (−0.5 °C, 92.5 kPa) and (80.5 °C, 119.5 kPa). It meets the axis at −278 °C.

So the result is −272 ± 6 °C. The literature value, −273.15 °C, lies inside this range. The 0.5 % percentage error is smaller than the uncertainty, so random error alone could explain the difference. If the literature value had been outside the range, a systematic error would be likely.

Real data scatter more than the error bars of the instruments suggest, because of other random errors (the gas not quite at the bath temperature, uneven heating). The worst-fit lines then have to pass through all the points’ error bars, not just the two end points. The virtual experiment uses the two end points to keep things simple.

The guide does not set a formal method for the uncertainty of a gradient or an intercept. Worst-fit lines are one common way. You will not need a statistical method such as least-squares error analysis.

Evaluation: errors and improvements

A random error scatters readings both ways; repeats reduce its effect. A systematic error pushes every reading the same way, so repeats do not help. You have to change the method.

For an error question, say three things: what the error is, which way it moves the result (bigger or smaller, and why), and a specific improvement. “Human error” and “be more careful” earn no marks.

Air behaves almost ideally between 0 °C and 90 °C and up to about 200 kPa, so non-ideal behaviour is not a significant error here. You do not need to correct for it.
