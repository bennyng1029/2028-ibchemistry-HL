The chemistry behind it

Chromatography separates the parts of a mixture because they have different attractions, through intermolecular forces, to two phases. The stationary phase stays still. The mobile phase is the solvent that moves up and carries the mixture with it.

The two techniques in this lab
TechniqueStationary phaseMobile phase used here
paper chromatographywater held in the cellulose fibres of the paper (polar)mixtures of ethanol and water
thin-layer chromatography (TLC)a thin layer of silica, SiO2, on a plate (polar, with –OH groups on its surface)mixtures of hexane and ethyl acetate

Both stationary phases are polar and can form hydrogen bonds. So a polar substance is held back strongly and moves only a short way. A less polar substance spends more time dissolved in the moving solvent and travels further. Making the mobile phase more polar helps it compete for the polar substances, so every spot moves further.

RF = distance moved by the spotdistance moved by the solvent front

A substance has the same RF value only if the stationary phase, the solvent and the temperature are the same. That is why known substances are run on the same paper as the mixture. The full theory is in Structure 2.2: chromatography, and the place of chromatography among other separation methods is in Structure 1.1 and Lab 1.

Locating agents (a UV lamp, iodine, ninhydrin) for colourless spots are not required. Nor are column chromatography, two-way chromatography, or how gas chromatographs and HPLC machines work. The equation the simulation uses to produce RF values is not part of the course.

Aim, variables and safety

Aim: to find out which of four known substances, A to D, are present in a mixture M, and to investigate how the polarity of the mobile phase affects RF values.

Equipment

  • chromatography paper or silica TLC plates
  • mixture M and reference solutions A, B, C, D
  • fine glass capillary tubes for spotting
  • chromatography tank or beaker with a lid
  • ethanol, water, hexane, ethyl acetate
  • 10 cm3 measuring cylinders
  • pencil and 30 cm ruler (1 mm divisions)
  • fume cupboard

Wear eye protection. Ethanol, hexane and ethyl acetate are highly flammable: there must be no naked flames anywhere in the room. Hexane and ethyl acetate vapours are harmful and irritating, and long exposure to hexane can damage nerves: run TLC in a fume cupboard and keep the tank covered.

Capillary tubes are thin glass: put broken ones in the glass bin. Do not scrape the silica layer, because the dust irritates the lungs. Pour used organic solvents into the labelled residue bottle, not down the sink.

Method

  1. Draw a baseline in pencil 1.5 cm from the bottom of the paper and mark five lanes. Why: graphite does not dissolve in the solvent; ink would run.
  2. Use a fine capillary to put a small spot of M on the baseline. Let it dry and add more to the same place two or three times. Spot A, B, C and D in the other lanes. Why: small, concentrated spots give compact spots that do not overlap.
  3. Pour the mobile phase into the tank to a depth of about 0.8 cm, put the lid on and leave it for a few minutes. Why: the air in the tank fills with solvent vapour, so the solvent does not evaporate from the paper as it rises.
  4. Stand the paper in the tank with the baseline above the solvent level, not touching the sides, and replace the lid. Why: spots below the solvent level would dissolve into the solvent in the tank.
  5. When the solvent front is about 1 cm from the top, take the paper out and mark the solvent front in pencil at once. Why: the front cannot be seen once the solvent evaporates, and if it reaches the top you cannot tell how far it would have gone.
  6. Let the paper dry in the fume cupboard. Draw a pencil ring round each spot.
  7. With the ruler's zero at the bottom edge, read the positions of the baseline, the solvent front and the centre of each spot. Subtract the baseline reading to get each distance.
  8. Repeat with mobile phases of different composition, keeping everything else the same.

Run the experiment

1. Identify the parts of mixture M

Run the paper with the method as written. Stop it before the solvent reaches the top. Then record readings for the baseline, the front and each spot, and decide which reference substances are in M. Afterwards, try each faulty set-up and see what goes wrong.

2. Change the polarity of the mobile phase

Now run mixture M in six mobile phases at once, from 0 % to 100 % of the more polar solvent. Look at the trend and choose the mobile phase that separates the three spots best.

Every RF value goes up as the mobile phase becomes more polar. The order of the spots does not change: the most polar substance, which is held most strongly by the polar stationary phase, always has the smallest RF.

Processing the data

RF values from ruler readings

A student ran mixture M on paper with 40 % water in ethanol. With the ruler's zero at the bottom edge, the readings were: baseline 1.5 cm, solvent front 9.7 cm, spots of M at 3.6 cm, 5.6 cm and 8.7 cm. On the same paper, the references gave RF values of B 0.25, C 0.69 and D 0.87. Calculate the RF values of the three spots and identify them.

  1. Both distances start at the baseline, not at the edge of the paper.

    solvent front: 9.7 cm − 1.5 cm = 8.2 cm
    spots: 3.6 − 1.5 = 2.1 cm; 5.6 − 1.5 = 4.1 cm; 8.7 − 1.5 = 7.2 cm

  2. The spot cannot move further than the solvent, so each answer is less than 1.

    spot 1: 2.1 / 8.2 = 0.256…
    spot 2: 4.1 / 8.2 = 0.500
    spot 3: 7.2 / 8.2 = 0.878…

  3. The distances have 2 significant figures, so give RF to 2 decimal places (2 significant figures). It has no unit.

    RF = 0.26, 0.50, 0.88

  4. Same RF on the same paper means probably the same substance.

    Spot 1 (0.26) matches B (0.25). Spot 3 (0.88) matches D (0.87). Spot 2 (0.50) does not match C (0.69) or any other reference.

RF = 0.26, 0.50 and 0.88. M contains B, D and one substance that is not A, B, C or D.

On a TLC plate, the ruler (zero at the bottom edge) reads 2.0 cm at the baseline, 10.4 cm at the solvent front and 7.1 cm at the centre of a spot. Calculate the RF value of the spot.

  1. Subtract the baseline reading from both of the other readings first.
  2. The spot moved 5.1 cm and the front moved 8.4 cm. Divide.
spot: 7.1 cm − 2.0 cm = 5.1 cm; front: 10.4 cm − 2.0 cm = 8.4 cm
RF = 5.1 / 8.4 = 0.61

Three slips cost marks: measuring from the edge of the paper instead of the baseline, dividing by the length of the paper instead of the distance to the solvent front, and giving RF a unit. An RF above 1 always means the fraction is upside down.

Tool 3

Uncertainties

A ruler with 1 mm divisions can be read to ±0.05 cm. Each distance is the difference of two readings (the feature and the baseline), so add the absolute uncertainties:

Δd = ±(0.05 + 0.05) cm = ±0.1 cm

RF is a division, so add the percentage uncertainties. For spot 1 in the worked example:

spot: (0.1 / 2.1) × 100 = 4.8 %
front: (0.1 / 8.2) × 100 = 1.2 %
% uncertainty of RF = 4.8 % + 1.2 % = 6.0 %
absolute uncertainty = 0.256 × 6.0 / 100 = ±0.015, so RF = 0.26 ± 0.02

A spot that has moved only a short way has a large percentage uncertainty. Letting the solvent run further makes all the distances bigger and the percentage uncertainties smaller. Judging the centre of a wide, fuzzy spot adds more uncertainty than the ruler does, so keep the spots small.

Calculate the percentage uncertainty of the RF value of spot 3 in the worked example (spot 7.2 cm, front 8.2 cm, each ±0.1 cm).

  1. Work out the percentage uncertainty of each distance.
  2. Add (0.1 / 7.2) × 100 and (0.1 / 8.2) × 100.
(0.1 / 7.2) × 100 = 1.39 %
(0.1 / 8.2) × 100 = 1.22 %
% uncertainty = 1.39 % + 1.22 % = 2.6 %

Comparing with a reference value

A data sheet gives RF = 0.25 for substance B under the same conditions. Spot 1 gave 0.256.

percentage error = |0.256 − 0.25| / 0.25 × 100 = 2.4 %

The percentage error (2.4 %) is smaller than the percentage uncertainty (6.0 %), so spot 1 agrees with B. Spot 2 (0.50 ± 0.02) differs from C (0.69) by 0.19, which is about ten times its uncertainty, so spot 2 is not C. Literature RF values change with the paper, the solvent and the temperature, which is why running references on the same paper is more reliable than a data sheet.

Markschemes accept RF values within a small range, because distances are read from a diagram. Show both distances and the division. When you compare two values, say whether the difference is bigger than the uncertainty.

Evaluation: errors and improvements

A random error, such as judging the centre of a spot, scatters the results; repeat runs and averages reduce it. A systematic error, such as measuring every distance from the edge of the paper, shifts every result the same way; only a change of method removes it.
