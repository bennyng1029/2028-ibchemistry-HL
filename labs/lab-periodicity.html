Periodic trends in the laboratory

Guiding questionHow can simple test-tube reactions show the patterns that the position of an element in the periodic table predicts?

The chemistry behind it

Down group 17

A halogen atom reacts by gaining one electron. Going down the group, the outer energy level is further from the nucleus and more shielded. An extra electron is attracted less strongly, so the halogens get less reactive down the group.

A more reactive halogen takes electrons from the halide ions of a less reactive one. This is a displacement reaction. The new halogen that forms shows up as a colour change.

Down group 1

A group 1 atom reacts by losing its one outer electron. Going down the group, that electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded, so it is lost more easily. The metals get more reactive down the group. With water they form a metal hydroxide and hydrogen:

2M(s) + 2H2O(l) -> 2MOH(aq) + H2(g)

Across a period: oxides

Metal oxides are basic: those that dissolve give alkaline solutions (pH above 7). Non-metal oxides are acidic: those that dissolve give acidic solutions (pH below 7). Aluminium oxide sits between them: it is amphoteric.

The full story, with the explanations, is on the topic page: group 1 and group 17 and oxides.

The guide does not give a list of colours to learn. Exam questions usually describe the colours, but knowing them helps you describe and interpret observations. You need equations with water only for the oxides of group 1 and group 2 metals, carbon and sulfur. The other oxides in this lab are here to show the trend.

Aim, variables and safety

Aim

Equipment

  • Part A: test tubes in a rack, stoppers, dropping pipettes; 0.1 mol dm−3 potassium chloride, potassium bromide and potassium iodide solutions; distilled water; chlorine water, dilute bromine water and iodine solution; cyclohexane; a white tile behind the rack.
  • Part B (teacher demonstration): lithium, sodium and potassium stored under oil; scalpel, tile, forceps, filter paper; a large glass trough of water with universal indicator; safety screen; stopwatch; ruler.
  • Part C: 100 cm3 beakers, 50 cm3 measuring cylinder, balance (±0.01 g), stirring rod; universal indicator; a pH meter calibrated with buffer solutions; the oxides (see the safety notes).

Wear eye protection throughout. Chlorine water and bromine water give off toxic, choking vapours, and bromine water is harmful on skin. Use small volumes of dilute solutions, in a fume cupboard or a well-ventilated room. Anyone with asthma should take extra care.

Cyclohexane is highly flammable and its vapour is harmful. No flames in the room during Part A. Keep the tubes stoppered, and pour the mixtures into the labelled halogenated waste bottle, not down the sink.

Group 1 metals: teacher demonstration only, behind a safety screen, with students at least 2 m away. Use a piece no bigger than a 3 mm cube, in a large trough of water, never in a test tube. Potassium can spit hot metal. The hydroxide solution formed is corrosive.

Oxides: sulfur is burned in a fume cupboard, because sulfur dioxide is toxic and can trigger asthma. Phosphorus(V) oxide reacts violently with water and is corrosive: teacher only, a tiny amount, in a fume cupboard. Sodium oxide and calcium oxide are corrosive and get hot with water. Do not look directly at burning magnesium.

Method

Part A: halogens and halide ions

  1. Put 2 cm3 of potassium chloride, potassium bromide and potassium iodide solution into three test tubes. Put 2 cm3 of distilled water into a fourth tube. The water tube is a control: it shows the colour of the halogen solution on its own.
  2. Add 10 drops of chlorine water to each tube. Shake gently and record the colour against a white tile.
  3. Add 1 cm3 of cyclohexane to each tube, stopper and shake for 10 seconds. Let the layers separate. Cyclohexane does not mix with water and floats on top. Halogens are non-polar molecules, so they dissolve better in the non-polar cyclohexane, where each has a clear colour.
  4. Record the colour of both layers. Compare each tube with the control.
  5. Repeat steps 1 to 4 with bromine water and then with iodine solution. Iodine dissolves only slightly in water, so iodine solution is made with a little potassium iodide.

Part B: group 1 metals with water (teacher demonstration)

  1. Cut a piece of lithium about 3 mm on each side, on a tile. Cut a fresh surface so that the oxide coating does not delay the reaction. Blot off the oil with filter paper.
  2. Drop the piece onto the water in the trough and start the stopwatch. Record what you see. Stop the stopwatch when the piece has gone.
  3. Repeat with fresh water at least twice for each metal, then with sodium and potassium. Keep the piece the same size each time.

Part C: oxides with water

  1. Put 50 cm3 of distilled water in a beaker. Measure its pH. This is the control.
  2. Add 0.50 g of a solid oxide to 50 cm3 of fresh distilled water, stir for one minute and measure the pH. For a gas, bubble it through the water for one minute, or shake the water in the gas jar where the element was burned.
  3. Use universal indicator for a quick estimate and a calibrated pH meter for a reading to ±0.1. Rinse the probe with distilled water between samples.

Processing the data

From observations to an ionic equation

Chlorine water and potassium bromide

Chlorine water was added to potassium bromide solution. The solution turned yellow-orange. After shaking with cyclohexane, the top layer was orange. The control (chlorine water and water) stayed almost colourless in both layers. Write the ionic equation, with state symbols, and state what it shows.

  1. An orange cyclohexane layer means bromine. The control shows that chlorine alone does not give this colour, so bromine has formed.

    product: Br2

  2. Chlorine takes the place of bromine in the salt. Balance the halogen atoms.

    Cl2(aq) + 2KBr(aq) -> 2KCl(aq) + Br2(aq)

  3. The potassium ions are the same on both sides, so they take no part.

    Cl2(aq) + 2Br-(aq) -> 2Cl-(aq) + Br2(aq)

  4. A halogen can only displace a halogen that is less reactive than itself.

    chlorine is more reactive than bromine

Cl2(aq) + 2Br-(aq) -> 2Cl-(aq) + Br2(aq). Chlorine is more reactive than bromine.

Repeated timings: mean and uncertainty

How long does lithium take?

Three pieces of lithium took 58.2 s, 52.6 s and 61.0 s to disappear. Calculate the mean time with its absolute and percentage uncertainty.

  1. Average the repeats to reduce the effect of random error.

    mean = (58.2 + 52.6 + 61.0) / 3 = 57.27 s

  2. The scatter of the repeats is much bigger than the stopwatch precision, so use half the range.

    uncertainty = (61.0 − 52.6) / 2 = 4.2 s ≈ 4 s

  3. Divide by the mean and multiply by 100.

    4.2 / 57.27 × 100 = 7.3 %

  4. Give the uncertainty to one significant figure and the mean to the same place.

    time = 57 ± 4 s (7 %)

57 ± 4 s, a percentage uncertainty of about 7 %.

Four pieces of sodium took 13.8 s, 15.1 s, 12.9 s and 14.6 s. Calculate the percentage uncertainty of the mean time, using half the range as the absolute uncertainty.

  1. Find the mean of the four times first.
  2. Half the range is (15.1 − 12.9) / 2. Divide it by the mean and multiply by 100.
mean = (13.8 + 15.1 + 12.9 + 14.6) / 4 = 14.1 s
uncertainty = (15.1 − 12.9) / 2 = 1.1 s
1.1 / 14.1 × 100 = 7.8 %, so time = 14.1 ± 1.1 s

Uncertainties

Colour observations

Most of Part A is qualitative. A colour has no ± value, but you can still make the observation reliable:

  • Controls: the halogen in water shows the colour before any reaction. A change is a difference from the control, not a colour on its own.
  • A second test: the cyclohexane layer separates brown iodine from yellow-orange bromine, which can look alike in water.
  • Same conditions: the same volumes, a white background and the same light for every tube.
  • Repeats: repeat each mixture and ask a partner to describe the colour without telling them what you expect.

Timings

The stopwatch reads to 0.01 s, but your reaction time is about 0.2 to 0.5 s, and judging when the piece has “gone” is harder still. The sizes of the pieces also differ. So the scatter of repeated runs is the best measure of uncertainty, as in worked example 2.

Do the results show a real difference? Lithium took 57 ± 4 s and sodium 14.1 ± 1.1 s. The ranges (53 to 61 s and 13.0 to 15.2 s) do not overlap, so the difference is larger than the uncertainty: sodium really does react faster.

pH readings

Universal indicator gives the pH to about ±1 from a colour chart. A calibrated pH meter reads to ±0.1. Compare every reading with the control, not with pH 7.

The literature value for saturated calcium hydroxide solution at 25 °C is about pH 12.4. A reading of 12.1 gives:

percentage error = (12.4 − 12.1) / 12.4 × 100 = 2.4 %

The difference, 0.3, is larger than the meter uncertainty of ±0.1. So there is probably a systematic effect: the solution was not saturated, it was warmer than 25 °C, or it had absorbed carbon dioxide from the air.

Evaluation: errors and improvements

Random errors scatter results on both sides of the true value, such as pieces of metal of slightly different size. Repeating and averaging reduces their effect. Systematic errors push every result the same way, such as an old chlorine water that has lost most of its chlorine. Repeating does not help.
