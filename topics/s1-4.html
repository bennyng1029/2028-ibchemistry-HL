Structure 1.4

Counting particles by mass: the mole

Guiding questionHow do we quantify matter on the atomic scale?

What you need to be able to do

This topic is almost all calculation, and every later calculation in the course is built on it. Tick each item when you can do it without looking at your notes. All of it is SL and HL core. There is no Additional HL content in 1.4.

  • Convert an amount in moles to a number of atoms, molecules, ions or formula units, and back, using the Avogadro constant.
  • Work out a relative formula mass, Mr, from relative atomic masses, and say why Ar and Mr have no units.
  • Solve problems that link mass, amount and number of particles using n = m / M.
  • Go from percentage composition by mass to an empirical formula, and from a formula to percentage composition.
  • Find an empirical formula from mass data, including combustion data and heating a hydrate.
  • Find a molecular formula from an empirical formula and a molar mass.
  • Solve problems with n = CV, and convert between mol dm-3 and g dm-3.
  • Describe how to prepare a standard solution and a dilution, and how a calibration curve gives an unknown concentration.
  • State Avogadro’s law and use the mole ratio of an equation to find volumes of reacting gases.

Before any mole calculation: three questions

Most lost marks in this topic do not come from hard chemistry. They come from pressing calculator buttons before you know where you are going. So use the same routine every time, even when the question looks short.

  1. What am I given, and in what units?Write each value with its unit and say what it is: a mass in g, a volume in cm3, a concentration in mol dm−3, a number of particles.
  2. What do I want, and in what units?Underline it in the question. “Amount” always means moles. “Number of atoms” is not the same as “number of molecules”.
  3. Which link connects them?Every route goes through moles. Pick the relationship that turns what you have into moles, then the one that turns moles into what you want.

There are only three relationships to choose from in this topic: n = N ÷ NA for particles, n = m ÷ M for mass, and n = C × V for solutions. The mole is the meeting point of all three.

Get the units right first

The relationships only work in their own units: mass in g, volume of solution in dm3. Questions often give cm3 or mg instead. Convert before you substitute, and write the conversion down as its own line.

Putting a volume in cm3 straight into n = CV gives an answer 1000 times too big. A quick check: 25 cm3 is a small amount of liquid, so in dm3 it must be a small number, 0.025.

A pipette delivers 25.0 cm3 of solution. What is this volume in dm3?

  1. A dm3 is bigger than a cm3. Will the number get bigger or smaller?
  2. Divide by 1000: 25.0 ÷ 1000. Keep three significant figures.
V = 25.0 cm3 ÷ 1000 = 0.0250 dm3

The zero at the end matters. It shows the value is known to three significant figures.

Significant figures: a short guide

Significant figures (s.f.) tell the reader how precisely a value is known. Your answer cannot be more precise than the data you started with.

Counting significant figures
Values.f.Why
4.503Zeros after the decimal point at the end count.
0.02503Zeros at the front only place the decimal point. They do not count.
250.04A volumetric flask is precise, so its volume is often given to 4 s.f.
6.02 × 10233In scientific notation, count the digits in front of the “× 10”.
  1. Keep the full value in your calculator through every step. Rounding in the middle can change the last digit of the answer.
  2. Round once, at the end. Look at the data in the question, find the value with the fewest significant figures, and give your answer to that many. In most questions that is 3.
  3. Counted and defined numbers do not limit you: the 2 in H2O, the coefficients in an equation, the 1000 in a unit conversion.
  4. Write the unit after every final answer.

IB markschemes give their answers to the precision of the data: usually 3 s.f., and 4 s.f. when the data are given to 4. Match the data and you are safe. A mark is not normally taken off for significant figures unless the question asks for a set number, so do not panic about them. Show your working too. If you slip in one step, the later steps can still earn marks when the method is correct.

Structure 1.4.1

The mole and the Avogadro constant

Eggs are sold by the dozen, and paper by the ream of 500 sheets. When things are small and you need a lot of them, you count in groups. Atoms are so small that the group has to be enormous.

The mole (symbol mol) is the SI unit of amount of substance, symbol n. One mole contains exactly the number of elementary entities given by the Avogadro constant, NA.

An elementary entity is whatever particle you are counting. It may be an atom, a molecule, an ion, an electron, or a specified group of particles such as one formula unit of NaCl. So always say what you have a mole of.

NA = 6.02 × 1023 mol−1. The unit mol−1 means “per mole”. You do not need to memorize the value, but you must know when to use it.

N = n × NA
N
number of particles (no unit)
n
amount of substance mol
NA
Avogadro constant mol-1

This relationship is not printed in the data booklet, so learn it. It follows from the definition. Moles to particles: multiply by NA. Particles to moles: divide by NA. A number of particles is always huge and an amount in moles is usually small, so check that your answer went the right way.

Atoms inside molecules, ions inside formulas

A formula is a recipe for one particle, and also for one mole of them. One molecule of NH3 contains 1 nitrogen atom and 3 hydrogen atoms. So one mole of NH3 molecules contains 1 mol of N atoms and 3 mol of H atoms.

The same works for ionic compounds. One formula unit of MgCl2 contains one Mg^2+ ion and two Cl- ions. So 1 mol of MgCl2 contains 1 mol of Mg^2+ ions, 2 mol of Cl- ions, and 3 mol of ions in total.

Method in words

  1. Name the particle the question asks about: molecules, atoms of one element, all atoms, or ions.
  2. If it asks about atoms or ions inside a formula, multiply the amount of the compound by the subscript first.
  3. Multiply moles by 6.02 × 1023 mol−1 to get the number, or divide a number by it to get moles.
  4. Give the answer in scientific notation, to the significant figures of the data.

Moles to number of molecules

Calculate the number of molecules in 0.350 mol of carbon dioxide, CO2.

  1. Given: an amount, 0.350 mol. Wanted: a number of molecules. Link: the Avogadro constant.

    N = n × NA

  2. mol × mol−1 cancels, leaving a pure number. That is what a count should be.

    N = 0.350 mol × 6.02 × 1023 mol−1 = 2.107 × 1023

  3. 0.350 and 6.02 both have 3 significant figures, so the answer has 3.

    N = 2.11 × 1023 molecules

2.11 × 1023 molecules of CO2

Atoms inside a molecule

Calculate the number of hydrogen atoms in 0.125 mol of ammonia, NH3.

  1. The question asks for hydrogen atoms, not ammonia molecules. Each molecule holds 3 of them.

    1 mol NH3 contains 3 mol H atoms

  2. Use the subscript from the formula as a multiplier while you are still in moles.

    n(H) = 3 × 0.125 mol = 0.375 mol

  3. Now it is the same step as before: multiply by the Avogadro constant.

    N(H) = 0.375 mol × 6.02 × 1023 mol−1 = 2.2575 × 1023

  4. The data have 3 significant figures. The 3 from the formula is an exact count and does not limit the answer.

    N(H) = 2.26 × 1023 atoms

2.26 × 1023 hydrogen atoms

“One mole of oxygen” is unclear. One mole of O2 molecules contains two moles of O atoms. Hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine exist as two-atom molecules, so check whether the question means atoms or molecules.

A piece of copper wire contains 1.50 × 1022 copper atoms. Calculate the amount of copper, in mol.

  1. You have a number of particles and you want moles. Will the answer be bigger or smaller than 1?
  2. Divide by the Avogadro constant: 1.50 × 1022 ÷ 6.02 × 1023.
n = N ÷ NA = 1.50 × 1022 ÷ 6.02 × 1023 mol−1 = 0.02492 mol

To 3 significant figures: 0.0249 mol. There are fewer atoms than one mole, so the amount is less than 1.

Calculate the total number of ions in 0.200 mol of calcium chloride, CaCl2.

  1. How many ions are in one formula unit of CaCl2? Count the Ca^2+ and the Cl- ions.
  2. There are 3 ions per formula unit, so n(ions) = 3 × 0.200 mol. Then multiply by 6.02 × 1023.
n(ions) = 3 × 0.200 mol = 0.600 mol
N = 0.600 mol × 6.02 × 1023 mol−1 = 3.612 × 1023

To 3 significant figures: 3.61 × 1023 ions.

You may see an older definition: “the amount that contains as many particles as 12 g of carbon-12”. The IB guide does not use it. It defines the mole by a fixed number of particles. That number is exactly 6.02214076 × 1023, but you only ever use the data booklet value, 6.02 × 1023. You do not need the history of the change.

Structure 1.4.2

Relative atomic mass and relative formula mass

One atom has a mass of around 10−23 g. Numbers like that are awkward, so chemists compare atoms with each other instead. The standard is the carbon-12 atom, ^12C, which is given a mass of exactly 12 units.

The relative atomic mass, Ar, of an element is the weighted average mass of its atoms compared with one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. It is an average because most elements are a mixture of isotopes (see topic 1.2). That is why chlorine has Ar = 35.45, although no single chlorine atom has that mass.

The relative formula mass, Mr, is the sum of the Ar values of every atom in the formula. For a substance made of molecules it is also called the relative molecular mass.

Ar and Mr are ratios: one mass divided by another mass. The units cancel, so they have no units.

The periodic table in the data booklet gives Ar to two decimal places: H 1.01, C 12.01, N 14.01, O 16.00, Na 22.99, Cl 35.45. Use these values in calculations, not rounded whole numbers.

Method in words

  1. List each element in the formula.
  2. Count its atoms. A subscript outside a bracket multiplies everything inside the bracket.
  3. Multiply each count by the element’s Ar (2 d.p.).
  4. Add the subtotals. Give Mr to 2 decimal places, with no unit.

Relative formula mass with brackets

Determine the relative formula mass of calcium nitrate, Ca(NO3)2.

  1. The 2 outside the bracket doubles everything inside it: 2 × N and 2 × 3 = 6 O.

    Ca: 1   N: 2   O: 6

  2. Use the two-decimal-place values from the data booklet.

    Ca: 1 × 40.08 = 40.08
    N: 2 × 14.01 = 28.02
    O: 6 × 16.00 = 96.00

  3. When you add, keep the decimal places of the data: two.

    Mr = 40.08 + 28.02 + 96.00 = 164.10

Mr = 164.10 (no units)

A hydrated salt has water molecules built into its crystals. The dot in CuSO4.5H2O means “with”, so add five whole waters: 63.55 + 32.07 + 4(16.00) + 5(18.02) = 249.72.

In Ca(NO3)2 it is easy to count only 3 oxygen atoms and get 116.10. The subscript after a bracket multiplies every atom inside it. Count atoms first, then use the calculator.

Determine the relative formula mass of aluminium sulfate, Al2(SO4)3. Give your answer to 2 decimal places. (Ar: Al 26.98, S 32.07, O 16.00)

  1. Count the atoms first. How many S atoms and how many O atoms are in three sulfate groups?
  2. There are 2 Al, 3 S and 12 O: 2(26.98) + 3(32.07) + 12(16.00).
Mr = 2(26.98) + 3(32.07) + 12(16.00)
Mr = 53.96 + 96.21 + 192.00 = 342.17

342.17, with no unit, because it is a relative mass.

You do not need the unified atomic mass unit (u or Da) or the mass of one atom in kilograms for this topic. Calculating Ar from isotope abundances belongs to topic 1.2.

Structure 1.4.3

Molar mass: weighing instead of counting

A bank does not count coins one by one. It weighs the bag, because it knows the mass of one coin. Chemists do the same with atoms. If you know the mass of one mole of a substance, a balance tells you how many particles you have.

The molar mass, M, is the mass of one mole of a substance. Its units are g mol-1. The mole is chosen so that the molar mass has the same number as Mr: water has Mr = 18.02, so M = 18.02 g mol−1.

Same number, different meaning

n = m / M is printed in the data booklet. Rearranged: m = n × M and M = m ÷ n.

Let the units check your rearrangement. Grams divided by grams-per-mole gives moles: g ÷ g mol−1 = mol. If your units come out as something else, the formula is upside down.

The mole map

Mass, particles, solutions and gases never convert directly into each other. Each one converts to moles, and moles convert to each of the others. That is the whole topic in one picture.

Method in words

  1. Ask the three questions. Find “given” and “wanted” on the mole map.
  2. If mass is involved, work out the molar mass M from the formula.
  3. Step 1: convert what you are given into moles.
  4. Step 2: convert moles into what you want. If the question asks about atoms or ions inside the formula, multiply by the subscript while you are in moles.
  5. Round once at the end and add the unit.

Mass to moles

Calculate the amount, in mol, of calcium carbonate in a 12.5 g piece of marble. Assume the marble is pure CaCO3.

  1. Given: a mass in g. Wanted: an amount in mol. Link: molar mass.

    n = m ÷ M

  2. You need M before you can use the relationship. One Ca, one C, three O.

    M(CaCO3) = 40.08 + 12.01 + 3(16.00) = 100.09 g mol−1

  3. Keep the extra digits in your calculator for now.

    n = 12.5 g ÷ 100.09 g mol−1 = 0.12489 mol

  4. The mass has 3 significant figures.

    n = 0.125 mol

0.125 mol of CaCO3

Mass to number of atoms (two links)

Calculate the number of oxygen atoms in 4.90 g of sulfuric acid, H2SO4.

  1. Mass and particles do not connect directly. Go mass → moles of H2SO4 → moles of O atoms → number of O atoms.

    g  →  mol H2SO4  →  mol O  →  number of O atoms

  2. Mass is involved, so M is needed.

    M(H2SO4) = 2(1.01) + 32.07 + 4(16.00) = 98.09 g mol−1

  3. This is the first link: n = m ÷ M.

    n(H2SO4) = 4.90 g ÷ 98.09 g mol−1 = 0.049954 mol

  4. Each H2SO4 contains 4 oxygen atoms.

    n(O) = 4 × 0.049954 mol = 0.19982 mol

  5. Second link: multiply by NA. Round to the 3 significant figures of the data.

    N(O) = 0.19982 mol × 6.02 × 1023 mol−1 = 1.2029 × 1023
    N(O) = 1.20 × 1023 atoms

1.20 × 1023 oxygen atoms

Multiplying when you should divide is the classic slip. Estimate first: 4.90 g is much less than one mole of H2SO4 (98.09 g), so the amount must be much less than 1 mol. An answer of 481 mol cannot be right.

Calculate the mass of 0.0450 mol of sodium hydroxide, NaOH. (Ar: Na 22.99, O 16.00, H 1.01)

  1. You have moles and want grams. Rearrange n = m / M to make m the subject.
  2. M(NaOH) = 22.99 + 16.00 + 1.01 = 40.00 g mol−1. Then m = n × M.
M(NaOH) = 22.99 + 16.00 + 1.01 = 40.00 g mol−1
m = n × M = 0.0450 mol × 40.00 g mol−1 = 1.80 g

1.80 g (3 significant figures).

A sample of carbon dioxide contains 3.01 × 1022 molecules. Calculate its mass. (Ar: C 12.01, O 16.00)

  1. Particles and mass do not connect directly. Which quantity do you go through?
  2. First n = N ÷ NA. Then m = n × M, with M(CO2) = 44.01 g mol−1.
n = 3.01 × 1022 ÷ 6.02 × 1023 mol−1 = 0.0500 mol
M(CO2) = 12.01 + 2(16.00) = 44.01 g mol−1
m = 0.0500 mol × 44.01 g mol−1 = 2.2005 g

To 3 significant figures: 2.20 g.

Structure 1.4.4

Empirical and molecular formulas

A recipe that says “2 parts flour to 1 part butter” gives you the ratio, but not how big the batch is. Chemical formulas come in the same two kinds.

The empirical formula of a compound gives the simplest ratio of atoms of each element present in it. The molecular formula gives the actual number of atoms of each element present in a molecule.

Empirical and molecular formulas compared

glucoseC6H12O6CH2O6

The molecular formula is always a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula. Sometimes the multiple is 1 and the two are the same. Ionic compounds such as NaCl have no molecules, so their formula is already an empirical formula.

From a formula to percentage composition

Percentage composition by mass says what share of a compound’s mass comes from each element.

% of element = atoms of element × ArMr × 100
Ar
relative atomic mass of the element (no units)
Mr
relative formula mass of the compound (no units)

For iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3: Mr = 2(55.85) + 3(16.00) = 159.70. So the percentage of iron is 111.70 ÷ 159.70 × 100 = 69.94 %, and oxygen is the rest, 30.06 %. The percentages must add up to 100.

Urea, CO(NH2)2, is used as a fertilizer because of its nitrogen content. Calculate the percentage by mass of nitrogen in urea. (Ar: C 12.01, O 16.00, N 14.01, H 1.01)

  1. How many nitrogen atoms are in the formula? The 2 outside the bracket matters.
  2. Mr = 12.01 + 16.00 + 2(14.01) + 4(1.01) = 60.07. Then % N = 2(14.01) ÷ 60.07 × 100.
Mr = 12.01 + 16.00 + 2(14.01) + 4(1.01) = 60.07
% N = 28.02 ÷ 60.07 × 100 = 46.65 %

To 3 significant figures: 46.6 %.

From composition to an empirical formula: the table method

Going the other way is the more common exam question. A formula is a ratio of atoms, which means a ratio of moles. But the data are masses. So the job is: convert each mass to moles, then simplify the ratio.

  1. Make a column for each element. Write its mass in g. If you are given percentages, imagine a 100 g sample, so 40 % becomes 40 g.
  2. Divide each mass by that element’s Ar. This gives the amount of each element in mol.
  3. Divide every amount by the smallest one. The smallest becomes 1.
  4. If every number is now within about 0.1 of a whole number, round. If one ends in about .5, multiply them all by 2. If one ends in about .33 or .67, multiply by 3. If one ends in .25 or .75, multiply by 4.
  5. Write the formula with the whole numbers as subscripts.

Empirical formula from percentage composition

A compound contains 48.64 % carbon, 8.16 % hydrogen and 43.20 % oxygen by mass. Determine its empirical formula.

  1. In a 100 g sample, each percentage is the mass in grams. Any sample size gives the same ratio, and 100 g is the easiest.

    C: 48.64 g   H: 8.16 g   O: 43.20 g

  2. A formula counts atoms, so you need amounts, not masses. Use n = m ÷ Ar.

    C: 48.64 g ÷ 12.01 g mol−1 = 4.050 mol
    H: 8.16 g ÷ 1.01 g mol−1 = 8.079 mol
    O: 43.20 g ÷ 16.00 g mol−1 = 2.700 mol

  3. This sets the smallest amount to 1 and shows the ratio clearly. The smallest is 2.700.

    C: 4.050 ÷ 2.700 = 1.50
    H: 8.079 ÷ 2.700 = 2.99
    O: 2.700 ÷ 2.700 = 1.00

  4. 1.50 is not close to a whole number, so it must not be rounded. Multiplying everything by 2 turns it into 3.

    C: 1.50 × 2 = 3.00   H: 2.99 × 2 = 5.98   O: 1.00 × 2 = 2.00

  5. 5.98 is within experimental error of 6, so the ratio is 3 : 6 : 2.

    empirical formula = C3H6O2

C3H6O2

Three slips cause most wrong formulas. Using the percentages directly as the ratio, without dividing by Ar. Dividing by atomic number instead of Ar. Rounding 1.5 up to 2, or 1.33 down to 1, instead of multiplying by 2 or 3.

An oxide of phosphorus contains 43.64 % phosphorus and 56.36 % oxygen by mass. Its empirical formula can be written P2Ox. Find the whole number x. (Ar: P 30.97, O 16.00)

  1. Use the table method: divide each percentage by Ar, then divide both by the smaller answer.
  2. You should get P : O = 1 : 2.50. What do you multiply by to make both numbers whole?
P: 43.64 ÷ 30.97 = 1.409 mol   O: 56.36 ÷ 16.00 = 3.523 mol
÷ 1.409:   P = 1.00   O = 2.50
× 2:   P = 2   O = 5

The empirical formula is P2O5, so x = 5.

Empirical formula from combustion data

Many compounds contain only carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. To analyse one, a weighed sample is burned completely in excess oxygen. The carbon dioxide and the water that form are collected and weighed.

Every carbon atom in the sample ends up in a CO2 molecule. Every hydrogen atom ends up in an H2O molecule. The oxygen in the products comes partly from the air, so the oxygen in the sample is found by subtraction.

  1. Mass of C in the sample = mass of CO2 × 12.01 ÷ 44.01.
  2. Mass of H in the sample = mass of H2O × 2(1.01) ÷ 18.02. The 2 is there because each water molecule holds two hydrogen atoms.
  3. Mass of O in the sample = mass of sample − mass of C − mass of H.
  4. Now use the table method on the three masses.

You can also work in moles: n(C) = n(CO2) and n(H) = 2 × n(H2O). You still need the masses of C and H to find the oxygen by difference.

Empirical formula from combustion analysis

A compound contains only carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Complete combustion of 0.660 g of it produces 1.319 g of CO2 and 0.540 g of H2O. Determine the empirical formula.

  1. All the carbon is now in the CO2. Carbon makes up 12.01 out of every 44.01 g of CO2.

    m(C) = 1.319 g × 12.0144.01 = 0.3599 g

  2. All the hydrogen is now in the H2O. Hydrogen makes up 2.02 out of every 18.02 g of water.

    m(H) = 0.540 g × 2(1.01)18.02 = 0.06053 g

  3. Whatever is not carbon or hydrogen must be oxygen. Use unrounded values from your calculator.

    m(O) = 0.660 g − 0.3599 g − 0.06053 g = 0.2395 g

  4. From here it is the ordinary table method.

    C: 0.3599 g ÷ 12.01 g mol−1 = 0.02997 mol
    H: 0.06053 g ÷ 1.01 g mol−1 = 0.05993 mol
    O: 0.2395 g ÷ 16.00 g mol−1 = 0.01497 mol

  5. The smallest is 0.01497. The ratios are whole numbers within experimental error.

    C: 2.00   H: 4.00   O: 1.00
    empirical formula = C2H4O

C2H4O

A typical 3-mark combustion question gives one mark for the masses or amounts of C and H, one for oxygen by difference, and one for the formula. Lay your working out in that order so each mark is easy to find.

A compound contains only C, H and O. Burning 1.150 g of it completely gives 2.197 g of CO2 and 1.349 g of H2O. Calculate the mass of oxygen in the 1.150 g sample, to 3 significant figures.

  1. Find the mass of carbon from the CO2 and the mass of hydrogen from the H2O. Then subtract both from 1.150 g.
  2. m(C) = 2.197 × 12.01 ÷ 44.01 and m(H) = 1.349 × 2.02 ÷ 18.02. Do not round them before you subtract.
m(C) = 2.197 g × 12.01 ÷ 44.01 = 0.5995 g
m(H) = 1.349 g × 2.02 ÷ 18.02 = 0.1512 g
m(O) = 1.150 g − 0.5995 g − 0.1512 g = 0.3992 g

0.399 g of oxygen. Carrying on: the amounts are C 0.04992 mol, H 0.1497 mol and O 0.02495 mol, a ratio of 2 : 6 : 1, so the empirical formula is C2H6O.

From empirical formula to molecular formula

The empirical formula gives the ratio. To find the real molecule you need one more fact: the molar mass. It usually comes from another experiment, often a gas measurement (topic 1.5).

  1. Work out the mass of the empirical formula, from Ar values.
  2. Divide the molar mass by the empirical formula mass. The answer should be a whole number.
  3. Multiply every subscript in the empirical formula by that whole number.

Molecular formula from molar mass

The compound in the last worked example has the empirical formula C2H4O. Its molar mass is 88.12 g mol−1. Determine its molecular formula.

  1. You need to know how heavy one “unit” of the ratio is.

    2(12.01) + 4(1.01) + 16.00 = 44.06

  2. This tells you how many empirical units fit into one molecule.

    88.12 ÷ 44.06 = 2.00

  3. Two units per molecule, so double every subscript.

    (C2H4O) × 2 = C4H8O2

C4H8O2

A sugar has the empirical formula CH2O and a molar mass of 180.18 g mol−1. How many carbon atoms are in one molecule of the sugar? (Ar: C 12.01, H 1.01, O 16.00)

  1. Find the mass of one CH2O unit first.
  2. The empirical formula mass is 30.03. Divide 180.18 by it to see how many units make one molecule.
empirical formula mass = 12.01 + 2(1.01) + 16.00 = 30.03
180.18 ÷ 30.03 = 6.00

The molecular formula is C6H12O6, so there are 6 carbon atoms.

Read which formula the question wants. If it asks for the empirical formula of C4H8O2, simplify to C2H4O. If it asks for the molecular formula, do not simplify.

Water of crystallization from heating a hydrate

The same table method finds the x in a hydrated salt such as CuSO4.xH2O. A weighed sample is heated so the water is driven off as steam. What is left is the anhydrous salt. The mass lost is the mass of water.

Here the two “elements” in the table are the anhydrous salt and the water. Divide each mass by its own molar mass, then divide by the amount of salt.

Finding x in a hydrate

4.99 g of hydrated copper(II) sulfate, CuSO4.xH2O, is heated to constant mass. 3.19 g of anhydrous CuSO4 remains. Determine x.

  1. The only thing that left the crucible was water.

    m(H2O) = 4.99 g − 3.19 g = 1.80 g

  2. The formula is a mole ratio of salt to water. M(CuSO4) = 63.55 + 32.07 + 4(16.00) = 159.62 g mol−1.

    n(CuSO4) = 3.19 g ÷ 159.62 g mol−1 = 0.01998 mol
    n(H2O) = 1.80 g ÷ 18.02 g mol−1 = 0.09989 mol

  3. You want the number of waters for each one CuSO4.

    x = 0.09989 ÷ 0.01998 = 5.00

  4. x must be a whole number, because it counts molecules.

    x = 5, so the formula is CuSO4·5H2O

x = 5

“Heat to constant mass” means heat, cool, weigh, and repeat until two weighings agree. It shows that all the water has gone. If some water stays behind, the mass lost is too small and the calculated x is too low.

6.16 g of hydrated magnesium sulfate, MgSO4.xH2O, is heated to constant mass. 3.01 g of anhydrous MgSO4 remains. Determine the whole number x. (M: MgSO4 120.38 g mol−1, H2O 18.02 g mol−1)

  1. The mass of water is the mass lost on heating: 6.16 g − 3.01 g.
  2. Convert 3.01 g of MgSO4 and 3.15 g of H2O to moles. Then divide the moles of water by the moles of salt.
m(H2O) = 6.16 g − 3.01 g = 3.15 g
n(MgSO4) = 3.01 g ÷ 120.38 g mol−1 = 0.02500 mol
n(H2O) = 3.15 g ÷ 18.02 g mol−1 = 0.1748 mol
x = 0.1748 ÷ 0.02500 = 6.99

Rounded to a whole number, x = 7: MgSO4·7H2O.

Combustion problems in this topic use compounds of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, with oxygen found by difference. You do not need to know how an elemental analyser works.

Structure 1.4.5

Concentration of solutions

Two glasses of squash can hold the same amount of syrup but taste different, because one has more water in it. What matters is how much is dissolved per unit volume. That is concentration.

A solute dissolves in a solvent to make a solution. The molar concentration is the amount of solute divided by the volume of the solution. Its units are mol dm-3.

n = CV
n
amount of solute mol
C
molar concentration mol dm-3
V
volume of solution dm3

n = CV is printed in the data booklet. Rearranged: C = n ÷ V and V = n ÷ C. The volume must be in dm3.

Square brackets mean “molar concentration of”. So [NaCl] = 0.400 mol dm−3 reads “the concentration of sodium chloride is 0.400 moles per cubic decimetre”. You are expected to use this notation.

V is the volume of the finished solution, not the volume of water you added. Dissolving a solid changes the volume a little, which is why solutions are made up to a mark and not by adding a measured volume of water.

Two ways to state a concentration

The mass concentration is the mass of solute per volume of solution, in g dm-3. The two are linked by the molar mass, in exactly the way mass and moles are.

mass concentration = C × M
C
molar concentration mol dm-3
M
molar mass of the solute g mol-1

The mass concentration comes out in g dm-3. For example, 0.0500 mol dm−3 sulfuric acid (M = 98.09 g mol−1) is 0.0500 × 98.09 = 4.90 g dm−3. To go from g dm−3 to mol dm−3, divide by M.

Method in words

  1. Ask the three questions. Is the solute given as a mass, an amount or a concentration?
  2. Convert every volume from cm3 to dm3 by dividing by 1000. Write this as its own line.
  3. If you have a mass of solute, convert it to moles with n = m ÷ M.
  4. Use n = CV, rearranged for what you want.
  5. Check the size of the answer, round, and write the unit.

Concentration from mass and volume

5.85 g of sodium chloride, NaCl, is dissolved in distilled water and the solution is made up to 250.0 cm3. Calculate the concentration in mol dm−3 and in g dm−3.

  1. n = CV only works with the volume in dm3.

    V = 250.0 cm3 ÷ 1000 = 0.2500 dm3

  2. Molar concentration needs an amount in mol, not a mass.

    M(NaCl) = 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44 g mol−1
    n = 5.85 g ÷ 58.44 g mol−1 = 0.10010 mol

  3. Rearranging n = CV gives C = n ÷ V.

    [NaCl] = 0.10010 mol ÷ 0.2500 dm3 = 0.4004 mol dm−3

  4. The mass has 3 significant figures, the fewest in the data.

    [NaCl] = 0.400 mol dm−3

  5. Mass per volume uses the mass directly. There is no need to go through moles.

    5.85 g ÷ 0.2500 dm3 = 23.4 g dm−3

0.400 mol dm−3, which is 23.4 g dm−3

Dividing by 250 instead of 0.2500 gives 0.000400, an answer 1000 times too small. Mixing up the two units is also common. For the same solution, the value in g dm-3 is always the bigger number, because every molar mass is greater than 1 g mol−1.

Calculate the amount, in mol, of solute in 20.0 cm3 of a 0.150 mol dm−3 solution.

  1. Which relationship links amount, concentration and volume? What unit must the volume be in?
  2. V = 20.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0200 dm3. Then n = C × V.
V = 20.0 cm3 ÷ 1000 = 0.0200 dm3
n = 0.150 mol dm−3 × 0.0200 dm3 = 0.00300 mol

0.00300 mol, or 3.00 × 10−3 mol.

1.20 g of sodium hydroxide, NaOH, is dissolved and made up to 250.0 cm3 of solution. Calculate [NaOH]. (M(NaOH) = 40.00 g mol−1)

  1. Two conversions are needed before you can divide: mass to moles, and cm3 to dm3.
  2. n = 1.20 ÷ 40.00 = 0.0300 mol and V = 0.2500 dm3. Now C = n ÷ V.
n = 1.20 g ÷ 40.00 g mol−1 = 0.0300 mol
V = 250.0 cm3 ÷ 1000 = 0.2500 dm3
[NaOH] = 0.0300 mol ÷ 0.2500 dm3 = 0.120 mol dm−3

0.120 mol dm−3

A bottle is labelled “sodium hydroxide, 8.00 g dm−3”. Convert this to a molar concentration. (M(NaOH) = 40.00 g mol−1)

  1. Going from grams to moles: do you multiply or divide by the molar mass?
  2. Divide: 8.00 g dm−3 ÷ 40.00 g mol−1.
[NaOH] = 8.00 g dm−3 ÷ 40.00 g mol−1 = 0.200 mol dm−3

0.200 mol dm−3. The units confirm it: g dm−3 ÷ g mol−1 = mol dm−3.

Concentration of the ions

When an ionic compound dissolves, its ions separate. The formula tells you how many of each you get. One MgCl2 gives one Mg^2+ and two Cl-. So in 0.150 mol dm−3 MgCl2, [Mg2+] = 0.150 mol dm−3 and [Cl−] = 2 × 0.150 = 0.300 mol dm−3.

Making a standard solution

A standard solution is one whose concentration is known accurately. It is made in a volumetric flask, which has a single mark on a narrow neck and holds one precise volume.

Mass needed for a standard solution

Calculate the mass of anhydrous sodium carbonate, Na2CO3, needed to prepare 250.0 cm3 of a 0.1000 mol dm−3 solution.

  1. Given: a concentration and a volume. Wanted: a mass. Route: solution → moles → mass.

    n = CV   then   m = n × M

  2. The concentration is per dm3, so the volume must match.

    V = 250.0 cm3 ÷ 1000 = 0.2500 dm3

  3. This is the amount of solute that must end up in the flask.

    n = 0.1000 mol dm−3 × 0.2500 dm3 = 0.02500 mol

  4. A balance measures grams, not moles.

    M(Na2CO3) = 2(22.99) + 12.01 + 3(16.00) = 105.99 g mol−1
    m = 0.02500 mol × 105.99 g mol−1 = 2.64975 g

  5. Standard-solution data are given to 4 significant figures, so the answer is too.

    m = 2.650 g

2.650 g of Na2CO3

Calculating the mass is half the job. You also need to be able to describe the procedure:

  1. Weigh the solid accurately on a balance.
  2. Dissolve it in a small volume of distilled (or deionized) water in a beaker, stirring until none is left.
  3. Pour the solution through a funnel into the volumetric flask. Rinse the beaker, the stirring rod and the funnel with distilled water, and add the rinsings to the flask.
  4. Add distilled water until the bottom of the meniscus sits on the mark. Add the last drops with a dropping pipette.
  5. Stopper the flask and invert it several times to mix.

Markschemes reward specific words: volumetric flask, distilled or deionized water, transfer with rinsings, make up to the mark, stopper and invert to mix. Writing only “water” can lose a mark. “Add 250 cm3 of water” is wrong chemistry: the flask is filled up to the mark, so the solution, not the water, is 250.0 cm3.

Calculate the mass of hydrated copper(II) sulfate, CuSO4.5H2O, needed to prepare 100.0 cm3 of a 0.200 mol dm−3 solution. (M(CuSO4.5H2O) = 249.72 g mol−1)

  1. Route: solution → moles → mass. Start by converting 100.0 cm3 to dm3.
  2. n = 0.200 × 0.1000 = 0.0200 mol. Then m = n × M, using the molar mass of the hydrate, because that is the solid you weigh.
V = 100.0 cm3 ÷ 1000 = 0.1000 dm3
n = 0.200 mol dm−3 × 0.1000 dm3 = 0.0200 mol
m = 0.0200 mol × 249.72 g mol−1 = 4.9944 g

To 3 significant figures (set by 0.200): 4.99 g.

Dilution

Adding water to squash makes it weaker, but the syrup is all still there. Dilution lowers the concentration by adding solvent. The amount of solute does not change.

Amount before = amount after. Since n = CV, that gives the dilution relationship.

C1V1 = C2V2
C1
concentration of the stock solution mol dm-3
V1
volume of stock solution taken cm3
C2
concentration after dilution mol dm-3
V2
final total volume cm3

This relationship is not in the data booklet, so learn where it comes from. The two volumes may both be in cm3 or both in dm3, as long as they match. The dilution factor is V2 ÷ V1. A “10 times” dilution divides the concentration by 10.

Volume of stock solution for a dilution

What volume of 2.00 mol dm−3 hydrochloric acid is needed to prepare 250.0 cm3 of 0.100 mol dm−3 hydrochloric acid?

  1. “1” is the stock solution you start with. “2” is the diluted solution you end with. The unknown is V1.

    C1 = 2.00 mol dm−3   V1 = ?
    C2 = 0.100 mol dm−3   V2 = 250.0 cm3

  2. The amount of HCl is the same before and after: C1V1 = C2V2.

    V1 = C2V2C1

  3. The concentration units cancel, so V1 comes out in the same unit as V2.

    V1 = 0.100 mol dm−3 × 250.0 cm32.00 mol dm−3 = 12.5 cm3

  4. The concentration falls 20 times (2.00 ÷ 0.100), so the volume must grow 20 times.

    12.5 cm3 × 20 = 250 cm3

12.5 cm3 of the 2.00 mol dm−3 acid

You also need to describe the procedure. Markschemes look for these points:

  1. Use a pipette (or a burette) to measure the stock solution. A measuring cylinder is not precise enough.
  2. Run it into a volumetric flask of the right size, here 250.0 cm3.
  3. Add distilled water up to the mark.
  4. Stopper and invert several times to mix.

“Add 237.5 cm3 of water” is not the right description. V2 is the total final volume, and volumes do not always add exactly. The correct instruction is to make the solution up to the mark.

A serial dilution repeats the same step. Dilute a 1.00 mol dm−3 stock 10 times to get 0.100 mol dm−3. Dilute that 10 times to get 0.0100 mol dm−3, and so on. It reaches very low concentrations without measuring tiny volumes.

10.0 cm3 of a 0.500 mol dm−3 solution is pipetted into a 250.0 cm3 volumetric flask and made up to the mark with distilled water. Calculate the concentration of the diluted solution.

  1. Which values are C1, V1 and V2? The unknown is C2. It must be smaller than 0.500.
  2. C2 = C1V1 ÷ V2 = 0.500 × 10.0 ÷ 250.0.
C2 = 0.500 mol dm−3 × 10.0 cm3250.0 cm3 = 0.0200 mol dm−3

0.0200 mol dm−3. The dilution factor is 250.0 ÷ 10.0 = 25, and 0.500 ÷ 25 = 0.0200.

Finding a concentration with a calibration curve

Some solutions are coloured, and the more concentrated they are, the more light they absorb. A colorimeter or spectrophotometer measures this as a number called absorbance. That gives a quick way to find an unknown concentration.

  1. Make several standard solutions of known concentration, usually by diluting one stock solution.
  2. Measure the absorbance of each one.
  3. Plot absorbance (y) against concentration (x) and draw the line of best fit. This graph is the calibration curve.
  4. Measure the absorbance of the unknown solution and read its concentration from the line.

You do not need the Beer–Lambert law or any equation for absorbance. You only need the idea: known standards, a graph, a line of best fit, and reading the unknown from it. Concentration units such as ppm, molality, percentage by mass and mole fraction are not in the IB guide. Use mol dm-3 and g dm-3.

Titration calculations, where a solution of known concentration is used to find another through a reaction, belong to Reactivity 2.1 and Reactivity 3.1. They combine n = CV from this section with the mole ratio of an equation.

Structure 1.4.6

Avogadro’s law and reacting gas volumes

In a gas, the particles are far apart. Almost all of the volume is empty space, so the size of the particles themselves hardly matters. A balloon of hydrogen and a balloon of carbon dioxide of the same size, at the same temperature and pressure, hold the same number of molecules, even though the carbon dioxide molecules are much heavier.

Equal volumes of all gases, measured under the same conditions of temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules.

Equal numbers of molecules means equal amounts in moles. So for gases at the same temperature and pressure, volume is directly proportional to amount. Double the moles, double the volume.

This has a very useful result. In a balanced equation, the coefficients give the mole ratio. For gases, the same coefficients also give the volume ratio. You can work in volumes and never calculate a single mole.

Method in words

  1. Write the balanced equation with state symbols. Only substances marked (g) take part in the volume ratio.
  2. Check that all volumes are measured at the same temperature and pressure.
  3. Find the coefficient of the gas you know and of the gas you want.
  4. Volume wanted = volume known × (coefficient wanted ÷ coefficient known).
  5. Keep the same volume unit throughout. There is no need to convert cm3 to dm3, because it is a ratio.

Volumes of reacting gases

Propane burns completely in oxygen:

C3H8(g) + 5O2(g) -> 3CO2(g) + 4H2O(l)

25.0 cm3 of propane is burned. All volumes are measured at the same temperature and pressure. Calculate the volume of oxygen needed and the volume of carbon dioxide formed.

  1. The coefficients tell you how many moles react. An unwritten coefficient is 1.

    C3H8 : O2 : CO2 = 1 : 5 : 3

  2. Same temperature and pressure, so the volume ratio of the gases equals the mole ratio.

    volume ratio = 1 : 5 : 3

  3. Multiply by coefficient wanted ÷ coefficient known. The coefficients are exact, so the answers keep 3 significant figures.

    V(O2) = 25.0 cm3 × 51 = 125 cm3
    V(CO2) = 25.0 cm3 × 31 = 75.0 cm3

  4. The water is a liquid, (l). It is not a gas, so it adds nothing to the gas volume.

    gas before: 25.0 + 125 = 150 cm3   gas after: 75.0 cm3

125 cm3 of O2 is needed and 75.0 cm3 of CO2 forms

Volumes of gases are not conserved in a reaction. In the example, 150 cm3 of gas becomes 75.0 cm3. Mass is conserved and atoms are conserved, but the number of gas molecules can change. Also watch for H2O(l): counting liquid water as a gas volume is a common wrong answer.

Carbon monoxide burns in oxygen: 2CO(g) + O2(g) -> 2CO2(g). What volume of oxygen is needed to react completely with 150 cm3 of carbon monoxide, at the same temperature and pressure? Give your answer to 3 significant figures.

  1. What is the mole ratio of CO to O2 in the equation? The volume ratio is the same.
  2. CO : O2 = 2 : 1, so V(O2) = 150 × (1 ÷ 2).
V(O2) = 150 cm3 × 12 = 75.0 cm3

75.0 cm3 of oxygen.

Ammonia is made from nitrogen and hydrogen: N2(g) + 3H2(g) -> 2NH3(g). What volume of ammonia forms when 45.0 cm3 of hydrogen reacts completely, at the same temperature and pressure?

  1. Find the coefficients of the gas you know (H2) and the gas you want (NH3).
  2. H2 : NH3 = 3 : 2, so V(NH3) = 45.0 × (2 ÷ 3).
V(NH3) = 45.0 cm3 × 23 = 30.0 cm3

30.0 cm3 of ammonia. It also needs 45.0 ÷ 3 = 15.0 cm3 of nitrogen.

When one gas is in excess

Sometimes more of one gas is supplied than the reaction needs. The question then says that gas is “in excess”. Some of it is left over at the end, mixed with the gases that were made.

  1. Use the volume ratio to find how much of the excess gas reacts.
  2. Gas left over = volume supplied − volume that reacted.
  3. Gas at the end = gas left over + gaseous products. Leave out anything marked (l) or (s).

Gas left at the end

10.0 cm3 of methane is burned in 50.0 cm3 of oxygen, which is an excess:

CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)

All volumes are measured at the same temperature and pressure. Calculate the total volume of gas at the end.

  1. All the methane burns, because oxygen is in excess. The ratio CH4 : O2 is 1 : 2.

    V(O2 reacting) = 10.0 cm3 × 21 = 20.0 cm3

  2. What was supplied, minus what was used.

    V(O2 left) = 50.0 cm3 − 20.0 cm3 = 30.0 cm3

  3. The ratio CH4 : CO2 is 1 : 1. The water is a liquid, so it is not counted.

    V(CO2) = 10.0 cm3 × 11 = 10.0 cm3

  4. The container now holds the oxygen left over and the carbon dioxide made.

    V(total) = 30.0 cm3 + 10.0 cm3 = 40.0 cm3

40.0 cm3 of gas: 30.0 cm3 of O2 and 10.0 cm3 of CO2

40.0 cm3 of carbon monoxide is burned in 50.0 cm3 of oxygen, which is an excess: 2CO(g) + O2(g) -> 2CO2(g). Calculate the total volume of gas at the end, at the same temperature and pressure.

  1. Two gases are present at the end: the oxygen left over and the carbon dioxide made. Find each one.
  2. O2 reacting = 40.0 × (1 ÷ 2) = 20.0 cm3, so 30.0 cm3 is left. CO2 made = 40.0 × (2 ÷ 2).
V(O2 reacting) = 40.0 cm3 × 12 = 20.0 cm3
V(O2 left) = 50.0 cm3 − 20.0 cm3 = 30.0 cm3
V(CO2) = 40.0 cm3 × 22 = 40.0 cm3

Total: 30.0 + 40.0 = 70.0 cm3.

If you are asked to state Avogadro’s law, include all three parts: equal volumes of gases, same temperature and pressure, equal numbers of molecules. In a calculation, write “volume ratio = mole ratio” before you use the coefficients.

The molar volume of a gas (22.7 dm3 mol−1 at STP) and PV = nRT are in topic 1.5. In this topic, the question tells you which gas is in excess. Working out for yourself which reactant runs out first (the limiting reactant) is taught in Reactivity 2.1.

Linking it up

The guide lists linking questions that connect this topic to others. Here are short answers to four of them.

How can molar masses be used with chemical equations to determine the masses of the products of a reaction?

An equation gives a ratio in moles, but a balance measures grams. So you convert the mass of a reactant to moles with its molar mass, use the mole ratio from the equation, then convert the moles of product back to a mass with the product’s molar mass. The two outer steps are from this topic. The full method is taught in Reactivity 2.1.

What is the importance of approximation in the determination of an empirical formula?

Measured masses always carry some error, so the mole ratio never comes out as perfect whole numbers. You have to judge when 2.99 means 3. You also have to judge when a value is not an error: 1.50 or 1.33 are real ratios that must be multiplied up, not rounded. Good approximation means rounding small differences and respecting large ones.

What are the considerations in the choice of glassware used in preparing a standard solution and a serial dilution?

The concentration is only as accurate as the volumes. A volumetric flask and a pipette are each made to deliver one volume very precisely, so they are used for the final volume and for the portion of stock solution. A beaker or measuring cylinder has a much larger uncertainty and is only used for rough work, such as dissolving the solid. In a serial dilution, any error in one step is carried into every later step, which makes precise glassware even more important.

Avogadro’s law applies to ideal gases. Under what conditions might a real gas deviate most from an ideal gas?

At low temperature and high pressure. Then the particles are close together and moving slowly, so the forces between them and the space the particles take up are no longer negligible. Topic 1.5 explains this.
