Structure 1.3.1

Light, photons and emission spectra

Sprinkle salt into a gas flame and the flame turns yellow. A neon sign glows red. Fireworks get their colours from different metal compounds. In each case, atoms that have been given energy are giving it back out as light.

The pattern of light an element gives out is its emission spectrum. This section explains where that light comes from.

How an atom emits light

Electrons in an atom can only have certain fixed amounts of energy. These allowed energies are called energy levels. Think of a ladder: you can stand on a rung, but not between rungs.

A photon is one packet of light energy. The excited state does not last. When the electron falls, the atom emits exactly one photon, and the photon carries away exactly the energy difference between the two levels.

Emission spectra are produced by atoms emitting photons when electrons in excited states return to lower energy levels. A big energy gap gives a high-energy photon. A small gap gives a low-energy photon.

Colour, wavelength, frequency and energy

Light is one kind of electromagnetic radiation. So are radio waves, microwaves, infrared (IR), ultraviolet (UV), X-rays and gamma rays. Together they make up the electromagnetic spectrum. They differ in wavelength and frequency.

Wavelength is the distance from one wave peak to the next. Frequency is the number of waves passing a point each second. All electromagnetic radiation travels at the same speed in a vacuum, so if the waves are shorter, more of them pass each second.

Section 5 of the data booklet shows the electromagnetic spectrum on a wavelength scale, with an arrow for increasing energy and the visible region from 400 nm to 700 nm. Section 15 gives the wavelengths of each colour. You do not need to memorize the numbers.

Continuous and line spectra

Pass white light from a hot filament through a prism and you see every colour, merging smoothly from red to violet. That is a continuous spectrum: it contains all the wavelengths in a range.

Now pass an electric current through a tube of low-pressure hydrogen gas. The tube glows. Send that light through a prism and you see only a few sharp coloured lines on a dark background. That is a line spectrum: it contains only certain separate wavelengths.

In the lab, the light source is a gas discharge tube and the light is split with a prism or a diffraction grating. You can record what you see (the colours and pattern of lines) and what you measure (the wavelength of each line).

“Shorter wavelength means lower energy” is the wrong way round. Shorter waves have a higher frequency, and a higher frequency means more energy per photon. Blue and violet photons carry more energy than red ones.

To distinguish the two spectra, say what each contains: a continuous spectrum has all wavelengths (or frequencies) in a range; a line spectrum has only certain, discrete wavelengths (or frequencies).

At SL the link between energy, frequency and wavelength is descriptive only. There are no calculations with E = hf or c = λf in the SL part of this topic.

Structure 1.3.2

The hydrogen emission spectrum

Hydrogen is the simplest atom: one proton and one electron. Its line spectrum is the clearest evidence we have that electrons in atoms have fixed energy levels.

Why lines are evidence for energy levels

If the electron in a hydrogen atom could have any energy, it could lose any amount of energy when it falls. The atom would then emit photons of every energy, and you would see a continuous spectrum.

That is not what happens. Hydrogen emits only certain wavelengths. So the electron can only lose certain amounts of energy. So the electron can only have certain energies. We say the energy levels are discrete.

Each line in the spectrum is produced by electrons falling between one particular pair of energy levels. The line emission spectrum of hydrogen is evidence that electrons exist in discrete energy levels.

The energy levels of hydrogen

The main energy levels are numbered n = 1, 2, 3, … going up in energy. n = 1 is the lowest. The levels are not evenly spaced. The gap from n = 1 to n = 2 is large. Each gap after that is smaller. The levels get closer and closer together and converge at high energy. The point where they meet is labelled n = ∞.

Three sets of lines

The lines fall into sets. Each set contains all the transitions that end on the same lower level.

Transitions in the hydrogen emission spectrum
Electron falls toSize of the energy dropRegion of the spectrum
n = 1largestultraviolet (UV)
n = 2mediumvisible (from n = 3, 4, 5, 6)
n = 3smallestinfrared (IR)

The four visible lines come from electrons falling to n = 2. The red line is the smallest of these drops (from n = 3). The violet line is the largest of the four (from n = 6).

Convergence

Within each set, the lines get closer together as the frequency gets higher. Eventually they merge. This happens because the energy levels themselves get closer together at higher energy. A drop from n = 6 is only a little bigger than a drop from n = 5, so their two lines sit close together.

The lines converge at the high-energy end of each set: high frequency, short wavelength. They do not converge at the low-energy end. In the visible set, the lines bunch together towards violet, not towards red.

The visible lines end on n = 2, not on n = 1. Drops to n = 1 release so much energy that the photons are ultraviolet, which you cannot see.

“Describe the emission spectrum of hydrogen” wants: a series of lines (not a continuous band); the lines converge at higher frequency or energy; lines arise from electron transitions from higher levels down to a lower level (n = 2 for visible). On a diagram, emission arrows point downwards. An arrow pointing up shows an electron gaining energy, which is not emission.

The names of the sets of lines (Lyman, Balmer, Paschen) will not be assessed. You do not need the Rydberg equation, a formula for the energy of each level, or the energy values printed on the diagram above. Transitions ending on levels above n = 3 are not required either.

Structure 1.3.4

Sublevels and orbital shapes

Stay with the building picture. Each floor (main energy level) is divided into corridors called sublevels. Each corridor contains rooms called orbitals. Each room holds at most two electrons.

There are four types of sublevel: s, p, d and f. Within one main energy level, their energy increases in that order: s is lowest, then p, then d, then f.

What an orbital looks like

An electron is not a tiny planet on a fixed track. We cannot say exactly where it is. An orbital is a region of space where there is a high probability of finding the electron. You need to recognize two shapes.

“Orbit”, “orbital”, “sublevel” and “energy level” are not the same thing. An orbital is one region holding up to two electrons. A sublevel is a set of orbitals of the same type (the 2p sublevel has three orbitals). A main energy level is a set of sublevels.

You only need to recognize the s shape and the three p shapes. Shapes of d and f orbitals, quantum numbers, nodes, wave functions, the Schrödinger equation and the Heisenberg uncertainty principle are not in the IB Chemistry guide.

How many orbitals are there in total in the n = 3 main energy level?

  1. List the sublevels in n = 3 first. There are three types.
  2. 3s has 1 orbital, 3p has 3 and 3d has 5. Add them.
1 (3s) + 3 (3p) + 5 (3d) = 9 orbitals

Check: 9 orbitals × 2 electrons = 18 electrons, which matches 2n2 for n = 3.

Structure 1.3.5

Writing electron configurations

An electron configuration is the address list for the electrons in an atom: which sublevels are occupied, and by how many electrons. For sodium it is 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s1. The big number is the main energy level, the letter is the sublevel, and the superscript is the number of electrons in it.

Three rules for filling orbitals

Picture people getting onto a bus. They take the nearest seats first. They each take an empty double seat before anyone sits next to a stranger. And a double seat holds two people at most. Electrons follow three rules that work the same way.

The three rules, and what each one tells you to do
RuleWhat it saysIn a diagram
Aufbau principleElectrons fill the lowest-energy sublevel that has space, before going into a higher one.Fill from the bottom up.
Hund’s ruleWithin a sublevel, every orbital gets one electron before any orbital gets a second. The single electrons have the same spin.One arrow in each box first, all pointing the same way. Then go back and pair.
Pauli exclusion principleAn orbital holds at most two electrons, and the two must have opposite spins.Never more than two arrows in a box: one up, one down.

Spin is a property of an electron with two possible values. We draw them as an up arrow and a down arrow.

The filling order

For the elements you need (up to Z = 36), the sublevels fill in this order:

1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p
s
holds up to 2 electrons
p
holds up to 6 electrons
d
holds up to 10 electrons

The one surprise is that 4s fills before 3d. In an atom where both are empty, the 4s sublevel is slightly lower in energy than 3d, so potassium and calcium put their outer electrons into 4s.

Three ways to show a configuration

Full configuration. Every sublevel is written out: 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p4 for sulfur.

Condensed configuration. The inner electrons are replaced by the symbol of the previous noble gas in square brackets: [Ne] 3s2 3p4 for sulfur. The noble gas cores you need are [He] = 2 electrons, [Ne] = 10 and [Ar] = 18.

Orbital diagram (arrow-in-box diagram). Each orbital is a box and each electron is an arrow. Boxes for higher-energy sublevels are drawn higher up. This is the only notation that shows Hund’s rule and spin.

Configuration and orbital diagram of iron

Deduce the full electron configuration and the condensed electron configuration of an iron atom, Fe (Z = 26). Draw the orbital diagram for its 4s and 3d sublevels.

  1. A neutral atom has the same number of electrons as protons, and Z is the number of protons.

    Fe: Z = 26, so 26 electrons

  2. Aufbau principle: lowest energy first. Keep a running total so you know when to stop.

    1s2 (2) 2s2 (4) 2p6 (10) 3s2 (12) 3p6 (18) 4s2 (20)
    6 electrons left → 3d6 (26)

  3. Check that the superscripts add up to 26.

    1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6 4s2 3d6
    2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 = 26

  4. The first 18 electrons, up to 3p6, are the configuration of argon. Replace them with [Ar].

    [Ar] 4s2 3d6

  5. Hund’s rule: put one electron in each of the five 3d boxes first (5 electrons), then pair the sixth. Pauli: the pair has opposite spins.

    4s ↑↓ 3d ↑↓↑↑↑↑
    4 unpaired electrons

Full: 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6 4s2 3d6. Condensed: [Ar] 4s2 3d6. In a full diagram the 3d boxes are drawn slightly higher than the 4s box.

Examiners accept the 4s and 3d sublevels in either order: [Ar] 4s2 3d6 or [Ar] 3d6 4s2. This site writes them in filling order. If the question says “full”, do not use a noble gas core.

Two exceptions: chromium and copper

Following the filling order, you would expect chromium to be [Ar] 4s2 3d4 and copper to be [Ar] 4s2 3d9. Both are wrong. In each atom, one electron sits in 3d instead of 4s.

Chromium and copper

State the condensed electron configurations of a chromium atom (Z = 24) and a copper atom (Z = 29), and the number of unpaired electrons in each.

  1. Argon accounts for 18 electrons. The rest go into 4s and 3d.

    Cr: 24 − 18 = 6 electrons Cu: 29 − 18 = 11 electrons

  2. Cr and Cu are the two atoms where 4s holds one electron, not two. If the filling order gives you 3d4 or 3d9, stop and move one electron from 4s to 3d.

    Cr: 4s2 3d4 → [Ar] 4s1 3d5
    Cu: 4s2 3d9 → [Ar] 4s1 3d10

  3. Hund’s rule: five d electrons occupy the five d orbitals singly. Ten d electrons fill all five orbitals in pairs.

    Cr: 1 (in 4s) + 5 (in 3d) = 6 unpaired
    Cu: 1 (in 4s) + 0 (in 3d) = 1 unpaired

Cr: [Ar] 4s1 3d5, 6 unpaired electrons. Cu: [Ar] 4s1 3d10, 1 unpaired electron.

Cr and Cu are the only exceptions you need, and you are asked to know them, not to explain them. You do not need configurations beyond Z = 36 (krypton), or exceptions among heavier elements.

Ions

For an ion, start from the atom and then change the number of electrons.

  • Negative ion: add electrons to the next available space. Cl- has 17 + 1 = 18 electrons.
  • Positive ion: remove electrons from the highest main energy level. Ca^2+ has 20 − 2 = 18 electrons.

The ions in calcium sulfide

Calcium sulfide, CaS, contains Ca^2+ and S^2- ions. Deduce the full electron configuration of each ion.

  1. An ion’s configuration is built from the atom’s. Ca has Z = 20 and S has Z = 16.

    Ca: 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6 4s2
    S: 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p4

  2. A 2+ ion has lost two electrons. They come from the highest main energy level, which is 4s.

    Ca2+: 20 − 2 = 18 electrons → 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6

  3. A 2− ion has gained two electrons. They go into the next space, which is in 3p.

    S2−: 16 + 2 = 18 electrons → 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6

  4. Both ions have 18 electrons, the same configuration as an argon atom. That is a good sign for ions of main-group elements.

    Ca2+ = S2− = [Ar]

Both ions are 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6.

Do not write an ion with the atom’s configuration. S^2- is not …3p4. Always adjust the electron count for the charge: a negative charge means extra electrons, a positive charge means fewer.

Ions of the d-block metals: 4s goes first

When a first-row d-block atom (Sc to Zn) forms a positive ion, the 4s electrons are removed before any 3d electrons. This seems backwards, because 4s filled first. But once the 3d sublevel contains electrons, it is lower in energy than 4s. The 4s electrons are then the outermost and the easiest to remove.

The syllabus places these ions under Structure 3.1 at HL, so you will meet them again there. They also appear in questions on this topic.

Iron(II) and iron(III) ions

Deduce the condensed electron configurations of Fe^2+ and Fe^3+.

  1. You cannot write the ion without the atom. Do not start from the electron count of the ion and fill from 1s.

    Fe: [Ar] 4s2 3d6

  2. For d-block ions, the 4s electrons are lost first.

    Fe2+: [Ar] 3d6

  3. 4s is now empty, so the next electron comes from 3d.

    Fe3+: [Ar] 3d5

  4. Fe2+ should have 26 − 2 = 24 electrons and Fe3+ should have 26 − 3 = 23.

    Fe2+: 18 + 6 = 24 Fe3+: 18 + 5 = 23

Fe2+ is [Ar] 3d6 and Fe3+ is [Ar] 3d5.

Writing Fe^2+ as [Ar] 4s2 3d4 removes the electrons from 3d. That is the most common error with these ions. Take them from 4s. For ions of Cr and Cu, start from the real atom configurations (4s1): Cu^2+ is [Ar] 3d9 and Cr^3+ is [Ar] 3d3.

How many unpaired electrons are there in an Fe^3+ ion?

  1. Write the configuration of the ion first. Which electrons leave an iron atom first?
  2. Fe^3+ is [Ar] 3d5. Put five electrons into five d orbitals using Hund’s rule.
Fe3+: [Ar] 3d5 3d ↑↑↑↑↑

Each of the five 3d orbitals holds one electron, so there are 5 unpaired electrons.

How many unpaired electrons are there in a phosphorus atom (Z = 15) in its ground state?

  1. Write the configuration. Only a partly filled sublevel can contain unpaired electrons.
  2. P is [Ne] 3s2 3p3. Place three electrons in the three 3p orbitals using Hund’s rule.
P: [Ne] 3s2 3p3 3p ↑↑↑

Hund’s rule puts one electron in each 3p orbital, so there are 3 unpaired electrons.

How many electrons are in the 3d sublevel of a copper atom (Z = 29)?

  1. Copper is one of the two exceptions. What happens to its 4s sublevel?
  2. Cu is [Ar] 4s1 3d?. There are 29 − 18 = 11 electrons after the argon core, and one of them is in 4s.
Cu: [Ar] 4s1 3d10 11 − 1 = 10

The 3d sublevel holds 10 electrons. Following the filling order without the exception would give 9, which is wrong.

Structure 1.3.6

The convergence limit and ionization energy

Go back to the hydrogen energy levels. The levels converge at n = ∞. An electron that reaches n = ∞ is no longer held by the nucleus. It has left the atom. The atom has been ionized.

The first ionization energy (first IE) is the minimum energy needed to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms in their ground state:

X(g) -> X+(g) + e-
X(g)
one mole of gaseous atoms
X+(g)
one mole of gaseous ions with a 1+ charge
IE
the energy needed for this change, in kJ mol-1

For hydrogen, that means lifting the electron from n = 1 to n = ∞. Now look at the emission spectrum. The lines from transitions down to n = 1 converge at a limit. A photon at that limit has exactly the energy of a fall from n = ∞ to n = 1. That is the same energy gap as ionization, travelled in the opposite direction.

In an emission spectrum, the limit of convergence at higher frequency corresponds to ionization. Find the frequency or wavelength of that limit, and you can calculate the first ionization energy.

In the hydrogen explorer above, choose “falls from ∞” and “to 1” to see this limit and its calculation.

Both equations, and the values of h, c and the Avogadro constant (6.02 × 1023 mol-1), are in the data booklet. First ionization energies of the elements are in section 9.

E = hf gives the energy of one photon, which ionizes one atom. Ionization energies are quoted per mole of atoms, in kJ. So there are always two extra steps: multiply by the Avogadro constant, and convert J to kJ.

Method in words

From the convergence limit to the first IE:

  1. If you are given a wavelength, convert it to metres (1 nm = 1 × 10−9 m), then find the frequency with f = c / λ.
  2. Find the energy of one photon with E = hf. This is in joules, for one atom.
  3. Multiply by 6.02 × 1023 to get joules per mole.
  4. Divide by 1000 to get kJ mol-1.

From the first IE to the convergence limit (the same steps, backwards):

  1. Multiply by 1000 to convert kJ mol-1 to J mol-1.
  2. Divide by 6.02 × 1023 to get joules for one atom.
  3. Find the frequency with f = E / h.
  4. Find the wavelength with λ = c / f. Convert to nm if asked.

From convergence limit to first ionization energy

In the emission spectrum of hydrogen, the lines from transitions to n = 1 converge at a wavelength of 91.2 nm. Calculate the first ionization energy of hydrogen, in kJ mol-1.

  1. The speed of light is in m s−1, so the wavelength must be in m. Nano means × 10−9.

    λ = 91.2 nm = 91.2 × 10−9 m = 9.12 × 10−8 m

  2. E = hf needs a frequency. Rearrange c = λf to get f = c / λ.

    f = c / λ = (3.00 × 108 m s−1) / (9.12 × 10−8 m)
    f = 3.289… × 1015 s−1

  3. One photon at the limit has the energy needed to ionize one atom. Keep the unrounded frequency in your calculator.

    E = hf = (6.63 × 10−34 J s) × (3.289… × 1015 s−1)
    E = 2.180… × 10−18 J

  4. Ionization energy is per mole of atoms. One mole is 6.02 × 1023 atoms.

    2.180… × 10−18 J × 6.02 × 1023 mol−1 = 1.312… × 106 J mol−1

  5. Divide by 1000. The data have 3 significant figures, so the answer does too.

    1.312… × 106 J mol−1 ÷ 1000 = 1312.9… kJ mol−1
    IE = 1310 kJ mol−1 (3 s.f.)

1310 kJ mol−1 (1.31 × 103 kJ mol−1). The data booklet value is 1312 kJ mol−1, so the answer is sensible.

From first ionization energy to wavelength

The first ionization energy of sodium is 496 kJ mol−1. Calculate the wavelength, in nm, of the convergence limit in the emission spectrum of sodium that corresponds to this ionization.

  1. The Planck constant is in J s, so the energy must be in J.

    496 kJ mol−1 × 1000 = 4.96 × 105 J mol−1

  2. E = hf is about one photon and one atom. Divide by the number of atoms in a mole.

    E = (4.96 × 105 J mol−1) / (6.02 × 1023 mol−1)
    E = 8.239… × 10−19 J

  3. Rearrange E = hf to get f = E / h.

    f = E / h = (8.239… × 10−19 J) / (6.63 × 10−34 J s)
    f = 1.242… × 1015 s−1

  4. Rearrange c = λf to get λ = c / f. The answer comes out in metres.

    λ = c / f = (3.00 × 108 m s−1) / (1.242… × 1015 s−1)
    λ = 2.414… × 10−7 m

  5. 1 nm = 1 × 10−9 m, so divide by 10−9. Three significant figures in the data.

    λ = 2.414… × 10−7 m = 241.4… × 10−9 m = 241 nm

241 nm (2.41 × 10−7 m). This is shorter than 400 nm, so the limit is in the ultraviolet.

Three slips cost most of the marks here. Leaving the wavelength in nm gives a frequency that is 109 times too small. Forgetting the Avogadro constant gives an answer that is wrong by a factor of 6.02 × 1023. Forgetting kJ ↔ J gives an answer wrong by 1000. Write the unit on every line and you will catch them.

This calculation is usually worth 3 marks: one for the energy per atom (the Avogadro constant and the kJ ↔ J step), one for using E = hf, one for using c = λf. A wrong early step can still earn later marks if your working is clear, so show every line.

The convergence limit in the emission spectrum of helium that corresponds to its first ionization has a frequency of 5.94 × 1015 Hz. Calculate the first ionization energy of helium in kJ mol−1.

  1. You already have the frequency, so you can skip c = λf. Which equation turns a frequency into an energy?
  2. E = hf = 6.63 × 10−34 × 5.94 × 1015 gives joules for one atom. Then two more steps: per mole, and J to kJ.
E = hf = (6.63 × 10−34 J s) × (5.94 × 1015 s−1) = 3.938… × 10−18 J
3.938… × 10−18 J × 6.02 × 1023 mol−1 = 2.370… × 106 J mol−1
÷ 1000 = 2370.8… kJ mol−1 = 2370 kJ mol−1 (3 s.f.)

The data booklet value for helium is 2372 kJ mol−1.

The first ionization energy of lithium is 520 kJ mol−1. Calculate the wavelength, in nm, of the convergence limit that corresponds to this ionization.

  1. Work backwards: kJ mol−1 → J mol−1 → J for one atom → frequency → wavelength.
  2. Energy for one atom: 520 × 1000 / (6.02 × 1023) J. Then f = E / h and λ = c / f. Your wavelength will be in metres.

You do not need the electronvolt, the photoelectric effect, or calculations that use a formula for the energy of each level. Everything here uses only E = hf, c = λf and the Avogadro constant.

Structure 1.3.6

Trends in first ionization energy

How hard is it to pull the outermost electron off an atom? It depends on how strongly the nucleus attracts that electron. Three things decide that.

  • Nuclear charge. More protons means a stronger pull.
  • Distance. An electron in a higher main energy level is further from the nucleus, so the pull is weaker.
  • Shielding. Electrons in inner main energy levels sit between the nucleus and the outer electron. They repel the outer electron and cancel part of the pull. This is called shielding.

Across a period: a general increase

From left to right across a period, each atom has one more proton. The extra electrons go into the same main energy level, so the shielding by inner electrons stays about the same. The outer electrons are pulled in more strongly, and more energy is needed to remove one.

Down a group: a decrease

Going down a group, the outer electron is in a higher main energy level each time. It is further from the nucleus and there are more inner electrons shielding it. These two effects outweigh the larger nuclear charge, so less energy is needed to remove it.

Two discontinuities in each period

The increase across a period is not smooth. A discontinuity is a place where the trend briefly goes the other way. There are two in period 2, and the same two in period 3.

The orbital diagrams show the second case clearly:

N 2p ↑↑↑ O 2p ↑↓↑↑

These discontinuities are evidence that main energy levels are divided into sublevels. If n = 2 were a single level, first ionization energy would rise steadily from Li to Ne.

Explaining a discontinuity

(a) Explain why the first ionization energy of boron (801 kJ mol−1) is lower than that of beryllium (900 kJ mol−1). (b) Explain why the first ionization energy of oxygen (1314 kJ mol−1) is lower than that of nitrogen (1402 kJ mol−1).

  1. The explanation is always about which electron is removed. You cannot see that without the configurations.

    Be: 1s2 2s2 B: 1s2 2s2 2p1
    N: 1s2 2s2 2p3 O: 1s2 2s2 2p4

  2. One mark is for identifying the two sublevels.

    Be loses a 2s electron. B loses a 2p electron.

  3. The second mark is for the reason, stated in terms of the energy of the electron removed.

    2p is higher in energy than 2s,
    so less energy is needed to remove the 2p electron.

  4. Both atoms lose a 2p electron, so the sublevel is not the difference this time. Look at the orbitals.

    N: three 2p orbitals, each with one electron.
    O: one 2p orbital holds a pair of electrons.

  5. Repulsion raises the energy of the paired electron, which makes it easier to remove.

    The two electrons in the same orbital repel each other,
    so less energy is needed to remove one of them.

(a) The electron removed from B is in the 2p sublevel, which is higher in energy than the 2s sublevel that Be loses an electron from, so it is removed more easily. (b) The electron removed from O is paired in a 2p orbital; repulsion between the two electrons in that orbital means it is removed more easily than an electron from one of the singly occupied 2p orbitals in N.

Base your explanation on the energy of the electron being removed. Do not write that a half-filled or full sublevel is “specially stable”: the syllabus asks you not to, and examiners have refused that kind of answer. Name the sublevels or orbitals, then say why the electron is easier to remove.

Shielding does not increase across a period, and atoms do not get bigger across a period. The outer electrons are in the same main energy level, so the number of inner, shielding electrons stays the same while the nuclear charge rises.

You are not asked to calculate an “effective nuclear charge” or to explain the small changes across the d-block (Sc to Zn). The periodic trend in ionization energy comes back in Structure 3.1, with atomic radius and electronegativity. There, the general trend across a period and down a group is also SL content. The discontinuities stay HL only.

Structure 1.3.7

Successive ionization energies

You can keep removing electrons from an atom, one at a time. The energies needed are the successive ionization energies: first, second, third and so on. The second ionization energy is for this change:

X+(g) -> X^2+(g) + e-
X+(g)
one mole of gaseous 1+ ions
X2+(g)
one mole of gaseous 2+ ions

Two patterns to explain

They always increase. Each electron is pulled away from an ion with a greater positive charge than the last. The same nuclear charge attracts fewer electrons, and there is less repulsion between the electrons that remain. The remaining electrons are held more strongly.

Sometimes there is a large jump. A large jump means the next electron comes from a main energy level closer to the nucleus. That electron is nearer to the nucleus and has fewer inner electrons shielding it, so it is held far more strongly.

Count the electrons removed before the first large jump. That is the number of electrons in the outer main energy level, and it tells you the group. One electron before the jump: group 1. Three before the jump: group 13.

Why the graph uses a log scale

Successive ionization energies cover a huge range. For sodium, the first is 496 kJ mol−1 and the last is 159 076 kJ mol−1. On an ordinary scale, the small early values are squashed flat against the axis and the jumps among them disappear. Plotting log(IE) compresses the range, so every jump is visible on one graph.

A log scale also shows what matters: how many times bigger each value is than the one before, not how many kJ bigger.

Deducing the group from data

The first six successive ionization energies of a period 3 element X, in kJ mol−1, are: 738, 1451, 7733, 10 543, 13 630, 18 020. Deduce the group of X, identify X, and state its electron configuration.

  1. You are looking for one step that is much bigger than the others. Divide each value by the previous one.

    1451 / 738 = 1.97 7733 / 1451 = 5.33 10 543 / 7733 = 1.36
    13 630 / 10 543 = 1.29 18 020 / 13 630 = 1.32

  2. Most steps are less than 2 times. One step is more than 5 times.

    Large jump between the 2nd and 3rd ionization energies

  3. Electrons removed before the jump are in the outer main energy level. The third electron comes from the next level in.

    2 outer electrons → group 2

  4. Period 3 means the outer electrons are in n = 3. Group 2 means there are two of them, in 3s.

    X = Mg: 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2

X is in group 2. It is magnesium, 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2.

Two slips to avoid. First, off by one: if the jump is between the 3rd and 4th values, the element has 3 outer electrons, not 4. Second, do not judge by the size of the gap in kJ. In the magnesium data the gap from the 8th to the 9th value is 5992 kJ mol−1, almost as big as the true jump (6282 kJ mol−1), but it is only a 1.23 times increase.

Give the IUPAC group number. Three outer electrons means group 13, not group 3. Five means group 15. To explain a jump, write: the electron is removed from a main energy level closer to the nucleus, with less shielding, so it is attracted more strongly.

The first seven successive ionization energies of an element, in kJ mol−1, are: 1012, 1907, 2914, 4964, 6274, 21 267, 25 431. Which group of the periodic table is the element in?

  1. Find the step where the value becomes several times bigger. Count how many electrons were removed before it.
  2. The large jump is from 6274 to 21 267, between the 5th and 6th values. So there are 5 outer electrons. Which group has 5 outer electrons?
21 267 / 6274 = 3.39 (all other steps are less than 2 times)

The jump comes after the 5th electron, so the element has 5 outer electrons. It is in group 15. (The data are for phosphorus.)

You never need to remember ionization energy values. Questions give you the data, as a table or a graph. Databases are a good source if you want to plot your own.

Linking it up

The syllabus asks questions that connect this topic to others. Here are short answers to four of them.

How do emission spectra provide evidence for the existence of different elements?

Every element has its own set of energy levels, because every element has a different nuclear charge and a different number of electrons. So every element emits its own pattern of lines, like a fingerprint. A set of lines that matches no known element points to a new one. Helium was detected in the spectrum of the Sun before it was found on Earth.

How does an element’s highest main energy level relate to its period number?

They are the same number. Sodium is 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s1. Its highest occupied main energy level is n = 3, and it is in period 3. You will use this in Structure 3.1.

What is the relationship between energy sublevels and the blocks of the periodic table?

The block tells you which type of sublevel is being filled. Groups 1 and 2 are the s-block. Groups 13 to 18 are the p-block. Groups 3 to 12 are the d-block. The widths match the sublevels: 2 columns for s (2 electrons), 6 for p, 10 for d. This is developed in Structure 3.1.

Why are log scales useful when discussing ionization energies and [H+]?

Both quantities range over many powers of ten. A log scale turns “ten times bigger” into “one unit higher”, so very large and very small values fit on one readable axis. For ionization energies that makes the jumps visible. For hydrogen ion concentration it gives the pH scale, which you will meet in Reactivity 3.1.

How do successive ionization energies help to explain variable oxidation states?

For a first-row transition element, the 4s and 3d electrons are close in energy, so the first few successive ionization energies rise gradually with no large jump between them. Several different numbers of electrons can be lost for a similar energy cost. That is why iron forms both Fe^2+ and Fe^3+. This is developed in Structure 3.1 at HL.
