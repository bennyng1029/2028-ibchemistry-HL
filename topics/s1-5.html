Ideal gases

Structure 1.5.1

The ideal gas model

Pump up a bicycle tyre and you can feel the gas pushing back. That push is pressure. It comes from billions of tiny particles hitting the inside of the tyre.

Real gas particles are complicated. So chemists use a simplified picture called an ideal gas. It is a model: it leaves out details to make the mathematics manageable.

The model makes four assumptions. Learn these four.

  1. The gas consists of moving particles.
  2. The particles have negligible volume. The particles themselves take up almost none of the space in the container.
  3. There are no intermolecular forces. The particles do not attract or repel each other.
  4. All collisions between particles are elastic.

An elastic collision is one where no kinetic energy is lost overall. The particles bounce apart with the same total kinetic energy they had before.

Negligible means small enough to ignore. A gas at room conditions is almost entirely empty space, so ignoring the size of the particles is usually a very good approximation.

An ideal gas does not exist. It is a model that real gases follow closely under everyday conditions. That is why one equation works for hydrogen, oxygen and carbon dioxide alike.

If you are asked to state assumptions, use the exact phrases: negligible volume (of the particles), no intermolecular forces, elastic collisions. Writing “the gas has no volume” loses the mark. The gas fills its container. It is the particles whose volume is negligible.

Textbooks often list more assumptions and go on to derive gas pressure from particle speeds. The four statements above are what the IB guide lists. You do not need the kinetic energy equation (E = 3/2 RT), root-mean-square speeds or any derivation.

Structure 1.5.2

Real gases and the limits of the model

Cool steam and it turns into liquid water. An ideal gas could never do that, because its particles do not attract each other. So real gases cannot be exactly ideal.

A real gas breaks two of the assumptions. Its particles do have some volume, and they do attract each other. Under most conditions both effects are tiny. Under two conditions they are not.

High pressure: particle volume matters

At high pressure the gas is squeezed into a small space. The particles are close together. Their own volume is now a noticeable share of the container, so it is no longer negligible. Because the particles are close, the forces between them are also more significant.

Low temperature: intermolecular forces matter

At low temperature the particles have less kinetic energy and move more slowly. Slow particles are affected more by the attractions between them, so the intermolecular forces are no longer negligible. Cool a real gas far enough and those forces pull the particles together into a liquid.

Real gases deviate most from ideal behaviour at low temperature and high pressure. They behave most like an ideal gas at high temperature and low pressure.

Which gases are closest to ideal?

Gases with weak intermolecular forces and small particles match the model best. Helium and hydrogen are very close to ideal under ordinary conditions. Gases with stronger attractions between their molecules, such as ammonia, deviate more under the same conditions.

You will study the types of intermolecular force in Structure 2.2. For now, one clue is enough: a gas with a higher boiling point has stronger forces between its particles.

For “explain why a real gas deviates” questions, give a condition and a reason for each mark. High pressure: particles are close together, so the volume of the particles is not negligible. Low temperature: particles move more slowly, so intermolecular forces become significant.

Do not swap the conditions. “High temperature and low pressure” describes where a gas is most ideal. A quick check: think of where a gas turns into a liquid (cold and squeezed). That is where it is least ideal.

No mathematics is required for real gases. You do not need the van der Waals equation, the compressibility factor Z, or any calculation of how far a gas deviates. A reasoned explanation in words is all the IB asks for.

Structure 1.5.3

Molar volume of an ideal gas

Picture a box a little under 30 cm along each side, about the space taken up by four footballs. At 0 °C and 100 kPa, that box holds one mole of gas. It does not matter which gas.

This follows from Avogadro’s law, which you met in Structure 1.4: equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules. Turn it around: equal numbers of molecules occupy equal volumes.

The molar volume, Vm, is the volume of one mole of an ideal gas. It is a constant at a specific temperature and pressure.

STP stands for standard temperature and pressure: 273.15 K (0 °C) and 100 kPa. At STP the molar volume of an ideal gas is 22.7 dm3 mol−1.

n = VVm

The data booklet gives the molar volume of an ideal gas at STP as 22.7 dm3 mol−1 (also written 2.27 × 10−2 m3 mol−1), with STP defined as 273.15 K and 100 kPa. The relationship n = V ÷ Vm is not printed, so learn it.

Older books and many websites use 22.4 dm3 mol−1. That value belongs to an older definition of standard pressure. In IB Chemistry the value is 22.7. Using 22.4 gives a wrong answer.

Method in words

  1. Check the conditions are STP. If they are not, use PV = nRT instead (later on this page).
  2. Put the volume in dm3. To convert from cm3, divide by 1000.
  3. To find amount: n = V ÷ 22.7. To find volume: V = n × 22.7.

Amount from a gas volume at STP

A sample of nitrogen gas occupies 568 cm3 at STP. Calculate the amount of nitrogen, in mol.

  1. The molar volume is in dm3 mol−1, so the volume must be in dm3 too. There are 1000 cm3 in 1 dm3.

    V = 568 cm3 ÷ 1000 = 0.568 dm3

  2. The gas is at STP, so one mole occupies 22.7 dm3.

    n = V ÷ Vm

  3. dm3 divided by dm3 mol−1 leaves mol. The units confirm the method.

    n = 0.568 dm3 ÷ 22.7 dm3 mol−1 = 0.025022… mol

  4. 568 cm3 and 22.7 both have 3 significant figures.

    n = 0.0250 mol

0.0250 mol of N2

Calculate the volume, in dm3, occupied by 0.0420 mol of carbon dioxide at STP.

  1. At STP, every mole of an ideal gas occupies the same volume. Which data booklet value is that?
  2. V = n × Vm = 0.0420 mol × 22.7 dm3 mol−1.
V = 0.0420 mol × 22.7 dm3 mol−1 = 0.9534 dm3

To 3 significant figures: 0.953 dm3.

A balloon contains 1250 cm3 of helium at STP. Calculate the amount of helium, in mol.

  1. Convert the volume first: 1250 cm3 is how many dm3?
  2. n = V ÷ Vm = 1.25 dm3 ÷ 22.7 dm3 mol−1.
V = 1250 cm3 ÷ 1000 = 1.25 dm3
n = 1.25 dm3 ÷ 22.7 dm3 mol−1 = 0.055066… mol

To 3 significant figures: 0.0551 mol.

22.7 dm3 mol−1 works only at STP. If a question gives any other temperature or pressure, such as 25 °C, the molar volume is different and you need PV = nRT.

Structure 1.5.3

How pressure, volume and temperature are related

Push the plunger of a sealed syringe and it pushes back harder. Leave a football outside on a cold night and it goes soft. These are the relationships in this section.

A gas sample is described by four variables: pressure P, volume V, temperature T and amount n. To see how two of them are related, you hold the others constant. A fixed mass of gas means n is constant.

Pressure and volume (temperature constant)

Squeeze a gas into half the volume and the particles hit the walls twice as often. The pressure doubles.

Pressure is inversely proportional to volume: P ∝ 1/V, so PV = constant. A graph of P against V is a curve. A graph of P against 1/V is a straight line through the origin.

Volume and temperature (pressure constant)

Heat a gas in a container with a freely moving piston. The particles move faster and push the piston out until the pressure inside matches the pressure outside again. The volume increases.

Volume is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvin: V ∝ T, so V/T = constant. A graph of V against T in kelvin is a straight line through the origin.

Plot the same data against temperature in °C and you still get a straight line, but it does not pass through the origin. Extend the line backwards (extrapolate) and it reaches zero volume at −273.15 °C. That temperature is absolute zero, 0 K.

Pressure and temperature (volume constant)

Heat a gas in a rigid container. The particles move faster, so they hit the walls harder and more often. The pressure increases.

Pressure is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvin: P ∝ T, so P/T = constant. A graph of P against T in kelvin is a straight line through the origin.

Three relationships for a fixed mass of an ideal gas
Held constantRelationshipWhat stays the sameGraph
TP ∝ 1/V (inverse)P1V1 = P2V2P against V: curve. P against 1/V: line through origin.
PV ∝ T (direct, T in K)V1/T1 = V2/T2V against T/K: line through origin.
VP ∝ T (direct, T in K)P1/T1 = P2/T2P against T/K: line through origin.

The direct proportions work only in kelvin. Heating a gas from 20 °C to 40 °C does not double its volume or pressure. In kelvin that is 293 K to 313 K, an increase of about 7 %.

When you sketch a graph, label both axes with the quantity and its unit, and make it clear whether a straight line passes through the origin. For V against T in °C, show the line meeting the temperature axis at −273 °C, usually as a dashed extrapolation.

These relationships have historical names (Boyle’s law, Charles’s law, Gay-Lussac’s law). The IB will not assess the names. You need the relationships and their graphs, not who discovered them.

Structure 1.5.4

The combined gas law

A weather balloon rises. The air outside gets colder and the pressure drops, both at once. To find the new volume you need one equation that handles pressure, volume and temperature together.

Putting the three relationships together gives the combined gas law. It links two sets of conditions for the same sample of gas: state 1 (before) and state 2 (after).

P1V1T1 = P2V2T2
P
pressure, same unit on both sides kPa
V
volume, same unit on both sides dm3
T
temperature, always in kelvin K
1, 2
before and after the change, for a fixed amount of gas

The combined gas law is printed in the data booklet. You do not need to memorise it, but you do need to rearrange it.

Method in words

  1. List P1, V1, T1 and P2, V2, T2. Mark the unknown with a question mark.
  2. Convert both temperatures to kelvin: add 273.15.
  3. Check that the two pressures share a unit and the two volumes share a unit. They do not have to be Pa or m3, because the units cancel.
  4. If a variable is constant, it cancels. Cross it out on both sides.
  5. Rearrange for the unknown, substitute, and round at the end.
  6. Sense check: did the answer move in the direction you expected?

New volume when pressure and temperature both change

A gas syringe contains 85.0 cm3 of gas at 22.0 °C and 101 kPa. Calculate the volume of the gas, in cm3, at 65.0 °C and 125 kPa.

  1. Sorting the data first stops you mixing up “before” and “after”.

    P1 = 101 kPa   V1 = 85.0 cm3   T1 = 22.0 °C
    P2 = 125 kPa   V2 = ?   T2 = 65.0 °C

  2. The gas relationships are proportional only on the kelvin scale.

    T1 = 22.0 + 273.15 = 295.15 K
    T2 = 65.0 + 273.15 = 338.15 K

  3. Multiply both sides by T2 and divide by P2 to leave V2 alone.

    V2 = (P1 × V1 × T2) ÷ (T1 × P2)

  4. kPa cancels with kPa and K with K, leaving cm3. No conversion to dm3 is needed here.

    V2 = (101 kPa × 85.0 cm3 × 338.15 K) ÷ (295.15 K × 125 kPa)
    V2 = 78.685… cm3

  5. Heating makes the gas expand, but the larger pressure squeezes it more. A slightly smaller volume is reasonable. The data have 3 significant figures.

    V2 = 78.7 cm3

78.7 cm3

A gas occupies 2.40 dm3 at 15.0 °C. It is heated to 80.0 °C at constant pressure. Calculate the new volume, in dm3.

  1. The pressure is constant, so P cancels. You are left with V1/T1 = V2/T2. What must you do to the temperatures first?
  2. T1 = 288.15 K and T2 = 353.15 K. Then V2 = V1 × T2 ÷ T1.
T1 = 15.0 + 273.15 = 288.15 K;   T2 = 80.0 + 273.15 = 353.15 K
V2 = 2.40 dm3 × 353.15 K ÷ 288.15 K = 2.9413… dm3

To 3 significant figures: 2.94 dm3. If you got 12.8 dm3, you used °C.

A sealed, rigid steel container holds gas at 310 kPa and 20.0 °C. It is left in the sun and warms to 55.0 °C. Calculate the new pressure, in kPa.

  1. Rigid means the volume is constant, so V cancels: P1/T1 = P2/T2.
  2. P2 = P1 × T2 ÷ T1 = 310 kPa × 328.15 K ÷ 293.15 K.
T1 = 20.0 + 273.15 = 293.15 K;   T2 = 55.0 + 273.15 = 328.15 K
P2 = 310 kPa × 328.15 K ÷ 293.15 K = 347.01… kPa

To 3 significant figures: 347 kPa.

The combined gas law is only for a fixed amount of gas. If gas is added or escapes between the two states, n has changed and you need PV = nRT for each state.

Finding a volume with kPa and dm³

Calculate the volume, in dm3, occupied by 0.150 mol of carbon dioxide at 25.0 °C and 98.5 kPa.

  1. The pressure is already in kPa, so the answer will come out in dm3. Only the temperature needs converting.

    n = 0.150 mol   P = 98.5 kPa   V = ?
    T = 25.0 + 273.15 = 298.15 K

  2. Divide both sides of PV = nRT by P.

    V = nRT ÷ P

  3. mol × J K−1 mol−1 × K leaves J. Then J ÷ kPa gives dm3.

    V = (0.150 mol × 8.31 J K−1 mol−1 × 298.15 K) ÷ 98.5 kPa
    V = 371.64… J ÷ 98.5 kPa = 3.7730… dm3

  4. One mole at STP is 22.7 dm3, so 0.150 mol near room conditions should be a few dm3. The data have 3 significant figures.

    V = 3.77 dm3

3.77 dm3

Finding an amount, then a mass, with Pa and m³

A 2.50 dm3 flask contains oxygen gas, O2, at 27.0 °C and 150 kPa. Calculate the mass of oxygen in the flask.

  1. This example uses the SI base set to show that it gives the same kind of answer. You could also keep kPa and dm3.

    P = 150 kPa × 1000 = 1.50 × 105 Pa
    V = 2.50 dm3 ÷ 1000 = 2.50 × 10−3 m3
    T = 27.0 + 273.15 = 300.15 K

  2. You need the amount before you can find a mass. Divide both sides by RT.

    n = PV ÷ RT

  3. Pa × m3 = J on top. J K−1 mol−1 × K = J mol−1 underneath. J ÷ J mol−1 leaves mol.

    n = (1.50 × 105 Pa × 2.50 × 10−3 m3) ÷ (8.31 J K−1 mol−1 × 300.15 K)
    n = 375 J ÷ 2494.2… J mol−1 = 0.15034… mol

  4. Oxygen gas is O2, so M = 2 × 16.00 = 32.00 g mol−1. Keep the unrounded value of n in your calculator.

    m = n × M = 0.15034… mol × 32.00 g mol−1 = 4.8110… g

  5. The data have 3 significant figures.

    m = 4.81 g

4.81 g of O2

Calculate the pressure, in kPa, of 0.250 mol of gas in a 5.00 dm3 container at 30.0 °C.

  1. The volume is in dm3, so the pressure will come out in kPa. Convert the temperature to kelvin.
  2. P = nRT ÷ V = (0.250 × 8.31 × 303.15) ÷ 5.00.
T = 30.0 + 273.15 = 303.15 K
P = (0.250 mol × 8.31 J K−1 mol−1 × 303.15 K) ÷ 5.00 dm3 = 125.95… kPa

To 3 significant figures: 126 kPa.

0.100 mol of gas occupies 2.00 dm3 at a pressure of 150 kPa. Calculate the temperature of the gas, in K.

  1. kPa and dm3 already match, so no conversion is needed. Rearrange PV = nRT for T.
  2. T = PV ÷ nR = (150 × 2.00) ÷ (0.100 × 8.31).
T = (150 kPa × 2.00 dm3) ÷ (0.100 mol × 8.31 J K−1 mol−1) = 361.01… K

To 3 significant figures: 361 K. The equation always gives temperature in kelvin.

A gas syringe holds 250 cm3 of methane, CH4, at 18.0 °C and 102 kPa. Calculate the mass of methane, in g.

  1. Two conversions are needed before you start: cm3 to dm3 and °C to K.
  2. n = PV ÷ RT = (102 × 0.250) ÷ (8.31 × 291.15). Then m = n × M, with M(CH4) = 16.05 g mol−1.
V = 250 cm3 ÷ 1000 = 0.250 dm3;   T = 18.0 + 273.15 = 291.15 K
n = (102 kPa × 0.250 dm3) ÷ (8.31 J K−1 mol−1 × 291.15 K) = 0.010539… mol
M(CH4) = 12.01 + 4(1.01) = 16.05 g mol−1
m = 0.010539… mol × 16.05 g mol−1 = 0.16915… g

To 3 significant figures: 0.169 g.

The two classic errors are leaving the temperature in °C, and putting cm3 straight in with kPa. The first gives an answer that is wildly out. The second is out by exactly 1000. If your amount of gas in a small flask comes out as several moles, check your volume unit.

In a 2-mark gas equation question, one mark is usually for correct unit conversions (kelvin, and matching pressure and volume units) and one for the answer. Write the conversions as separate lines so the first mark is safe even if your final answer slips.

IB uses SI units only for pressure and volume, so you will not meet atm, mmHg, bar or litres in gas questions. Partial pressures (Dalton’s law), Graham’s law of effusion and mole fractions are not in the guide at all. If a textbook chapter turns to those, you can stop.

Structure 1.5.4

Molar mass of a gas from experimental data

You have an unknown liquid that evaporates easily. Inject a weighed drop into a warm gas syringe and it turns to vapour. Read the volume, the temperature and the pressure, and you can work out its molar mass.

The idea has two links. The gas equation gives the amount, n. Then molar mass is mass divided by amount.

M = mn = mRTPV

The combined form M = mRT ÷ PV is not in the data booklet. You do not need to memorise it. Working in two steps (find n, then M = m ÷ n) is safer and shows your method.

Method in words

  1. Convert T to K, and P and V to a matching pair (kPa with dm3).
  2. Find the amount of gas: n = PV ÷ RT.
  3. Find the molar mass: M = m ÷ n, with the mass in g.
  4. If you also know the empirical formula, divide M by the empirical formula mass to find how many empirical units make one molecule.

Molar mass of a volatile liquid

0.180 g of a volatile liquid is injected into a gas syringe kept at 100.0 °C. It vaporises completely and the vapour occupies 95.1 cm3 at 101 kPa. Calculate the molar mass of the liquid.

  1. With the pressure in kPa, the volume must be in dm3. The temperature must be in kelvin.

    V = 95.1 cm3 ÷ 1000 = 0.0951 dm3
    T = 100.0 + 273.15 = 373.15 K

  2. The gas equation counts the moles of vapour without needing to know what the substance is.

    n = PV ÷ RT = (101 kPa × 0.0951 dm3) ÷ (8.31 J K−1 mol−1 × 373.15 K)
    n = 9.6051 J ÷ 3100.8… J mol−1 = 0.0030975… mol

  3. Molar mass is the mass of one mole, so it is grams divided by moles.

    M = m ÷ n = 0.180 g ÷ 0.0030975… mol = 58.11… g mol−1

  4. The mass, volume and pressure have 3 significant figures.

    M = 58.1 g mol−1

58.1 g mol−1

From percentage composition and gas data to a molecular formula

A hydrocarbon contains 85.6 % carbon and 14.4 % hydrogen by mass. A 0.500 g sample of the gas occupies 221 cm3 at 25.0 °C and 100 kPa. Determine the molecular formula of the hydrocarbon.

  1. This is the Structure 1.4 method: imagine 100 g, divide each mass by Ar, then divide by the smallest.

    C: 85.6 g ÷ 12.01 g mol−1 = 7.127… mol
    H: 14.4 g ÷ 1.01 g mol−1 = 14.25… mol
    ratio H : C = 14.25… ÷ 7.127… = 2.00, so the empirical formula is CH2

  2. kPa needs dm3, and the temperature must be in kelvin.

    V = 221 cm3 ÷ 1000 = 0.221 dm3;   T = 25.0 + 273.15 = 298.15 K

  3. The conditions are not STP, so use the gas equation, not 22.7.

    n = (100 kPa × 0.221 dm3) ÷ (8.31 J K−1 mol−1 × 298.15 K) = 0.0089198… mol

  4. Mass divided by amount.

    M = 0.500 g ÷ 0.0089198… mol = 56.05… g mol−1

  5. The molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula. Experimental data rarely give an exact whole number, so round to the nearest one.

    M(CH2) = 12.01 + 2(1.01) = 14.03 g mol−1
    56.05… ÷ 14.03 = 3.995… ≈ 4
    molecular formula = (CH2)4 = C4H8

C4H8

1.26 g of a gas occupies 0.750 dm3 at 20.0 °C and 96.0 kPa. Calculate the molar mass of the gas.

  1. Find the amount of gas first with n = PV ÷ RT. Remember kelvin.
  2. n = (96.0 × 0.750) ÷ (8.31 × 293.15). Then M = 1.26 g ÷ n.
T = 20.0 + 273.15 = 293.15 K
n = (96.0 kPa × 0.750 dm3) ÷ (8.31 J K−1 mol−1 × 293.15 K) = 0.029555… mol
M = 1.26 g ÷ 0.029555… mol = 42.63… g mol−1

To 3 significant figures: 42.6 g mol−1.

Gas density uses the same idea

Density is mass divided by volume. If a question gives the density of a gas, it has given you the mass of a known volume. For example, a density of 1.25 g dm−3 means 1.25 g occupies 1.00 dm3. Put those two values into the method above. There is no new formula to learn.

These questions are often chained: empirical formula in one part, molar mass from gas data in the next, molecular formula at the end. Each part is marked with “error carried forward”. If an earlier answer is wrong but you use it correctly afterwards, you still earn the later marks. So keep going, and show every line.

Do not use 22.7 dm3 mol−1 to find the amount unless the gas is at STP. Vapour in a syringe at 100 °C takes up much more space per mole than that.

Linking it up

The IB guide asks linking questions that connect this topic to others. Short answers to four of them:

Avogadro’s law applies to ideal gases. Under what conditions might a real gas deviate most?

At low temperature and high pressure. There the particles are slow and close together, so intermolecular forces and the volume of the particles are no longer negligible. Under those conditions, equal volumes of two real gases may not contain exactly equal numbers of molecules.

Under comparable conditions, why do some gases deviate more than others?

Gases whose particles attract each other more strongly deviate more, because the “no intermolecular forces” assumption fits them less well. Larger particles also make the “negligible volume” assumption less accurate. Structure 2.2 explains why some molecules attract more strongly than others.

Graphs can be sketches or accurately plotted points. What are the advantages and limitations of each?

A sketch shows the shape of a relationship quickly: curve or line, through the origin or not. It carries no scale, so you cannot read values from it. Plotted data points let you find a gradient or an intercept, judge the scatter and spot anomalous results, and extrapolate (for example to absolute zero). They take longer, and they are only as reliable as the measurements and the range that was measured.

How can the ideal gas law be used to find the molar mass of a gas from experimental data?

Measure the mass, pressure, volume and temperature of a gas sample. Use n = PV ÷ RT to find the amount, then M = m ÷ n. The result assumes the gas behaves ideally, so it is most accurate at high temperature and low pressure.
