Structure 1.2.1

The nuclear model of the atom

Picture a sports stadium with one pea on the centre spot. That is roughly how an atom is laid out. The pea is the nucleus. The rest of the stadium is where the electrons are. Almost all of it is empty space.

The nucleus is tiny, dense and positively charged. It contains protons and neutrons. Together, protons and neutrons are called nucleons. The negatively charged electrons occupy the space outside the nucleus.

Nearly all the mass of an atom is in its nucleus. Nearly all the volume of an atom is the space where its electrons are.

The three subatomic particles

The real masses and charges are awkward numbers, so chemists compare the particles with each other. These relative values are the ones to learn.

Relative masses and charges of the subatomic particles
ParticleWhere it isRelative massRelative charge
protonin the nucleus1+1
neutronin the nucleus10
electronoutside the nucleusnegligible−1

“Negligible” means small enough to ignore. A proton is about 1800 times heavier than an electron, so the electrons add almost nothing to the mass of an atom.

The actual masses (in kg) of the proton, neutron and electron, and the elementary charge (in C), are in the data booklet. You do not need to remember them. You do need to know the relative values in the table.

A proton and an electron have charges of the same size but opposite sign. A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so the charges cancel.

Using the table: charged plates

You can be asked to apply these values. Suppose beams of protons, neutrons and electrons, all moving at the same speed, pass between a positive plate and a negative plate.

  • Protons are positive, so they bend towards the negative plate.
  • Electrons are negative, so they bend towards the positive plate. They bend much more than protons, because the charge is the same size but the mass is far smaller.
  • Neutrons have no charge, so they go straight on.

The syllabus does not ask you to recall the history of atomic models or the details of the experiments that led to the nuclear model. Quarks, and the forces that hold the nucleus together, are not part of IB Chemistry.

Structure 1.2.1

Nuclear symbols: counting protons, neutrons and electrons

Every atom or ion can be described by three numbers. The nuclear symbol packs them around the symbol of the element.

The atomic number, Z, is the number of protons. It decides which element the atom is. Every atom with 17 protons is chlorine.

The mass number, A, is the number of protons plus neutrons. It is always a whole number, because it is a count.

Method in words

  1. Protons = Z, the bottom number. If it is not given, look up the element in the periodic table.
  2. Neutrons = A − Z, top number minus bottom number.
  3. Electrons: start with the number of protons. For a positive ion, subtract the charge. For a negative ion, add the size of the charge.
neutrons = A − Z     electrons = Z − q
A
mass number (no unit)
Z
atomic number (no unit)
q
charge on the species, with its sign: +2 for a 2+ ion, −1 for a 1− ion, 0 for an atom

An ion is an atom that has gained or lost electrons. A cation is positive: it has lost electrons. An anion is negative: it has gained electrons. The nucleus does not change.

A positive charge does not mean extra protons, and it does not mean extra electrons. Mg^2+ has the same 12 protons as a magnesium atom and two fewer electrons: 12 − 2 = 10.

Particles in an ion

Deduce the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in the ion 2713Al3+.

  1. The bottom number is the atomic number, and the atomic number is the number of protons.

    protons = Z = 13

  2. The top number counts protons and neutrons together, so take the protons away.

    neutrons = A − Z = 27 − 13 = 14

  3. A 3+ charge means the atom has lost 3 electrons. A neutral aluminium atom would have 13.

    electrons = Z − q = 13 − 3 = 10

  4. Protons minus electrons must give the charge on the symbol.

    13 − 10 = +3, which matches 3+
    13 protons, 14 neutrons, 10 electrons

13 protons, 14 neutrons, 10 electrons

How many neutrons are in the ion 5626Fe3+?

  1. The charge is about electrons. It has no effect on the nucleus.
  2. Neutrons = mass number − atomic number = 56 − 26.
neutrons = A − Z = 56 − 26 = 30

How many electrons are in the sulfide ion 3216S2−?

  1. A negative ion has gained electrons. Will the answer be more or less than 16?
  2. Electrons = protons + size of the negative charge = 16 + 2.
electrons = Z − q = 16 − (−2) = 16 + 2 = 18

When you write a nuclear symbol, the mass number goes top left and the atomic number bottom left. For an ion, write the number before the sign: 2+, not +2. A missing or wrong charge loses the mark.

Structure 1.2.2

Isotopes

Take a jar of chlorine gas. Every atom in it has 17 protons. But about three quarters of the atoms have 18 neutrons, and about one quarter have 20. They are all chlorine, with two different masses.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. So isotopes have the same atomic number and different mass numbers.

You name an isotope by its mass number: chlorine-35 and chlorine-37, or 35Cl and 37Cl.

The two isotopes of chlorine found in nature
IsotopeProtonsNeutronsElectrons (in the atom)Mass number
chlorine-3517181735
chlorine-3717201737

Same chemistry, different physical properties

Chemical properties are the same. How an atom reacts depends on its electrons. Isotopes of an element have the same number of electrons, arranged in the same way, so they react in the same way.

Physical properties are different. Isotopes have different masses. Any property that depends on mass is a little different: density, the rate at which a gas diffuses, and melting and boiling points.

For example, “heavy water” made with hydrogen-2 reacts like ordinary water, but it is denser and boils at a slightly higher temperature.

To explain the same chemical properties, write “same number of electrons” or “same electron configuration”. To explain a different physical property, write “different mass” and name the property, such as density.

Isotopes do not differ in protons or electrons. If the number of protons changes, it is a different element. If the number of electrons changes, it is an ion. Only a change in neutrons gives an isotope.

You do not need to learn specific examples of isotopes or their uses. The data will be given in the question. Radioactive decay, half-life and nuclear equations are not part of this topic.

Structure 1.2.2

Relative atomic mass from isotope data

Look up chlorine in the periodic table and you find 35.45. No chlorine atom has that mass. Each one is either chlorine-35 or chlorine-37. The number 35.45 is an average for the mixture found in nature.

The relative atomic mass, Ar, is the weighted average mass of the atoms of an element, compared with one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. It has no units.

What “weighted” means

Suppose 3 of every 4 chlorine atoms have mass 35, and 1 of every 4 has mass 37. The average of four typical atoms is (35 + 35 + 35 + 37) ÷ 4 = 35.5. The answer is closer to 35 because there are more atoms of mass 35. Each isotope counts in proportion to its abundance.

The abundance of an isotope is the percentage of the atoms that are that isotope.

Ar = (m1 × a1) + (m2 × a2) + …100

Method in words: abundances to Ar

  1. Check that the abundances add up to 100%.
  2. Multiply the mass of each isotope by its percentage abundance.
  3. Add the results.
  4. Divide by 100.
  5. Round at the end, usually to two decimal places, and give no units.

Try “Mass number only” in the mixer. The answer changes slightly. The real mass of an isotope is very close to its mass number, but not exactly equal to it. Use whichever masses the question gives you. If it gives only mass numbers, use those.

Relative atomic mass from percentage abundances

A sample of silicon contains 92.2% 28Si, 4.7% 29Si and 3.1% 30Si. Calculate the relative atomic mass of silicon in this sample, to two decimal places.

  1. If they do not add up to 100%, an isotope is missing or you have misread the data.

    92.2 + 4.7 + 3.1 = 100.0%

  2. This is the weighting. A common isotope contributes more to the total.

    28 × 92.2 = 2581.6
    29 × 4.7 = 136.3
    30 × 3.1 = 93.0

  3. The abundances are percentages, so the total is for 100 atoms. Dividing by 100 gives the average for one atom.

    2581.6 + 136.3 + 93.0 = 2810.9
    Ar = 2810.9 / 100 = 28.109

  4. The answer must lie between 28 and 30, and close to 28 because silicon-28 is by far the most abundant.

    Ar = 28.11 (2 d.p., no units)

28.11. This is not quite the data booklet value of 28.09. The question asked for the value from this data, so 28.11 is the answer that scores.

If a question says “calculate” and gives isotope data, your answer must come from that data. Markschemes do not accept the periodic table value copied from the data booklet. Show the weighted-sum line of working, then the answer.

A sample of neon contains 90.48% 20Ne, 0.27% 21Ne and 9.25% 22Ne. Calculate the relative atomic mass of neon in this sample, to two decimal places.

  1. Multiply each mass number by its percentage. You will have three products to add.
  2. (20 × 90.48 + 21 × 0.27 + 22 × 9.25) ÷ 100
20 × 90.48 = 1809.6
21 × 0.27 = 5.67
22 × 9.25 = 203.5
Ar = (1809.6 + 5.67 + 203.5) / 100 = 2018.77 / 100 = 20.1877

To two decimal places: 20.19. (The data booklet gives 20.18. Your calculated value is the one to write.)

Do not add the masses and divide by the number of isotopes. That simple average treats every isotope as equally common. For chlorine it gives 36, but the real mixture is mostly chlorine-35, so the weighted average is about 35.5.

Working backwards: abundance from Ar

Sometimes you are given the relative atomic mass and asked for the abundances. With two isotopes there is one unknown, so one equation is enough. The key is that the two percentages must add up to 100.

Method in words

  1. Call the percentage of one isotope x. The other is then 100 − x.
  2. Write the weighted-mean equation with x in it, and set it equal to Ar.
  3. Multiply both sides by 100 to remove the fraction.
  4. Expand the bracket, collect the x terms, and solve.
  5. Check: the more abundant isotope must be the one whose mass is closer to Ar.

Percentage abundance from relative atomic mass

Boron has two isotopes, 10B and 11B. Its relative atomic mass is 10.81. Calculate the percentage abundance of each isotope. Use the mass numbers as the masses of the isotopes.

  1. There are two percentages, but they add up to 100, so one letter covers both.

    % of 10B = x
    % of 11B = 100 − x

  2. It is the same formula as before, with x and (100 − x) in place of the abundances.

    10x + 11(100 − x)100 = 10.81

  3. This removes the fraction, so the next lines are easier.

    10x + 11(100 − x) = 1081

  4. Multiply 11 by each term inside the bracket. Watch the minus sign.

    10x + 1100 − 11x = 1081

  5. 10x − 11x is −x. Then move the numbers to one side.

    1100 − x = 1081
    x = 1100 − 1081 = 19

  6. 10.81 is closer to 11 than to 10, so 11B should be the more abundant isotope. It is.

    10B = 19%, 11B = 100 − 19 = 81%
    (10 × 19 + 11 × 81) / 100 = 1081 / 100 = 10.81

10B 19% and 11B 81%

Copper has two isotopes. 63Cu has a relative mass of 62.93 and 65Cu has a relative mass of 64.93. The relative atomic mass of copper is 63.55. Calculate the percentage abundance of 63Cu.

  1. Let the percentage of 63Cu be x. Then 65Cu is 100 − x. Use the masses given, not the mass numbers.
  2. 62.93x + 64.93(100 − x) = 6355. Expand the bracket: 64.93 × 100 = 6493.
62.93x + 64.93(100 − x) = 63.55 × 100 = 6355
62.93x + 6493 − 64.93x = 6355
6493 − 2.00x = 6355
2.00x = 6493 − 6355 = 138
x = 138 / 2.00 = 69.0%

63Cu is 69.0% (so 65Cu is 31.0%). Check: 63.55 is closer to 62.93, so 63Cu should be more abundant.

Mass number belongs to one isotope and is a whole number (35). Relative atomic mass belongs to the element as a mixture of isotopes, and is usually not a whole number (35.45). Neither has units. Molar mass, with units g mol-1, comes in Structure 1.4.

Structure 1.2.3

Mass spectra

Where do isotope abundances come from? Imagine tipping a bag of mixed coins into a sorting machine. It sorts the coins by mass and counts how many there are of each kind. A mass spectrometer does the same job for atoms.

The result is a mass spectrum: a bar chart with one peak for each isotope.

  • The horizontal axis is m/z, the mass-to-charge ratio of the ion detected. For an ion with a 1+ charge, m/z is equal to the mass of the isotope. So the position of a peak tells you which isotope it is.
  • The vertical axis is relative abundance. The height of a peak tells you how much of that isotope is present.

For the atoms of an element: number of peaks = number of isotopes. Position of a peak = mass of that isotope. Height of a peak = its relative abundance.

How a mass spectrometer works will not be assessed. You do not need the stages inside the machine (how the sample is ionized, accelerated, deflected or detected). You only need to read the spectrum.

When the peak heights do not add up to 100

Sometimes the vertical axis is a percentage. Then the heights add up to 100 and you use the method you already know.

Often the tallest peak is set to 100 and the others are scaled against it. Then the heights do not add up to 100. The fix is small: divide by the total of the peak heights, not by 100.

Method in words

  1. List each peak: its m/z and its height. Read heights as carefully as the scale allows.
  2. Add up the heights. If the total is 100, they are percentages.
  3. Multiply each m/z by its height, and add the results.
  4. Divide by the total of the heights.
  5. Round at the end. Check that the answer is between the lightest and heaviest isotope, nearest the tallest peak.

The particles detected in a mass spectrometer are positive ions. If you are asked which species causes a peak, include the charge: 35Cl+, not 35Cl. Without the + the mark is not given.

Elements that exist as molecules

Chlorine gas is made of Cl2 molecules. Each molecule contains two atoms, and each atom can be either isotope. So the spectrum of the Cl2+ ions has three peaks, not two.

Peaks for Cl2+, where 35Cl : 37Cl is about 3 : 1

The middle peak has a factor of 2 because the mixed molecule can form in two ways: 35Cl–37Cl or 37Cl–35Cl. It is like tossing two coins: one head and one tail is twice as likely as two heads.

Bromine has two isotopes, 79Br and 81Br, in almost equal amounts. The same reasoning gives peaks for Br2+ at m/z 158, 160 and 162 with heights in the ratio 1 : 2 : 1.

Some molecules break into single atoms inside the instrument. So the full spectrum of chlorine also has two peaks from 35Cl+ and 37Cl+ ions, at m/z 35 and 37, with heights in the ratio 3 : 1. The full spectrum of bromine also has peaks at m/z 79 and 81, with almost equal heights.

When a compound goes through a mass spectrometer its molecules break into pieces, giving a fragmentation pattern. Using that pattern to work out the structure of a compound is taught in Structure 3.2, not here.

Linking it up

The IB guide asks these linking questions. Short answers are enough at this stage.

What determines the different chemical properties of atoms?

The electrons. Chemical reactions involve electrons being lost, gained or shared, so the number of electrons and how they are arranged decide how an atom behaves. The nucleus matters because its number of protons fixes the number of electrons in the neutral atom. Neutrons make almost no difference, which is why isotopes react in the same way. Structure 1.3 is about how the electrons are arranged.

How does the atomic number relate to the position of an element in the periodic table?

The elements are listed in order of increasing atomic number. Hydrogen, with Z = 1, is first, helium with Z = 2 is second, and so on. Position is set by the number of protons, not by mass. You will use this in Structure 3.1.

How can isotope tracers provide evidence for a reaction mechanism?

Isotopes of an element react in the same way but have different masses. If one reactant is made with an unusual isotope, such as oxygen-18 in place of oxygen-16, that atom is “labelled”. Finding which product contains the heavier atom shows where that atom went during the reaction, which is evidence for which bonds break and form. Mechanisms themselves come later, in Reactivity 3.4.

How does the fragmentation pattern of a compound help to determine its structure?

This belongs to Structure 3.2. For now, the link is that the same instrument that separates the isotopes of an element can also separate the pieces of a broken molecule by mass.
