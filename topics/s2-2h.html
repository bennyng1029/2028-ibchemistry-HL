The covalent model: Additional HL

Guiding questionWhat determines the covalent nature and properties of a substance?

What you need from the core page

This page builds on the SL and HL core content of Structure 2.2. Check that you can do these five things first. If one is not secure, go back to 2.2 The covalent model.

  • Draw a Lewis formula for a molecule or ion with up to four electron pairs on each atom. Show every lone pair.
  • Say that single, double and triple bonds are one, two and three shared pairs. More shared pairs give a shorter, stronger bond.
  • Count electron domains. A lone pair, a single bond, a double bond and a triple bond each count as one domain.

The core page stops at four electron pairs and at one Lewis formula for each species. This page goes further in four directions: more than one Lewis formula (resonance and formal charge), more than four pairs (expanded octets), what a bond is made of (σ and π), and how atomic orbitals are prepared for bonding (hybridization).

Structure 2.2.11

Resonance structures and delocalization

Try to draw the Lewis formula of the carbonate ion, CO3^2-. Carbon needs one double bond to complete its octet. But which oxygen atom gets it? There are three equally good choices.

Resonance structures occur when there is more than one possible position for a double bond in a molecule or ion. The atoms stay in the same places. Only electrons are drawn in different positions.

None of the drawings is the real particle

A mule is a cross between a horse and a donkey. It is always a mule. It does not change into a horse and then into a donkey. Resonance is like this.

The real carbonate ion is one fixed structure, called a resonance hybrid. It is a mix of all the resonance structures at once. We need several drawings only because one Lewis formula cannot show it.

In the hybrid, one pair of electrons is not fixed between two atoms. It is spread over several atoms. This spreading is called delocalization. The electrons that spread out are the second pair of each double bond. They are called π (pi) electrons. You will meet π bonds properly in the section on sigma and pi bonds.

Delocalization makes the bonds equal. Each bond is between a single and a double bond in length and in strength.

How to deduce resonance structures

  1. Draw one correct Lewis formula. Count the valence electrons and include the charge of an ion.
  2. Look at the atom with the double bond. Is it also joined to another atom of the same kind by a single bond?
  3. If yes, redraw the formula with the double bond in that other position. Move a lone pair so that every atom keeps its octet.
  4. Repeat until you have used every possible position. Do not move any atoms.
  5. Write a double-headed arrow, ↔, between the structures.

Resonance structures of an ion

Deduce the resonance structures of the nitrite ion, NO2-. Nitrogen is the central atom. Then predict the lengths of the two nitrogen–oxygen bonds. The data booklet gives N–O 136 pm and N=O 114 pm.

  1. A Lewis formula must show every valence electron. A negative charge adds one electron.

    N: 5
    O: 2 × 6 = 12
    charge 1−: + 1
    Total = 5 + 12 + 1 = 18 electrons = 9 pairs

  2. Start with single bonds, complete the outer atoms, then put what is left on the central atom.

    2 bonds: 4 electrons. Two O atoms: 6 + 6 = 12 electrons. Left: 18 − 16 = 2 electrons on N.

    Nitrogen now has only 6 electrons around it: two bonds and one lone pair.

  3. Nitrogen needs one more shared pair for an octet. One oxygen shares a lone pair with it.

    The structure has one N=O bond, one N–O bond and one lone pair on nitrogen. All three atoms have an octet.

  4. The double bond could be to either oxygen atom. Both drawings are equally good, so there is resonance.

  5. The question asks for bond lengths. Use the idea of delocalization.

    The π electrons are delocalized over all three atoms. Both nitrogen–oxygen bonds are the same length. The length is between 114 pm and 136 pm.

Two resonance structures. Both N–O bonds are equal, with a length between 114 pm and 136 pm (measured: about 124 pm).

Wrong idea: “the ion flips quickly between the structures”. It does not. The real ion is always the hybrid, with equal bonds. The ↔ arrow does not mean a reaction or an equilibrium.

To explain equal bond lengths, write that the (π) electrons are delocalized, so the bonds are identical and intermediate between single and double. If you are asked for a Lewis formula only, one resonance structure is enough. A drawing with dashed bonds is not a Lewis formula.

Section 11 of the data booklet lists single and double bond lengths, such as C–O 143 pm and C=O 122 pm. A bond in a resonance hybrid has a length between the two values.

How many resonance structures can you draw for the nitrate ion, NO3-? Nitrogen is the central atom.

  1. One correct Lewis formula has one N=O bond and two N–O bonds.
  2. The double bond can go to each oxygen atom in turn.

There are three oxygen atoms, so the double bond has three possible positions: 3 resonance structures. In the real ion all three bonds are equal.

You do not need the term “bond order”, fractional bond orders such as 1⅓, or molecular orbital theory. You also do not need curly arrows to turn one resonance structure into another. Draw the structures and use the word delocalization.

Structure 2.2.12

Benzene: a molecule with resonance

Benzene, C6H6, is a ring of six carbon atoms. Each carbon is also bonded to one hydrogen atom. A Lewis formula needs three C=C double bonds in the ring to give every carbon four bonds.

The double bonds can be drawn in two positions, so benzene has two resonance structures. You can see them in the resonance flipper above. An early model, often called the Kekulé structure, treated benzene as one of these drawings: a ring with alternating single and double bonds.

The structure we use now

Each carbon atom forms three ordinary bonds: two to carbon and one to hydrogen. That uses three of its four valence electrons. The fourth electron of each carbon is a π electron.

The six π electrons are not fixed in three double bonds. They are delocalized over all six carbon atoms, in a ring above and below the plane of the molecule. Benzene is therefore drawn as a hexagon with a circle inside.

The result is a flat, regular hexagon. All six carbon–carbon bonds are identical. All bond angles are 120°.

The evidence

You need to discuss why we accept the delocalized structure. The evidence comes in two kinds. Physical evidence is measured on the molecule itself. Chemical evidence comes from reactions and their energy changes.

The last row needs numbers. Cyclohexene has one C=C bond in a six-carbon ring. Adding hydrogen to it releases about 120 kJ mol-1. Three such bonds should release about 360 kJ mol-1. Benzene releases only about 208 kJ mol-1.

The enthalpy of hydrogenation of cyclohexene is −120 kJ mol-1. The enthalpy of hydrogenation of benzene is −208 kJ mol-1. By how much is benzene more stable than a ring with three separate C=C bonds? Give a positive value.

  1. First predict the value for three separate C=C bonds.
  2. Predicted: 3 × (−120). Compare the size of this with 208.
Predicted = 3 × (−120) = −360 kJ mol−1
Difference = 360 − 208 = 152 kJ mol−1

Benzene releases less energy than predicted, so it started at a lower energy. It is more stable.

Describe benzene as having delocalized π electrons. The word π is needed for the mark. When a question asks for one physical and one chemical piece of evidence, give one of each. Two physical points score only once.

“Benzene is unsaturated” and “benzene can be hydrogenated” are true, but they are not evidence against alternating bonds. A ring with three C=C bonds would do the same. Choose evidence that the two models predict differently.

You do not need to calculate a “resonance energy” from bond enthalpies or draw orbital diagrams of the ring. How benzene reacts, step by step (electrophilic substitution), comes later in Reactivity 3.4.

Structure 2.2.13

Expanded octets: five and six electron domains

Phosphorus forms PCl3, as the octet rule predicts. It also forms PCl5. In PCl5 phosphorus has five bonding pairs: ten electrons in its valence shell.

An atom with more than eight electrons in its valence shell has an expanded octet. You need to deal with central atoms that have five or six electron domains.

Which atoms can do this?

Only atoms in period 3 and below can expand their octet. Examples are P, S, Cl, Br, I and Xe. Their valence shell is the third main energy level or higher. From Structure 1.3, a level with n = 3 can hold more than eight electrons. These atoms are also larger, so more atoms fit around them.

Period 2 atoms such as C, N, O and F never have more than eight valence electrons. Their valence shell (n = 2) is full with eight. So NCl5 does not exist, but PCl5 does.

Method: from formula to Lewis formula

This quick count works when each outer atom forms one single bond, such as F, Cl, Br, I or H.

  1. Write the number of valence electrons of the central atom. For groups 13 to 18 this is the group number minus 10.
  2. Add 1 electron for each outer atom.
  3. For an ion, add 1 for each negative charge. Subtract 1 for each positive charge.
  4. Divide by 2. This is the number of electron domains around the central atom.
  5. The number of bonding domains equals the number of outer atoms. The rest are lone pairs on the central atom.
  6. Draw the Lewis formula. Give each outer halogen atom three lone pairs. Add brackets and the charge for an ion.

Shapes for five and six domains

Electron domains repel each other and move as far apart as possible. Five domains point to the corners of a trigonal bipyramid. Six domains point to the corners of an octahedron.

A trigonal bipyramid has two kinds of position. Three equatorial positions lie in a flat triangle, 120° apart. Two axial positions lie above and below, at 90° to the triangle.

Lone pairs take equatorial positions. An equatorial lone pair has only two neighbours at 90°. An axial lone pair would have three. Fewer 90° neighbours means less repulsion.

In an octahedron all six positions are the same. When there are two lone pairs, they sit opposite each other, 180° apart.

Polarity of these molecules

Use the same two steps as on the core page. First, are the bonds polar? Second, do the bond dipoles cancel?

  • No lone pairs and identical outer atoms: the shape is symmetrical, the dipoles cancel, and the molecule is non-polar. Examples: PCl5, SF6.
  • Lone pairs usually spoil the symmetry, so the molecule is polar. Examples: SF4, ClF3, BrF5.
  • Two exceptions: linear XeF2 and square planar XeF4. Their lone pairs are arranged symmetrically, the bond dipoles cancel, and the molecules are non-polar.

Lewis formula, shape, angle and polarity: a molecule

For sulfur tetrafluoride, SF4: deduce the Lewis formula, the electron domain geometry, the molecular geometry, the bond angles and the polarity. (Electronegativity: S 2.6, F 4.0.)

  1. The number of electron pairs around the central atom decides the shape.

    S (group 16): 6 electrons; four F atoms: + 4; total = 10 electrons = 5 domains
    4 bonding domains, so 5 − 4 = 1 lone pair on S

  2. Every valence electron must be shown: 6 + (4 × 7) = 34 electrons = 17 pairs.

    Four bonds (4 pairs), three lone pairs on each F (12 pairs) and one lone pair on S (1 pair): 17 pairs. Sulfur has 10 electrons, an expanded octet.

  3. This describes all five domains, lone pair included.

    Five domains: trigonal bipyramidal.

  4. The molecular geometry describes the atoms only. The lone pair goes where repulsion is least.

    The lone pair is equatorial. The four fluorine atoms make a seesaw shape.

  5. A lone pair repels more than a bonding pair, so it pushes the bonds closer together.

    Ideal angles are 90° and 120°. In SF4 they are less than 90° and less than 120°.

  6. A molecule is polar when it has polar bonds whose dipoles do not cancel.

    Each S–F bond is polar (4.0 − 2.6 = 1.4). The seesaw shape is not symmetrical, so the dipoles do not cancel. SF4 is polar.

Trigonal bipyramidal electron domain geometry; seesaw molecular geometry; angles less than 90° and less than 120°; polar.

Lewis formula, shape and angle: an ion

For the ion ICl4-: deduce the Lewis formula, the electron domain geometry, the molecular geometry and the Cl–I–Cl bond angle.

  1. Remember the charge. A 1− charge means one extra electron.

    I (group 17): 7; four Cl atoms: + 4; charge 1−: + 1; total = 12 electrons = 6 domains
    4 bonding domains, so 6 − 4 = 2 lone pairs on I

  2. Check the total: 7 + (4 × 7) + 1 = 36 electrons = 18 pairs.

    Four bonds (4 pairs), three lone pairs on each Cl (12 pairs) and two lone pairs on I (2 pairs): 18 pairs. Brackets and the charge are shown.

  3. Six domains get as far apart as possible.

    Six domains: octahedral.

  4. Two lone pairs repel each other most, so they go opposite each other.

    The lone pairs are 180° apart. The four chlorine atoms lie in one plane: square planar.

  5. The two lone pairs push equally from above and below, so the angles in the plane are not changed.

    Neighbouring Cl–I–Cl angles are 90°.

Octahedral electron domain geometry; square planar molecular geometry; 90°.

How many lone pairs are on the xenon atom in XeF2?

  1. Xenon is in group 18. How many valence electrons does it have?
  2. Two electrons are used in the two Xe–F bonds. Pair up the rest.
8 − 2 = 6 electrons = 3 lone pairs

Two bonds and three lone pairs make five domains. The three lone pairs are equatorial, so the molecule is linear.

How many electron domains are around the bromine atom in BrF5?

  1. Bromine has 7 valence electrons. Add 1 for each fluorine atom.
  2. Divide the total by 2.
(7 + 5) ÷ 2 = 6 domains: 5 bonding and 1 lone pair

Octahedral electron domain geometry; square pyramidal molecular geometry.

Do not name the shape from the number of bonds alone. SF4 and XeF4 both have four bonds, but neither is tetrahedral. Always find the lone pairs on the central atom first.

Electron domain geometry and molecular geometry are separate marks. Read which one is asked. To explain a shape, state the number of bonding domains and lone pairs around the central atom.

The data booklet has no table of shapes or angles. You need to know the seven names and the ideal angles 90°, 120° and 180°.

You do not need seven electron domains (such as IF7), exact measured angles for the distorted shapes, or a hybridization label for a five- or six-domain atom. sp3d and sp3d2 are not in the IB course.

Structure 2.2.14

Formal charge

Sometimes you can draw two or more correct-looking Lewis formulas that are not resonance structures of equal value. Carbon dioxide can be drawn as O=C=O or as O≡C–O. Which one is better? Formal charge gives a way to decide.

Formal charge is a way of counting. Pretend that every bond is shared exactly equally. Then compare the electrons each atom “owns” in the drawing with the electrons it has as a free atom.

FC = V − N − ½B
FC
formal charge of the atom (no unit; write the sign)
V
number of valence electrons of the free atom
N
number of non-bonding electrons on the atom (2 for each lone pair)
B
number of bonding electrons around the atom (2 for each line)

Method: calculate, then choose

  1. Draw each possible Lewis formula with all its lone pairs.
  2. For each atom, find V from its group, count N (dots on that atom) and count B (2 for a single bond, 4 for a double bond, 6 for a triple bond).
  3. Calculate FC = V − N − ½B for every atom.
  4. Check: the formal charges must add up to the overall charge of the species.
  5. Prefer the formula whose formal charges are closest to zero.
  6. If two formulas are equal on that test, prefer the one with the negative formal charge on the more electronegative atom.

Formal charges and the preferred Lewis formula

Three Lewis formulas can be drawn for the thiocyanate ion, SCN-. Deduce which is preferred. (Electronegativity: N 3.0, S 2.6.)

  1. A has two double bonds. The nitrogen atom and the sulfur atom each have two lone pairs (4 non-bonding electrons, so N = 4 in the formula) and four bonding electrons.

    N: 5 − 4 − ½(4) = −1
    C: 4 − 0 − ½(8) = 0
    S: 6 − 4 − ½(4) = 0

  2. B has N≡C and C–S. N has one lone pair; S has three.

    N: 5 − 2 − ½(6) = 0
    C: 4 − 0 − ½(8) = 0
    S: 6 − 6 − ½(2) = −1

  3. C has N–C and C≡S. N has three lone pairs; S has one.

    N: 5 − 6 − ½(2) = −2
    C: 4 − 0 − ½(8) = 0
    S: 6 − 2 − ½(6) = +1

  4. Each set must add up to the charge on the ion, 1−.

    A: −1 + 0 + 0 = −1
    B: 0 + 0 − 1 = −1
    C: −2 + 0 + 1 = −1

  5. First find charges closest to zero. Then look at where the negative charge is.

    C has the largest charges, so reject it. A and B each have one −1. In A the −1 is on nitrogen, which is more electronegative than sulfur. A is preferred.

A is the preferred Lewis formula: formal charges −1 (N), 0 (C), 0 (S).

Sulfate: the classic case

Formula B has several resonance structures, because the two double bonds can go to any two of the four oxygen atoms. So all four sulfur–oxygen bonds in the real ion are equal.

Formal charge is not oxidation state. In the chlorate ion drawn with three single bonds, chlorine has formal charge +2 but oxidation state +5. Formal charge shares each bond equally. Oxidation state gives all the bonding electrons to the more electronegative atom.

For “which formula is preferred?”, show the formal charges and then write the reason: “formal charges closest to zero” or “negative formal charge on the more electronegative atom”. If a question only asks for a Lewis formula of a species such as SO2 or SO4^2-, both the octet version and the expanded-octet version are normally accepted.

In one Lewis formula of ozone, the central oxygen atom has one lone pair, one double bond and one single bond. Calculate the formal charge on the central oxygen atom.

  1. Oxygen is in group 16, so V = 6. One lone pair gives N = 2.
  2. A double bond and a single bond contain 4 + 2 = 6 bonding electrons.
FC = 6 − 2 − ½(6) = +1

The single-bonded oxygen has 6 − 6 − ½(2) = −1 and the double-bonded oxygen has 0. The total is 0, as it must be for a molecule.

In the Lewis formula of carbon monoxide, C≡O, each atom has one lone pair. Calculate the formal charge on the carbon atom.

  1. Carbon is in group 14, so V = 4. One lone pair gives N = 2.
  2. A triple bond contains 6 bonding electrons.
FC(C) = 4 − 2 − ½(6) = −1
FC(O) = 6 − 2 − ½(6) = +1

The total is 0, as it must be for a molecule. Here carbon is negative although oxygen is more electronegative. This formula is still the accepted one, because it is the only one that gives both atoms an octet.

Structure 2.2.15

Sigma (σ) and pi (π) bonds

So far a covalent bond has been a line or a pair of dots. Now look closer. A covalent bond forms when an atomic orbital of one atom overlaps with an atomic orbital of another atom. The shared electrons are in the region where the orbitals overlap.

Orbitals can overlap in two ways. The line through the two nuclei is called the bond axis.

  • A sigma bond (σ) forms by the head-on combination of atomic orbitals. The electron density is concentrated along the bond axis, between the two nuclei.
  • A pi bond (π) forms by the lateral (sideways) combination of p orbitals. The electron density is concentrated on opposite sides of the bond axis.

Single, double and triple bonds

Two atoms can make only one head-on overlap, so there is only one σ bond between any two atoms. Any further bonds between the same two atoms must be π bonds.

Sideways overlap is less effective than head-on overlap. So a π bond is weaker than a σ bond between the same atoms. This fits the data booklet: C=C (614 kJ mol-1) is stronger than C–C (346 kJ mol-1), but it is not twice as strong.

Method: counting σ and π bonds

  1. Draw the full structural formula. Show every bond, including every C–H bond.
  2. σ bonds: count one for every pair of atoms that are joined, whatever the bond is.
  3. π bonds: count one for each double bond and two for each triple bond.
  4. For a species with resonance, count using one resonance structure.

Counting σ and π bonds

Deduce the number of σ bonds and π bonds in one molecule of propenoic acid, CH2=CH–COOH.

  1. A condensed formula hides the C–H and O–H bonds. You cannot count what you cannot see.

  2. Every single bond is one σ bond.

    C–H: 3; C–C: 1; C–O: 1; O–H: 1; total 6 single bonds

  3. Each double bond is one σ bond and one π bond.

    C=C: 1; C=O: 1; total 2 double bonds

  4. σ comes from every bond. π comes only from the multiple bonds.

    σ = 6 + 2 = 8
    π = 2

  5. A molecule with no ring has one σ bond fewer than its number of atoms.

    C3H4O2 has 3 + 4 + 2 = 9 atoms; 9 − 1 = 8 σ bonds

8 σ bonds and 2 π bonds.

How many σ bonds are there in one molecule of propene, CH2=CH–CH3?

  1. Draw all six C–H bonds.
  2. Add the C–C single bond and the one σ bond inside C=C.
C–H: 6; C–C: 1; C=C: 1 σ; total = 8 σ bonds (and 1 π bond)

How many π bonds are there in one molecule of HC≡C–CH=CH2?

  1. Find the multiple bonds: one triple bond and one double bond.
  2. A triple bond has two π bonds. A double bond has one.
C≡C: 2 π; C=C: 1 π; total = 3 π bonds

A π bond is drawn with two regions, one above and one below the bond axis. Together they are one π bond holding two electrons. Do not count them as two bonds.

For σ write “head-on (end-to-end) overlap” and “electron density along the bond axis”. For π write “sideways (lateral) overlap of p orbitals” and “electron density above and below the bond axis”. When counting, the most common lost mark is forgetting the C–H bonds.

You do not need molecular orbital theory: no bonding and antibonding orbitals, no σ* or π*, and no energy-level diagrams for molecules. Describe bonds as the overlap of atomic orbitals.

Structure 2.2.16

Hybridization

Carbon has the configuration 1s2 2s2 2p2. Its valence orbitals are one 2s orbital and three 2p orbitals. They have different shapes and different energies. Yet in methane the four C–H bonds are identical and point to the corners of a tetrahedron.

The model that explains this is hybridization: the mixing of atomic orbitals to form new hybrid orbitals for bonding.

Think of mixing paint. One tin of red and three tins of white make four tins of the same pink. In the same way, one s orbital and three p orbitals make four identical hybrid orbitals. The number of orbitals does not change.

The three types of hybridization you need
Electron domainsOrbitals mixedHybridizationp orbitals left unmixedElectron domain geometryExamples

Count the electron domains around the atom. 4 domains → sp3. 3 domains → sp2. 2 domains → sp. Check: the name counts the orbitals that were mixed. sp3 is 1 + 3 = 4 orbitals, for 4 domains.

What the orbitals do

  • Hybrid orbitals point at the neighbouring atoms. They form σ bonds by head-on overlap, or they hold lone pairs.
  • Unmixed p orbitals stand at right angles to the hybrid orbitals. They form π bonds by sideways overlap.

Look at ethene, CH2=CH2. Each carbon has three domains, so it is sp2. Two sp2 orbitals overlap with hydrogen 1s orbitals to make C–H σ bonds. The third overlaps with an sp2 orbital of the other carbon to make the C–C σ bond. Each carbon has one unmixed p orbital. These two overlap sideways to make the π bond.

In ethyne, HC≡CH, each carbon has two domains, so it is sp. Each carbon has two unmixed p orbitals, which make the two π bonds of the triple bond.

Lone pairs count

Nitrogen in ammonia has three bonds and one lone pair. That is four domains, so nitrogen is sp3. The lone pair sits in one of the four sp3 orbitals. The electron domain geometry is tetrahedral, and the molecular geometry is trigonal pyramidal.

The same rule works for outer atoms. Each oxygen in O=C=O has one double bond and two lone pairs: three domains, so sp2.

Both directions

You must be able to go from structure to hybridization, and back.

  • Lewis formula → count domains → electron domain geometry → hybridization.
  • Hybridization → geometry. An sp2 atom has its bonds at about 120° in one plane. An sp atom has its bonds at 180°. An sp3 atom has its domains at about 109.5°.

This connects to the carbon allotropes on the core page. Each carbon in diamond has four single bonds and is sp3. Each carbon in graphite and graphene has three σ bonds and is sp2. The unmixed p orbitals overlap across the whole layer, which gives the delocalized electrons that conduct electricity.

Hybridization of each atom in a molecule

Deduce the hybridization of both carbon atoms and both oxygen atoms in ethanoic acid, CH3COOH.

  1. Count what is around the atom: bonded atoms plus lone pairs.

    Bonded to 3 H and 1 C, no lone pairs: 4 domains → sp3 (tetrahedral, 109.5°).

  2. A double bond is one domain, not two.

    Bonded to C, O (double bond) and O (single bond), no lone pairs: 3 domains → sp2 (trigonal planar, 120°).

  3. Lone pairs are domains too.

    1 bonded atom + 2 lone pairs: 3 domains → sp2.

  4. This oxygen is like the oxygen in water.

    2 bonded atoms + 2 lone pairs: 4 domains → sp3. The C–O–H angle is less than 109.5°.

CH3 carbon sp3; COOH carbon sp2; C=O oxygen sp2; O–H oxygen sp3.

How many of the three carbon atoms in propene, CH2=CH–CH3, are sp2 hybridized?

  1. sp2 means three electron domains.
  2. Count the atoms joined to each carbon. Which carbons are part of the double bond?

The two carbons of the C=C bond each have three domains: sp2. The CH3 carbon has four domains: sp3. Answer: 2.

Do not read hybridization from the number of bonds. Nitrogen in NH3 has three bonds but four domains (sp3). Carbon in CO2 has four bonds but two domains (sp). Count domains.

A typical question labels three atoms in a molecule and asks for the hybridization of each. Work on one atom at a time. Draw in any lone pairs and hidden hydrogen atoms before you count.

Only sp, sp2 and sp3 are needed. You do not need hybrid orbitals that use d orbitals, percentage “s character”, or drawings of hybrid orbital shapes with phases.

Structure 2.2.3 (HL part)

Coordination bonds in transition element complexes

From the core page: a coordination bond is a covalent bond in which both electrons of the shared pair come from the same atom. The ammonium ion and carbon monoxide are examples.

At HL you also need to find coordination bonds in complex ions of transition elements. A complex ion has a metal ion in the centre. Around it are molecules or ions called ligands. Each ligand has a lone pair, which it donates to the metal ion.

So every bond between a ligand and the central metal ion is a coordination bond. Both electrons come from the ligand.

How many coordination bonds are there in the complex ion [Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]^2+?

  1. Count the ligands joined to the copper ion.
  2. Each ammonia molecule and each water molecule donates one lone pair.
4 (from NH3) + 2 (from H2O) = 6 coordination bonds

The N–H and O–H bonds inside the ligands are ordinary covalent bonds.

For this topic you only identify the coordination bonds. Names of complex ions, their shapes, and terms such as “bidentate” are not in the IB course. The properties and colour of transition element complexes come later in Structure 3.1. Complex ions as Lewis acid–base products, and their charges, come in Reactivity 3.4.

One ion, five questions

The ethanoate ion, CH3COO-, forms when ethanoic acid loses H+.

(a) Draw the two resonance structures of the ion. (b) Explain why its two carbon–oxygen bonds have the same length. (c) Calculate the formal charge on each oxygen atom in one resonance structure. (d) Deduce the number of σ and π bonds in the ion. (e) Deduce the hybridization of each carbon atom and the O–C–O bond angle.

  1. The C=O double bond can be drawn to either oxygen atom. Nothing else moves.

  2. Use the key word. Say what is delocalized and over which atoms.

    The π electrons are delocalized over the carbon atom and both oxygen atoms. So the two carbon–oxygen bonds are identical, with a length between C–O (143 pm) and C=O (122 pm).

  3. Use FC = V − N − ½B for each oxygen in the first structure.

    O in C=O: 6 − 4 − ½(4) = 0
    O in C–O: 6 − 6 − ½(2) = −1

    All the other atoms are 0, so the total is −1, the charge on the ion.

  4. Count using one resonance structure. Include the three C–H bonds.

    σ: C–H 3; C–C 1; C=O 1; C–O 1; total 6
    π: C=O 1; total 1

  5. Count the electron domains around each carbon.

    CH3 carbon: 4 domains → sp3. COO carbon: 3 domains → sp2, trigonal planar, so the O–C–O angle is about 120°.

Two resonance structures; delocalized π electrons give equal C–O bonds; formal charges 0 and −1; 6 σ and 1 π; sp3 and sp2, about 120°.

Linking it up

The syllabus asks questions that connect this topic to others. Here are short answers to four of them.

Why are oxygen and ozone dissociated by different wavelengths of light?

O2 has a double bond. In ozone, delocalization makes both bonds intermediate between single and double, so they are weaker. A weaker bond needs less energy to break. Light of lower energy has a longer wavelength (Structure 1.3). So ozone is broken by longer wavelengths of ultraviolet light than oxygen is.

How does the resonance energy in benzene explain its relative unreactivity?

Delocalization makes benzene about 152 kJ mol-1 more stable than a ring with three separate C=C bonds. This extra stability is called the resonance energy. An addition reaction would destroy the delocalized ring and lose that stability. So benzene does not readily undergo addition. Substitution keeps the ring. You will study this in Reactivity 3.4.

How does the ability of some atoms to expand their octet relate to their position in the periodic table?

Atoms in period 2 never have more than eight valence electrons, because their valence shell is the second main energy level. Atoms in period 3 and below have a larger valence shell that can hold more than eight electrons. So expanded octets are found for elements such as P, S, Cl, Br, I and Xe (Structure 3.1).

What are the different assumptions made in the calculation of formal charge and of oxidation states?

Formal charge assumes that the electrons in every bond are shared equally. It treats the bonding as purely covalent. Oxidation state assumes that the electrons in every bond belong completely to the more electronegative atom. It treats the bonding as purely ionic. Real bonds are between these two extremes. You will use oxidation states in Structure 3.1 and Reactivity 3.2.
