Structure 2.4.1

Bonding is a continuum

Think of a colour chart for paint. Red, yellow and blue sit at the corners. Most paints are a mixture, somewhere between the corners. Bonding works in a similar way.

In Structure 2.1, 2.2 and 2.3 you met three bonding models. Each model describes one extreme kind of bonding.

The three bonding models
ModelWhat attracts whatTypical properties
IonicElectrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in a lattice.High melting point. Brittle. Conducts electricity only when molten or dissolved, because then the ions can move.
CovalentElectrostatic attraction between a shared pair of electrons and the two positively charged nuclei.Does not conduct, because there are no mobile charged particles. Molecular substances have low melting points. Covalent networks have very high melting points.
MetallicElectrostatic attraction between a lattice of cations and delocalized electrons.Conducts electricity and heat as a solid. Malleable.

Real substances do not fall into three separate boxes. The bonding in most substances has some character of more than one model. Chemists say that bonding is a continuum: it changes gradually from one type to another.

One example is hydrogen chloride. Its bond is covalent, but chlorine attracts the shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen does. The bond is polar. It is covalent with some ionic character.

Ionic, covalent and metallic are models, not boxes. The bonding in a real material is usually somewhere between them. The position between the models explains the properties of the material.

The bonding triangle

The bonding triangle is a map of this continuum. The three pure models sit at the three corners. A substance gets a position on the map from the electronegativities of its elements.

Remember that electronegativity, symbol χ, measures how strongly an atom attracts a shared pair of electrons.

  • Bottom left, metallic. Both elements have low electronegativity. Neither holds its outer electrons strongly, so the electrons are delocalized.
  • Bottom right, covalent. Both elements have high electronegativity. Both attract electrons strongly, so the electrons are shared.
  • Top, ionic. One element has low electronegativity and one has high electronegativity. Electrons are transferred, so ions form.

Between the covalent corner and the ionic top, the sharing becomes more and more unequal. This part is labelled polar covalent. The booklet prints no line between “covalent” and “polar covalent”, because the change is gradual.

The triangular bonding diagram is in section 17 of the data booklet. Electronegativity values are in section 9. You do not need to remember either.

When you explain a property, name the particles and the attraction. For example: “strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions” or “delocalized electrons are free to move”. A bare phrase such as “strong bonds” does not earn the mark.

Placing a compound in the bonding triangle

A map needs two coordinates. For the bonding triangle, you calculate both from the electronegativities of the two elements.

Δχ = |χa − χb|     average χ = χa + χb2
χa, χb
electronegativities of the two elements (no unit)
Δχ
electronegativity difference: the vertical axis (no unit)
average χ
average electronegativity: the horizontal axis (no unit)

The two vertical bars mean “the size of the difference”. Always subtract the smaller value from the larger value, so the answer is positive.

Method in words

  1. Look up the electronegativity of each element (data booklet, section 9).
  2. Subtract the smaller value from the larger value. This is the difference, Δχ. It goes up the triangle.
  3. Add the two values and divide by 2. This is the average. It goes along the triangle.
  4. Find the point on the triangle and read the region.
  5. Use the region to predict the properties.

The numbers in the formula do not matter. For MgCl2 you use one value for Mg and one value for Cl. You do not count the chlorine twice.

Magnesium oxide on the triangle

Use χ(Mg) = 1.3 and χ(O) = 3.4. Determine the position of magnesium oxide, MgO, in the bonding triangle. Predict its bonding type and two properties.

  1. The difference is the vertical coordinate. Subtract the smaller value from the larger value.

    Δχ = 3.4 − 1.3 = 2.1

  2. The average is the horizontal coordinate. Add, then divide by 2.

    average χ = (1.3 + 3.4) / 2 = 4.7 / 2 = 2.35

  3. Go along to 2.35 and up to 2.1. The point is in the upper part of the triangle.

    (2.35, 2.1) is in the ionic region

  4. Use the ionic model: strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, and ions that are fixed in the solid.

    high melting point; conducts electricity when molten but not as a solid

Δχ = 2.1 and average χ = 2.35, so MgO is ionic. It has a high melting point and conducts only when molten.

Phosphorus trichloride on the triangle

Use χ(P) = 2.2 and χ(Cl) = 3.2. Determine the position of phosphorus trichloride, PCl3, in the bonding triangle. Predict its bonding type and two properties.

  1. Use one value for each element. The 3 in the formula is not used.

    Δχ = 3.2 − 2.2 = 1.0

  2. Add the two values, then divide by 2.

    average χ = (2.2 + 3.2) / 2 = 5.4 / 2 = 2.7

  3. Go along to 2.7 and up to 1.0. The point is on the right, well above the base.

    (2.7, 1.0) is in the polar covalent region

  4. Use the covalent model: electrons are held in bonds, so no charged particles can move. PCl3 is made of small molecules, with only weak forces between them.

    does not conduct electricity; low melting point

Δχ = 1.0 and average χ = 2.7, so PCl3 is polar covalent. It does not conduct and it has a low melting point.

Compare the two examples. Both compounds contain a period 3 element joined to a more electronegative element. The larger difference puts MgO high in the triangle, in the ionic region. The smaller difference and larger average put PCl3 lower and further right, in the polar covalent region.

Do not decide by “metal with non-metal means ionic”. Use the two numbers. Tin(IV) chloride contains a metal and a non-metal, but its point (2.6, 1.2) is in the polar covalent region. It is a liquid made of molecules.

The average is not the sum. For χ values 1.3 and 3.4, the sum is 4.7 but the average is 2.35. Also check the axes: the average goes along, the difference goes up.

Calcium oxide is CaO. Use χ(Ca) = 1.0 and χ(O) = 3.4. Calculate the electronegativity difference.

  1. The difference is the larger value minus the smaller value.
  2. Set up 3.4 − 1.0.
Δχ = 3.4 − 1.0 = 2.4

Now calculate the average electronegativity for calcium oxide, using the same values.

  1. Add the two values first. Then divide by 2.
  2. Set up (1.0 + 3.4) / 2.
average χ = (1.0 + 3.4) / 2 = 4.4 / 2 = 2.2

The point (2.2, 2.4) is in the ionic region. Check it with the plotter above.

Silicon tetrachloride is SiCl4. Use χ(Si) = 1.9 and χ(Cl) = 3.2. Calculate the average electronegativity.

  1. Use one value for silicon and one value for chlorine. Ignore the 4 in the formula.
  2. Set up (1.9 + 3.2) / 2. Keep both decimal places.
average χ = (1.9 + 3.2) / 2 = 5.1 / 2 = 2.55

The difference is 3.2 − 1.9 = 1.3. The point (2.55, 1.3) is in the polar covalent region.

Predicting properties from the position

What each region of the triangle predicts
RegionModel that fits bestProperties to predict
IonicLattice of ionsHigh melting point. Brittle. Does not conduct as a solid. Conducts when molten or dissolved in water.
Covalent and polar covalentShared electron pairsDoes not conduct in any state. Low melting point if it is made of molecules. Very high melting point if it is a covalent network.
MetallicCations and delocalized electronsConducts electricity and heat as a solid. Malleable.

A point near a boundary has properties between the two models. Aluminium chloride, at (2.4, 1.6), lies just inside the polar covalent region, almost on the line with the ionic region. It is a solid that turns into a gas at about 180 °C. The ionic model alone does not predict such a low temperature.

When a point is almost on a line, say so in your answer. Give both numbers and write that the bonding is between the two types.

The triangle has a limit. It tells you the type of bonding. It does not tell you the structure. Carbon dioxide and silicon dioxide are both polar covalent, but CO2 is molecular and SiO2 is a covalent network.

Structure 2.4.3

Alloys

A gold ring is rarely pure gold. Pure gold is so soft that a ring would bend out of shape. Jewellers mix the gold with a little copper or silver to make it harder.

An alloy is a mixture of a metal and other metals or non-metals. Alloys have enhanced properties: they are often harder, stronger or more resistant to corrosion than the pure metal.

Common examples are bronze (copper with tin), brass (copper with zinc) and stainless steel (iron with chromium, nickel and a little carbon). Carbon is a non-metal, so steel shows that the added element does not have to be a metal.

Why metals can form alloys

In a metal, cations sit in a lattice and are attracted to the delocalized electrons all around them. This attraction acts equally in every direction. Chemists say metallic bonding is non-directional.

Because the bonding is non-directional, the lattice does not need one fixed arrangement of particular atoms. An atom of a different element can take a place in the lattice, and the delocalized electrons still hold everything together.

Why alloys are often harder and stronger

In a pure metal, all the cations are the same size. They pack in regular layers. When a force is applied, the layers can slide over each other. That is why pure metals are malleable.

In an alloy, the atoms of the added element are a different size. They disrupt the regular layers. The layers cannot slide over each other as easily, so the alloy is harder and stronger.

Alloys are mixtures. The amounts of the elements can vary, and the elements are not chemically combined in a fixed ratio. That is why an alloy is not a compound.

Explaining the hardness of an alloy

Brass is an alloy of copper and zinc. Explain why brass is harder than pure copper. [2 marks]

  1. An “explain” answer needs the starting picture: what makes pure copper soft.

    In pure copper, the cations are all the same size and form regular layers. The layers can slide over each other.

  2. This is the first marking point. Mention the different size and the effect on the regular arrangement.

    Zinc atoms are a different size from copper atoms, so they disrupt the regular layers of the lattice.

  3. This is the second marking point. Connect the structure to hardness.

    The layers cannot slide over each other as easily, so brass is harder.

Zinc atoms have a different size, so they disrupt the regular layers. The layers slide over each other less easily.

Do not write that an alloy is harder “because the bonds are stronger”. The mark is for the disrupted layers that slide less easily. Do not call an alloy a compound.

You do not need to learn the composition or the uses of any particular alloy. Any alloy in a question will be described for you.

Structure 2.4.4

Polymers and plastics

A paper-clip chain is made by joining many identical clips, one after another. A polymer is made in the same way, from small molecules.

A polymer is a large molecule made from many repeating subunits. The small molecules that join together are called monomers. Another word for a very large molecule is macromolecule. One polymer chain can contain thousands of monomer units.

Natural and synthetic polymers

  • Natural polymers are made by living things. Examples are starch and cellulose in plants, proteins, DNA and natural rubber.
  • Synthetic polymers are made in factories. Examples are poly(ethene), used for bags and bottles, and nylon, used for fabrics. Plastics are synthetic polymers.

Properties of plastics, explained by structure

Two facts about the structure explain most properties of plastics.

  • Inside each chain, the atoms are joined by strong covalent bonds.
  • Between one chain and the next, there are only weak intermolecular forces.
Common properties of plastics
PropertyKindReason from the structure
Unreactive (chemically inert)chemicalThe covalent bonds in the chains are strong and take a lot of energy to break.
Non-biodegradablechemicalMicroorganisms cannot easily break the strong covalent bonds, so the chains stay intact.
Electrical insulatorphysicalAll outer electrons are held in covalent bonds. There are no delocalized electrons or mobile ions.
Poor conductor of heatphysicalThere are no delocalized electrons to carry energy quickly.
Low densityphysicalThe chains contain light atoms such as carbon and hydrogen, and long chains do not pack tightly.
Softens on heating and can be moulded (many plastics)physicalThe weak forces between chains are overcome, so the chains can slide past each other.
Strong for its massphysicalThe chains are very long, so the many weak forces along each chain add up.

The same property can be useful and harmful. Plastics are unreactive, so a plastic bottle does not react with the drink inside it. But for the same reason plastic waste is not broken down in nature. It stays in the environment for a very long time.

Read the command carefully. If a question asks for a chemical property of a plastic, write “unreactive”, “chemically inert” or “non-biodegradable”. A physical property, such as “strong” or “low density”, does not earn the mark.

You do not need to learn the names, structures or uses of particular plastics. You do not need high-density and low-density poly(ethene), thermoplastic and thermosetting polymers, plasticizers or recycling codes.

Structure 2.4.5

Addition polymers

Picture a line of people, each with their arms folded. If every person unfolds their arms and holds hands with the neighbours on both sides, the line becomes one long chain. Nobody leaves the line.

An addition polymer forms in this way. Each monomer has a carbon to carbon double bond, C=C. One bond of the double bond breaks. Each of the two carbon atoms then forms a new single bond to the next monomer.

Hydrocarbons with a C=C double bond are called alkenes. You will study alkenes as a class of compound in Structure 3.2.

In addition polymerization the monomers add together and nothing else is made. The polymer is the only product. The repeating unit contains the same atoms as one monomer.

A repeating unit is the smallest group of atoms that repeats along the chain. It is drawn with a bond at each end that passes through square brackets. A small n after the brackets stands for a very large number.

The equation for making poly(ethene) from ethene shows this notation.

n CH2=CH2 → [–CH2–CH2–]n

Method: repeating unit from a monomer

  1. Redraw the monomer with the two double-bonded carbons side by side. Put every other group above or below those two carbons.
  2. Change the double bond to a single bond.
  3. Draw one new bond out from each of the two carbons, to the left and to the right. These are the continuation bonds.
  4. Add brackets and n if you want. Check that each carbon has four bonds.

Repeating unit from a monomer

Propene has the structure shown. Draw the repeating unit of the addition polymer formed from propene.

CH2=CH(CH3)

  1. Only these two carbons go into the backbone of the chain. The CH3 group is not part of the double bond, so it is a side group.

    backbone carbons: the CH2 carbon and the CH carbon

  2. One bond of the double bond breaks. The carbons stay joined by a single bond.

    CH2=CH(CH3) becomes CH2–CH(CH3)

  3. Each backbone carbon now needs one more bond. These bonds join the unit to its neighbours.

    –CH2–CH(CH3)–

  4. Each carbon must have four bonds. The CH3 group hangs off the chain.

    [–CH2–CH(CH3)–]n

The repeating unit is –CH2–CH(CH3)–, with the CH3 group as a side group.

Do not put all three carbons of propene in a row in the backbone. Only the two carbons of the double bond are in the chain. Do not leave the double bond in the repeating unit either: it has become a single bond.

Method: monomer from a polymer

  1. Mark off the chain two backbone carbons at a time. Each pair, with its side groups, is one repeating unit.
  2. Copy one pair with all four side groups.
  3. Remove the continuation bonds and draw a double bond between the two carbons.

Monomer from a polymer

A section of an addition polymer is shown. Deduce the structure of the monomer.

–CH2–CH(CN)–CH2–CH(CN)–

  1. The repeating unit of an addition polymer has two backbone carbons. Here the pattern repeats every two carbons.

    [–CH2–CH(CN)–]–CH2–CH(CN)–

  2. Keep all four side groups exactly where they are.

    –CH2–CH(CN)–

  3. The continuation bonds were made from the double bond. Remove them and restore C=C.

    CH2=CH(CN)

The monomer is CH2=CH(CN).

The mark for a repeating unit needs the correct structure and a continuation bond at each end. Draw only one repeating unit: drawing two loses the mark. Brackets and n are not required, but they are a good habit. A bond must end on the correct atom, for example on the C of a CH3 group.

You do not need to know how the double bond breaks, step by step. The calculation called atom economy comes in Reactivity 2.1. Here you only need the idea that an addition polymer is the only product, so no atoms are wasted.

You do not need to remember the structures of monomers. A question gives you the monomer, or gives you the polymer so you can deduce the monomer.

Structure 2.4.6

Condensation polymers

Imagine a line of people where each person must take off a glove before shaking hands with a neighbour. A chain forms, and a pile of gloves is left over. In a condensation polymer, the “glove” is a small molecule, usually water.

A condensation polymer forms when functional groups on the monomers react with each other. Each time two monomers join, a small molecule is released. A functional group is a group of atoms that gives a molecule its typical reactions.

Each monomer needs two functional groups, so that it can join to a neighbour on both sides. You need two kinds of condensation polymer.

The two kinds of condensation polymer you need
PolymerMonomersGroups that reactLink formedReleased
Polyestera diol and a dicarboxylic acid–OH and –COOHester link, –COO–H2O
Polyamidea diamine and a dicarboxylic acid–NH2 and –COOHamide link, –CONH–H2O

“Di” means two. A diol has two –OH groups. A dicarboxylic acid has two –COOH groups. A diamine has two –NH2 groups.

The water is made from an H atom of the –OH or –NH2 group and the –OH part of the –COOH group. A new bond then joins the O or N atom to the C=O carbon.

Method: repeating unit from two monomers

  1. Draw the two monomers side by side, with a –COOH group of one next to an –OH or –NH2 group of the other.
  2. Remove the –OH from the –COOH group and one H from the other group. These atoms form H2O.
  3. Join the C=O carbon to the O (polyester) or to the N (polyamide).
  4. At the two outer ends, remove an H from one end and an –OH from the other end. Draw continuation bonds there.

Repeating unit of a polyester

A polyester is made from the two monomers shown. Draw its repeating unit and state the other product.

HO–(CH2)3–OH + HOOC–(CH2)2–COOH

  1. An –OH group of the diol reacts with a –COOH group of the acid. This makes an ester link.

    –OH + HOOC– → ester link + H2O

  2. The H comes from the diol. The –OH comes from the –COOH group of the acid.

    HO–(CH2)3–O[H] + [HO]OC–(CH2)2–COOH

  3. The oxygen of the diol bonds to the carbon of the C=O group. This –O–C(=O)– group is the ester link.

    –(CH2)3–O–CO–(CH2)2–

  4. The other –OH group and the other –COOH group react in the same way with the next monomers. So the unit has a continuation bond at each end.

    [–O–(CH2)3–O–CO–(CH2)2–CO–]n

The repeating unit is –O–(CH2)3–O–CO–(CH2)2–CO–. The other product is water.

Repeating unit of a polyamide

A polyamide is made from the two monomers shown. Draw its repeating unit.

H2N–(CH2)4–NH2 + HOOC–(CH2)6–COOH

  1. An –NH2 group of the diamine reacts with a –COOH group of the acid. This makes an amide link.

    –NH2 + HOOC– → amide link + H2O

  2. One H comes from the –NH2 group, so the nitrogen keeps one H. The –OH comes from the –COOH group.

    H2N–(CH2)4–NH[H] + [HO]OC–(CH2)6–COOH

  3. The nitrogen bonds to the carbon of the C=O group. This –N(H)–C(=O)– group is the amide link.

    –(CH2)4–NH–CO–(CH2)6–

  4. The far –NH2 group loses an H and the far –COOH group loses an –OH when they join the next monomers.

    [–NH–(CH2)4–NH–CO–(CH2)6–CO–]n

The repeating unit is –NH–(CH2)4–NH–CO–(CH2)6–CO–. Water is also formed.

Each nitrogen atom in a polyamide chain still has one hydrogen atom: the link is –NH–CO–, not –N–CO–. Also remember the water. A condensation equation without the small molecule is incomplete.

Hydrolysis: the reverse reaction

Hydrolysis means breaking a bond by reaction with water. It is the reverse of condensation. Water breaks each ester or amide link. The –OH of water joins the C=O carbon, and the H of water joins the O or N atom. The monomers form again.

Monomers from a condensation polymer

The repeating unit of a polymer is shown. Identify the type of polymer and deduce the structures of the two monomers.

[–NH–(CH2)2–NH–CO–(CH2)3–CO–]n

  1. Look for C=O next to N or O in the backbone. Here C=O is next to N–H.

    –NH–CO– is an amide link, so this is a polyamide

  2. Hydrolysis breaks the bond between the N and the C=O carbon. Remember that the bonds at the ends of the unit are amide links too.

    –NH–(CH2)2–NH–   and   –CO–(CH2)3–CO–

  3. Each N gains an H, giving –NH2. Each C=O carbon gains an –OH, giving –COOH.

    H2N–(CH2)2–NH2   and   HOOC–(CH2)3–COOH

  4. Each monomer must have two functional groups: a diamine and a dicarboxylic acid.

    H2N–(CH2)2–NH2 + HOOC–(CH2)3–COOH

It is a polyamide. The monomers are H2N–(CH2)2–NH2 and HOOC–(CH2)3–COOH.

50 molecules of a diol and 50 molecules of a dicarboxylic acid join to make one polyester chain. How many molecules of water are released?

  1. One molecule of water is released for each link that forms.
  2. The chain contains 100 monomer units in a row. Count the links between them, as you would count the gaps between 100 fence posts.
monomer units in the chain = 50 + 50 = 100
links = 100 − 1 = 99, so 99 molecules of H2O

Biological macromolecules

All biological macromolecules form by condensation reactions and break down by hydrolysis. Proteins are an example. Amino acids join by condensation, and water is released. The link between amino acids is an amide link, so a protein is a natural polyamide. When you digest protein, hydrolysis breaks the links and gives amino acids again.

The same pattern is true for carbohydrates such as starch, and for DNA. The builder above includes amino acids as its third option.

Linking it up

The IB guide asks these linking questions. Short answers are enough at this stage.

What are the limitations of discrete bonding categories?

“Discrete” means separate. If you sort every substance into only three boxes, you lose information. Many substances lie between the models, such as polar covalent molecules and compounds near a boundary in the triangle. A single label then predicts the wrong properties. The bonding triangle is a better model because it shows the gradual change.

How do the trends in properties of period 3 oxides reflect the trend in their bonding?

From sodium to sulfur, the electronegativity of the element gets closer to that of oxygen. The difference falls from 2.5 for Na2O to 0.8 for SO3, and the average rises. The points move down and to the right in the triangle, from ionic to polar covalent. The properties change in step: the oxides on the left are ionic solids with high melting points, and the oxides on the right are made of molecules. You will meet their acid–base behaviour in Structure 3.1.

Why are alloys more correctly described as mixtures rather than as compounds?

In a compound, the elements are chemically bonded in a fixed ratio. In an alloy, the proportions can vary, and the atoms of the added element simply take places in the metal lattice. Non-directional metallic bonding makes this possible. This matches the meaning of “mixture” from Structure 1.1.

Why is the atom economy 100% for an addition polymerization reaction?

The polymer is the only product. Every atom of every monomer ends up in the polymer, so no atoms are wasted. You will calculate atom economy in Reactivity 2.1.

Why do composites like reinforced concretes, which are made from ionic and covalently bonded components and steel bars, have unique properties?

A composite is made from two or more different materials. Each part keeps its own bonding, so the composite combines their properties. Concrete has ionic and covalent bonding. It is hard and strong when it is squeezed, but it is brittle and cracks when it is pulled or bent. Steel has metallic bonding. It is strong when it is pulled, and it bends without breaking. Reinforced concrete, with steel bars inside the concrete, resists both squeezing and pulling. Neither material can do this alone.

Composites appear in the guide only in this linking question. You do not need to learn examples of composites or how they are made.

What are the structural features of some plastics that make them biodegradable?

Most plastics have a backbone of carbon atoms joined by strong, non-polar bonds. Water and microorganisms cannot break these bonds easily. A biodegradable plastic has other groups of atoms in its backbone, such as ester links, which can react with water. The chain then breaks into small molecules. You will meet these groups in Structure 3.2.

Ester and amide links are found in condensation polymers. They are broken by hydrolysis. So some polyesters and polyamides can be broken down in the environment, but addition polymers cannot.
