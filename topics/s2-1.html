Structure 2.1.1

How atoms become ions

Table salt is made from sodium and chlorine. Sodium is a soft metal that reacts violently with water. Chlorine is a poisonous green gas. The salt on your food is safe because it contains neither sodium atoms nor chlorine atoms. It contains ions.

An ion is an atom, or a group of atoms, with an overall electric charge. An atom becomes an ion when it loses or gains electrons. The nucleus does not change.

  • A metal atom loses electrons. It then has more protons than electrons, so it is a positive ion. A positive ion is called a cation.
  • A non-metal atom gains electrons. It then has more electrons than protons, so it is a negative ion. A negative ion is called an anion.

Charge on an ion = number of protons − number of electrons. Losing electrons makes the charge positive. Gaining electrons makes the charge negative.

Predicting the charge from the electron configuration

Look at the outer shell of the atom. The outer shell is the shell with the highest number in the electron configuration.

A sodium atom is 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s1. Its outer shell holds one electron. Sodium loses that one electron. The ion Na+ is 1s2 2s2 2p6, the same configuration as the noble gas neon.

A chlorine atom is 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p5. Its outer shell holds seven electrons, one fewer than a full s and p set of eight. Chlorine gains one electron. The ion Cl- is 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6, the same configuration as argon.

This pattern works for the main-group elements. The ion usually has the electron configuration of the nearest noble gas.

Method in words

  1. Find the outer shell and count its electrons.
  2. One, two or three outer electrons: the atom is usually a metal and loses them all. The charge is 1+, 2+ or 3+.
  3. Five, six or seven outer electrons: the atom is usually a non-metal and gains electrons until the outer s and p sublevels hold eight. The charge is 3−, 2− or 1−.
  4. Check: protons − electrons must equal the charge.

Charge from an electron configuration

An atom has the electron configuration 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6 4s2. Predict the charge of its ion, and write the electron configuration of the ion.

  1. The outer shell is the one with the highest number. Here it is shell 4.

    outer shell: 4s2, so 2 outer electrons

  2. Two outer electrons means a metal. Losing 2 electrons takes far less energy than gaining 6.

    the atom loses 2 electrons

  3. Add the superscripts to count the electrons in the atom: 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 + 2 = 20. A neutral atom has the same number of protons.

    protons = 20, electrons in the ion = 20 − 2 = 18
    charge = 20 − 18 = 2+

  4. Remove the two 4s electrons. What is left is the configuration of argon, a noble gas.

    1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6

The ion has a charge of 2+ and the configuration 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6. (The element is calcium, so the ion is Ca^2+.)

An atom has the electron configuration 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p1. Its ion is positive. What is the size of the charge? Type the number only.

  1. Which shell is the outer shell? Count all the electrons in it, in both sublevels.
  2. Shell 3 holds 2 + 1 electrons. A metal atom loses all its outer electrons.
outer shell: 3s2 3p1 = 3 electrons, all lost
charge = 13 protons − 10 electrons = 3+

The size of the charge is 3. The ion is Al^3+.

A phosphorus atom is 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p3. How many electrons are in a phosphide ion?

  1. Phosphorus is a non-metal with 5 outer electrons. How many more does it need to reach 8?
  2. The atom has 15 electrons and gains 3: 15 + 3.
outer shell: 3s2 3p3 = 5 electrons, so the atom gains 3
electrons = 15 + 3 = 18

The phosphide ion, P^3-, has 18 electrons and the same configuration as argon.

Gaining electrons does not make an ion positive. Electrons are negative, so an atom that gains two electrons has a charge of 2−. An atom that loses two electrons has a charge of 2+.

Transition elements can form more than one ion

The table above works for main-group elements. It does not work for the transition elements in the middle of the periodic table. A transition element can form ions with different charges.

  • Iron forms Fe^2+ and Fe^3+.
  • Copper forms Cu+ and Cu^2+.

You cannot predict the charge of a transition element ion from its group number. The question must tell you, usually with a Roman numeral in the name. Iron(III) means Fe^3+. Try iron, copper and cobalt in the ion maker.

Here you only need to know that a transition element can form ions with different charges. The explanation uses successive ionization energies and is Additional HL content in Structure 3.1.

Write the charge with the number first and the sign second: 2+ and 3−, not +2 and −3. For a charge of one, write the sign only: Na+, Cl-.

Structure 2.1.2

The ionic bond

Rub a balloon on your hair and it sticks to a wall. Objects with opposite electric charges attract each other. This attraction is called an electrostatic attraction. Ions do the same thing, with far stronger forces.

An ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.

When sodium reacts with chlorine, each sodium atom passes one electron to a chlorine atom. This transfer makes the ions Na+ and Cl-. The bond is what happens next: the positive ions and the negative ions attract each other.

“An ionic bond is the transfer of electrons” is not accepted. The transfer of electrons is how the ions form. The bond is the attraction between the ions after they have formed.

The attraction acts in every direction. A sodium ion attracts every chloride ion near it, not one partner. This is why ionic compounds form giant structures and not small molecules. You will see this in the lattice section below.

The definition needs three things: “electrostatic attraction”, “between”, and “oppositely charged ions” (or “cations and anions”). An answer without the word electrostatic can lose the mark.

When should you expect ionic bonding?

Ionic bonding is expected between a metal and a non-metal. The metal atoms lose electrons and the non-metal atoms gain them.

A better test uses electronegativity. Electronegativity is a measure of how strongly an atom attracts the electrons in a bond. Metals have low values. Non-metals on the right of the periodic table have high values.

If the difference in electronegativity between two elements is large, one atom takes the electrons completely and ions form. Sodium is 0.9 and chlorine is 3.2. The difference is 3.2 − 0.9 = 2.3, which is large, so sodium chloride is ionic.

Electronegativity values are in section 9 of the data booklet. Use these values, because older books give slightly different numbers.

The IB guide gives no fixed number for “large”. Many books use a difference of about 1.8 as a rough guide, but it is not a rule. Bonding changes gradually from ionic to covalent. The proper IB tool is the bonding triangle in the data booklet, which you will use in Structure 2.4.

Structure 2.1.2

Formulas and names of ionic compounds

A crystal of salt does not give you an electric shock. An ionic compound has no overall charge. So the positive charges and the negative charges in its formula must add up to zero. That one idea gives you every ionic formula.

Polyatomic ions

Some ions contain more than one atom. A polyatomic ion is a group of atoms joined by covalent bonds, with an overall charge. The group stays together and behaves as one ion.

You must know these seven by name and by formula. They are not in the data booklet.

A compound such as ammonium nitrate, NH4NO3, contains two kinds of bond. The bonds inside each polyatomic ion are covalent. The bond between the cation and the anion is ionic. Questions ask you to say which is where.

Formula from the ions: method in words

  1. Write the two ions with their charges, cation first.
  2. Find the smallest total charge that both charges divide into. For 3+ and 2− this is 6.
  3. Work out how many of each ion give that total: 6 ÷ 3 = 2 cations and 6 ÷ 2 = 3 anions.
  4. Write the formula with these numbers as subscripts. Do not write the number 1. Do not write the charges.
  5. If you need two or more of a polyatomic ion, put the ion in brackets and write the number outside.
  6. Check that the total positive charge equals the total negative charge, and that the numbers are the smallest whole numbers.

Formula with a polyatomic ion: aluminium sulfate

Deduce the formula of aluminium sulfate.

  1. Aluminium is in group 13, so its ion is 3+. Sulfate is one of the seven polyatomic ions to learn.

    Al3+ and SO42−

  2. The smallest number that both 3 and 2 divide into is 6.

    total charge needed: 6+ and 6−

  3. Divide the total by the charge on each ion.

    6 ÷ 3 = 2 Al3+ ions
    6 ÷ 2 = 3 SO42− ions

  4. There are three sulfate ions, so sulfate goes in brackets with the 3 outside. The 4 inside the bracket belongs to the sulfate ion and does not change.

    Al2(SO4)3

  5. The total charge must be zero.

    2 × (3+) = 6+   and   3 × (2−) = 6−

Al2(SO4)3

Deduce the formula of ammonium carbonate.

    NH4+ and CO32−

    2 ÷ 1 = 2 NH4+ ions

A Roman numeral in the name: iron(III) oxide

Deduce the formula of iron(III) oxide.

    O2−

Brackets change the meaning. Mg(OH)2 has two hydroxide ions: 2 oxygen atoms and 2 hydrogen atoms. Without brackets, MgOH2 would mean 1 oxygen atom and 2 hydrogen atoms, which is wrong. Also use the simplest ratio: magnesium oxide is MgO, not Mg2O2.

Deduce the formula of ammonium sulfate. How many atoms, in total, are in one formula unit?

  1. The ions are NH4+ and SO4^2-. How many ammonium ions balance one sulfate ion?
  2. The formula is (NH4)2SO4. The 2 outside the bracket multiplies everything inside it.
2 × (1+) = 2+ balances 1 × (2−), so the formula is (NH4)2SO4
atoms in 2 NH4+ = 2 × (1 + 4) = 10
atoms in 1 SO42− = 1 + 4 = 5
total = 10 + 5 = 15

There are 15 atoms: 2 N, 8 H, 1 S and 4 O.

Naming ionic compounds

  1. Name the cation first, then the anion.
  2. A metal cation has the name of the metal: sodium, calcium, aluminium.
  3. An anion made from one element ends in -ide: chlorine becomes chloride, oxygen becomes oxide, sulfur becomes sulfide, nitrogen becomes nitride.
  4. A polyatomic ion keeps its own name: sulfate, nitrate, carbonate, hydroxide, ammonium.
  5. If the metal can form more than one ion, write its charge as a Roman numeral in brackets straight after the metal name, with no space: iron(II), iron(III), copper(I), copper(II).

A compound of only two elements is called a binary compound. Its name always ends in -ide: sodium chloride, magnesium oxide, potassium sulfide.

The name does not say how many ions there are. CaCl2 is calcium chloride, not “calcium dichloride”. You work out the numbers from the charges.

Name from a formula

Deduce the name of Cu(NO3)2.

  1. The cation is written first. The bracket shows a polyatomic ion that you should recognise.

    one copper ion and two NO3− (nitrate) ions

  2. Copper is a transition element, so you must work out its charge. The total negative charge is 2 × (1−) = 2−. The compound is neutral, so the one copper ion must be 2+.

    Cu2+

  3. Copper can also be 1+, so the name needs a Roman numeral.

    copper(II)

  4. A polyatomic ion keeps its own name. The number of ions is not part of the name.

    copper(II) nitrate

copper(II) nitrate

An oxide of manganese has the formula Mn2O3. What is the size of the positive charge on each manganese ion? Type the number only.

  1. An oxide ion is O^2-. What is the total negative charge of three oxide ions?
  2. The total positive charge must be 6+, shared between 2 manganese ions.
total negative charge = 3 × (2−) = 6−
total positive charge = 6+, from 2 Mn ions
charge on each Mn = 6 ÷ 2 = 3+

The size of the charge is 3, so the compound is manganese(III) oxide.

Read the command carefully. If the question asks for a name, a formula earns no mark. If it asks for a formula, a name earns no mark. For a transition element, the name is not complete without the Roman numeral.

You only need to remember the seven polyatomic ions in the table. If a question uses another ion, it will give you the formula and the charge. You will meet the Roman numeral again in Structure 3.1, where it is called the oxidation state.

Structure 2.1.3

The ionic lattice

Look at grains of table salt through a magnifying glass. Many of them are tiny cubes. The cube shape comes from the way the ions are stacked inside.

An ionic compound is a lattice: a regular, repeating, three-dimensional arrangement of ions. Each ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge. The electrostatic attraction acts in all directions and holds the whole structure together.

What the formula of an ionic compound means

There are no separate molecules in the lattice. You cannot say which chloride ion “belongs” to which sodium ion. So the formula NaCl cannot describe a molecule.

The formula of an ionic compound gives the ratio of the ions in the lattice, in the smallest whole numbers. In sodium chloride there is one Na+ for every Cl-. In calcium chloride, CaCl2, there are two Cl- for every Ca^2+.

A formula that gives the simplest ratio is an empirical formula. You met empirical formulas in Structure 1.4. One “NaCl” or one “CaCl2” is called a formula unit.

Do not write “a molecule of sodium chloride” or “the intermolecular forces in sodium chloride”. An ionic compound has no molecules. Write “ions”, “lattice” and “electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions”.

You do not need unit cells, coordination numbers (such as “6 : 6”), or the names of different lattice types. Different ionic compounds have different arrangements, but the idea is the same: a three-dimensional lattice of oppositely charged ions.

Structure 2.1.3

Explaining the properties of ionic compounds

You can heat salt in a frying pan and nothing seems to happen. It does not melt and it does not boil away. The lattice model explains this, and the other properties of ionic compounds.

Every explanation has the same two parts. First say what the particles are and what holds them together. Then say what that means for the property.

Volatility

Volatility describes how easily a substance turns into a gas. Ionic compounds have a low volatility. They also have high melting points and high boiling points. Sodium chloride melts at 801 °C and boils at 1413 °C.

The reason: the electrostatic attraction between the oppositely charged ions is strong, and it acts throughout the whole lattice. A large amount of energy is needed to overcome it and separate the ions.

Electrical conductivity

A substance conducts electricity if it contains charged particles that are free to move. An ionic compound always contains charged particles: its ions. The question is whether they can move.

  • Solid: does not conduct. The ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice. They can only vibrate.
  • Molten (melted to a liquid): conducts. The lattice has broken down and the ions are free to move.
  • Dissolved in water (aqueous solution): conducts. The ions are separated and free to move through the solution.

In a molten or dissolved ionic compound, the current is carried by moving ions, not by electrons. And a solid ionic compound does contain ions. It does not conduct because its ions cannot move, not because it has none.

What happens when the moving ions reach the electrodes is called electrolysis. It is taught in Reactivity 3.2. Here you only need to explain why the liquid conducts.

Solubility

Many ionic compounds dissolve in water. A water molecule is polar: its oxygen atom has a small negative charge and its hydrogen atoms have small positive charges. You will learn why in Structure 2.2.

The negative end of a water molecule is attracted to cations. The positive end is attracted to anions. Many water molecules together attract an ion strongly enough to pull it out of the lattice. The ion then moves away, surrounded by water molecules.

Ionic compounds do not dissolve in non-polar solvents such as hexane. Non-polar molecules have no charged ends, so they do not attract the ions strongly enough to separate them.

Not every ionic compound dissolves in water. In some, such as calcium carbonate, the attraction between the ions in the lattice is too strong for the water molecules to overcome.

Brittleness

Ionic crystals are hard, but they are also brittle: they shatter when hit. The syllabus names only volatility, conductivity and solubility. Brittleness is a short extra: the same lattice model explains it.

Put it together

For conductivity, the mark is for “ions are free to move” (or “mobile ions”) when molten or dissolved, and “ions are in fixed positions” in the solid. For melting point, the mark is for “strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions” that needs “a lot of energy to overcome”.

Structure 2.1.3

Lattice enthalpy: how strong is the ionic bonding?

Two fridge magnets that are stuck together need a pull to separate them. Stronger magnets need a harder pull. In the same way, you can measure how strongly ions are held in a lattice by the energy needed to pull them completely apart.

The lattice enthalpy is the enthalpy change when one mole of a solid ionic compound is separated into gaseous ions, so far apart that they no longer attract each other.

NaCl(s) → Na+(g) + Cl−(g)    ΔHlattice = +790 kJ mol−1
ΔHlattice
lattice enthalpy kJ mol-1
(s), (g)
solid lattice, separate gaseous ions

Separating ions that attract each other always needs energy. So this process is endothermic and every lattice enthalpy is a positive number.

Lattice enthalpy is a measure of the strength of the ionic bonding. A larger lattice enthalpy means stronger electrostatic attraction between the ions. It usually also means a higher melting point.

Section 16 of the data booklet lists lattice enthalpies. It defines them for the endothermic process: solid → gaseous ions. Ionic radii are in section 10. Some older books define lattice enthalpy the other way round and give negative values. Use the booklet.

Two things decide the size of the lattice enthalpy

  • The charges on the ions. Ions with higher charges attract each other more strongly. A 2+ ion and a 2− ion attract much more strongly than a 1+ ion and a 1− ion.
  • The radii of the ions. Smaller ions can get closer together. The shorter the distance between the centres of the ions, the stronger the attraction.

So the lattice enthalpy is largest for small ions with high charges.

Method in words: comparing two ionic compounds

  1. Write the ions in each compound, with their charges.
  2. Compare the charges. Say which compound has ions with higher charges.
  3. Compare the ionic radii. Say which compound has smaller ions.
  4. Link to the force: higher charge or smaller ionic radius gives stronger electrostatic attraction between the oppositely charged ions.
  5. Link to the property: stronger attraction means a larger lattice enthalpy, and more energy is needed to melt the solid.

Comparing melting points

Magnesium oxide melts at 2852 °C. Sodium chloride melts at 801 °C. Explain why the melting point of magnesium oxide is much higher. Use the data booklet.

  1. The explanation is about ions, so start by naming them with their charges.

    MgO: Mg2+ and O2−
    NaCl: Na+ and Cl−

  2. Higher charges give stronger attraction.

    the ions in MgO have higher charges (2+ and 2−) than the ions in NaCl (1+ and 1−)

  3. Use section 10 of the data booklet. Smaller ions are closer together, which gives stronger attraction.

    Mg2+ 72 pm is smaller than Na+ 102 pm
    O2− 140 pm is smaller than Cl− 181 pm

  4. This is the line that earns the final mark. Name the force and say what the energy is used for.

    stronger electrostatic attraction between the oppositely charged ions in MgO, so more energy is needed to overcome it

  5. Section 16 of the data booklet agrees: the lattice enthalpy of MgO is much larger.

    MgO 3791 kJ mol−1   NaCl 790 kJ mol−1

The ions in MgO have higher charges (2+ and 2−, against 1+ and 1−) and smaller ionic radii. So the electrostatic attraction between the oppositely charged ions is stronger in MgO (its lattice enthalpy is larger), and more energy is needed to overcome it.

The ionic radius of K+ is 138 pm and the ionic radius of Br- is 196 pm. Estimate the distance between the centres of a potassium ion and a bromide ion that touch in potassium bromide.

  1. When two spheres touch, the distance between their centres is one radius plus the other radius.
  2. 138 + 196
distance = 138 pm + 196 pm = 334 pm

This is larger than the 283 pm in NaCl, so the attraction is weaker. The data booklet agrees: the lattice enthalpy of KBr is 691 kJ mol-1, smaller than the 790 kJ mol-1 of NaCl.

The lattice enthalpy of MgO is 3791 kJ mol-1. The lattice enthalpy of NaCl is 790 kJ mol-1. How many times larger is the lattice enthalpy of MgO? Give your answer to 2 or 3 significant figures.

  1. “How many times larger” means divide the larger value by the smaller value.
  2. 3791 ÷ 790
3791 kJ mol−1 ÷ 790 kJ mol−1 = 4.7987…

About 4.80 times larger. The units cancel. Doubling both charges, and making the ions smaller, makes the attraction far stronger.

Write “ionic radius”, not “atomic radius” and not only “size”. The particles in the lattice are ions, and an ion does not have the same radius as its atom. Also do not write “MgO has stronger intermolecular forces”: there are no molecules.

A full comparison names the factor (ion charge, ionic radius, or both), says which compound has the higher charge or the smaller ionic radius, and ends with “stronger electrostatic attraction between the ions”. If you are asked which quantity measures the strength of ionic bonding, the answer is “lattice enthalpy”.

Lattice enthalpies are found by experiment using an energy cycle called a Born–Haber cycle. That is Additional HL content in Reactivity 1.2. Here you only compare lattice enthalpies using ion charge and ionic radius. You do not need any equation that calculates a lattice enthalpy.

Linking it up

The IB guide asks these linking questions. Short answers are enough at this stage.

How does the position of an element in the periodic table relate to the charge of its ion(s)?

The group number tells you the number of outer electrons. Metals in groups 1, 2 and 13 lose their 1, 2 or 3 outer electrons and form 1+, 2+ and 3+ ions. Non-metals in groups 15, 16 and 17 gain 3, 2 or 1 electrons and form 3−, 2− and 1− ions. Transition elements, in the middle block, can form ions with more than one charge. Structure 3.1 looks at the periodic table in detail.

Why is the formation of an ionic compound from its elements a redox reaction?

When sodium reacts with chlorine, each sodium atom loses an electron and each chlorine atom gains one. Losing electrons is called oxidation and gaining electrons is called reduction. Both happen together, so the reaction is a redox reaction. Redox is taught in Reactivity 3.2.

What experimental data demonstrate the physical properties of ionic compounds?

Three simple tests give the evidence. Measuring the melting point shows that it is high. Testing with a circuit shows that the solid does not conduct but the molten compound and the solution do. Shaking the solid with water and with a non-polar solvent shows where it dissolves. A substance with a high melting point that conducts only when molten or dissolved is very likely ionic.

How can lattice enthalpies and the bonding continuum explain the trend in melting points of metal chlorides across period 3?

Sodium chloride and magnesium chloride are ionic lattices with large lattice enthalpies, so they have high melting points. Moving across the period, the difference in electronegativity between the element and chlorine gets smaller. The bonding becomes more covalent. The chlorides of silicon and phosphorus are made of small molecules and have low melting points. Structure 2.4 and Structure 3.1 return to this.
