Structure 2.3.1

The metallic bond

Pick up a metal spoon. It is shiny and it bends before it breaks. It feels cold, because it carries heat away from your hand quickly. One model of bonding explains all of this.

A metal atom has only a few outer electrons, and it does not hold them strongly. In a piece of metal, these outer electrons leave their own atoms. They are no longer tied to any one atom. They can move through the whole piece of metal.

Electrons like this are called delocalized electrons. “Delocalized” means “not in one fixed place”.

Each atom that has lost its outer electrons is now a positive ion, a cation. The cations are packed in a regular, repeating pattern called a lattice.

The cations are positive. The delocalized electrons are negative. Opposite charges attract, and this attraction holds the metal together.

A metallic bond is the electrostatic attraction between a lattice of cations and delocalized electrons.

Electrostatic attraction means attraction between opposite charges. You used the same words for the ionic bond in Structure 2.1 and the covalent bond in Structure 2.2. The three bonds differ in which particles attract each other.

The bond has no direction

A covalent bond joins two particular atoms. A metallic bond does not. The delocalized electrons are spread through the whole lattice, so each cation is attracted to the electrons all around it, in every direction.

Chemists say the metallic bond is non-directional. This one idea explains why you can change the shape of a metal. You will use it in the next section.

A full description needs three things: “electrostatic attraction”, “lattice of cations” (or positive ions) and “delocalized electrons”. Some books call the electrons “a sea of electrons”. That is only a picture. In the exam, use the word “delocalized” and say what the electrons are attracted to.

The attraction is not between the metal ions. The cations are all positive, so they repel each other. It is also not between the electrons. The attraction is between the cations and the delocalized electrons.

A metal contains no molecules, so it has no intermolecular forces. Never write “intermolecular forces” or “molecules” in an answer about a metal. Write “metallic bonding”.

You do not need band theory. You do not need the names of the ways that metal atoms pack together (such as body-centred cubic, face-centred cubic or hexagonal close packing) or any unit cells. “A lattice of cations” is enough.

Structure 2.3.1

Explaining the properties of metals

The syllabus names three properties that you must be able to explain: electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity and malleability. Each explanation starts from the same picture: a lattice of cations and delocalized electrons.

Electrical conductivity

An electric current is a flow of charged particles. So a substance conducts electricity only if it contains charged particles that are free to move.

A metal has them: the delocalized electrons. When you connect a voltage across a metal, the delocalized electrons drift towards the positive terminal. New electrons enter the metal from the negative terminal. The cations stay where they are.

The delocalized electrons are still there when the metal melts. So a metal conducts electricity as a solid and as a liquid.

Do not write “the ions move” or “free ions” for a metal. In a solid metal the cations only vibrate about fixed positions. It is the delocalized electrons that move and carry the charge. Moving ions carry the charge in molten or dissolved ionic compounds (Structure 2.1).

Thermal conductivity

Thermal conductivity is how well a material lets heat pass through it. Heating one end of a metal gives the particles there more kinetic energy.

The delocalized electrons at the hot end move faster. They are free to move through the structure, so they carry this kinetic energy to the cooler parts quickly.

The cations help too. They are packed closely together. A cation that vibrates strongly passes some of its energy to its neighbours.

Malleability

A malleable material can be hammered or pressed into a new shape without breaking. Metals are malleable. Most can also be pulled into wires.

The cations in a metal are arranged in layers. When a force is applied, one layer can slide over the next.

After the slide, each cation has moved to a new position. But the delocalized electrons are still all around it. The attraction is still there, because the metallic bond is non-directional. The bond is not broken. The metal has a new shape.

Compare this with an ionic crystal. If a layer of ions slides, ions with the same charge come next to each other. They repel, and the crystal splits. That is why an ionic crystal is brittle. Choose “Hit it with a hammer” in the model above to see both.

For malleability, the marks are for: layers of cations can slide over each other, and the metallic bond is not broken (it is non-directional). “Delocalized electrons can move” on its own explains conductivity, not malleability.

Structure 2.3.2

How strong is a metallic bond?

Caesium melts in a warm room, at 28.44 °C. Magnesium melts at 650.0 °C. Both are metals, so why are they so different?

To melt a metal, you must give the cations enough energy to move out of their lattice positions and past each other. The stronger the attraction between the cations and the delocalized electrons, the more energy this takes. So a stronger metallic bond gives a higher melting point.

The strength of a metallic bond depends on two things about the metal ion.

  1. The charge of the ion. A cation with a greater charge attracts the delocalized electrons more strongly. A greater charge also means that each atom has given more delocalized electrons. A Mg^2+ ion comes with two delocalized electrons. A Na+ ion comes with one.
  2. The radius of the ion. In a smaller cation, the nucleus is closer to the delocalized electrons around the ion. The attraction is stronger.

A greater ion charge (with more delocalized electrons per atom) and a smaller ionic radius both give a stronger metallic bond, and so a higher melting point.

The IB guide also uses the words electron density. In a metal, this means how many delocalized electrons there are in a given volume. More delocalized electrons per atom and smaller ions both give a higher electron density. A higher electron density means a stronger attraction to the cations.

Some books say instead that a small ion with a large charge has a high “charge density”. It is the same idea. In an answer, it is safest to name the charge and the radius of the ion.

Melting points of the elements are in section 8 of the data booklet, in °C. Ionic radii are in section 10, in units of 10−12 m (the same as pm). You do not need to remember any of these values.

Method in words: comparing two melting points

  1. Write the ion that each metal forms. Use the group number: group 1 gives 1+, group 2 gives 2+, group 13 gives 3+.
  2. Compare the charges, and so the number of delocalized electrons per atom.
  3. Compare the ionic radii. Down a group the radius increases. Across a period, for these cations, it decreases.
  4. Say which metal has the stronger electrostatic attraction between its cations and its delocalized electrons.
  5. Link to energy: a stronger attraction needs more energy to overcome, so the melting point is higher.

Across a period: magnesium and sodium

Explain why magnesium has a higher melting point than sodium. [3 marks]

  1. The explanation is about the cations in each lattice, so start by naming them.

    sodium: Na+    magnesium: Mg2+

  2. A greater ion charge means more delocalized electrons per atom. Both make the attraction stronger.

    The Mg^2+ ion has a greater charge than the Na+ ion. Magnesium has two delocalized electrons per atom and sodium has one.

  3. A smaller cation attracts the delocalized electrons more strongly.

    The Mg^2+ ion (72 pm) is smaller than the Na+ ion (102 pm).

  4. The mark is for saying what attracts what. Then finish the chain: stronger attraction, more energy, higher melting point.

    The electrostatic attraction between the cations and the delocalized electrons is stronger in magnesium, so more energy is needed to overcome it.

Mg2+ has a greater charge than Na+, and magnesium has two delocalized electrons per atom, not one. Mg2+ also has a smaller ionic radius. So the electrostatic attraction between the cations and the delocalized electrons is stronger in magnesium, and more energy is needed to overcome it.

Down a group: the group 1 metals

The melting points of the group 1 metals decrease from lithium (180.5 °C) to caesium (28.44 °C). Explain this trend. [2 marks]

  1. If the charge does not change, it cannot be the reason for the trend. Saying so shows you have checked.

    Every group 1 metal forms a 1+ ion, and each atom gives one delocalized electron.

  2. Each step down the group adds one more occupied energy level, so the ion is larger.

    ionic radius: Li+ 76 pm, Na+ 102 pm, K+ 138 pm, Rb+ 152 pm, Cs+ 167 pm

    The ionic radius increases down the group.

  3. In a larger ion, the nucleus is further from the delocalized electrons, so it attracts them less strongly.

    The electrostatic attraction between the cations and the delocalized electrons becomes weaker down the group, so less energy is needed to overcome it.

The ions all have the same charge (1+), but the ionic radius increases down the group. The electrostatic attraction between the cations and the delocalized electrons becomes weaker, so less energy is needed to overcome it.

Do not explain a melting point by the mass of the atoms. Caesium atoms are about 19 times heavier than lithium atoms, but caesium has the lower melting point. The reasons are always the charge of the ion and the radius of the ion.

In a comparison, write about both metals and use comparing words: “greater charge”, “smaller radius”, “stronger attraction”. Always end with the attraction between the cations and the delocalized electrons, and the energy needed to overcome it.

This simple model gives the direction of the two trends above. It does not explain every number. The rise from magnesium to aluminium is small. In group 2, magnesium (650.0 °C) melts at a lower temperature than calcium (842.0 °C). Gallium, below aluminium, melts at only 29.77 °C. The way the cations are packed also matters, and that is not part of the IB course. You are not asked to explain these values. The guide asks only for “a simple treatment in terms of charge of cations and electron density”.

Structure 2.3.3

Transition elements: delocalized d-electrons

Iron melts at 1538 °C. Calcium, in the same period, melts at 842.0 °C, and potassium melts at only 63.38 °C. Transition elements have high melting points. The reason is the number of electrons they can delocalize.

In potassium and calcium, only the 4s electrons are delocalized. A transition element has electrons in the 3d sublevel as well. The 3d and 4s sublevels are close in energy.

Transition elements have delocalized d-electrons. In a first-row transition element, 3d electrons are delocalized as well as 4s electrons.

High melting points

With d-electrons delocalized as well as s-electrons, a transition element has more delocalized electrons per atom than an s-block metal in the same period. The electrostatic attraction between the cations and the delocalized electrons is stronger. More energy is needed to overcome it, so the melting point is high.

Electrical conductivity

The explanation has the same form as for any metal. The delocalized electrons are free to move through the structure and carry charge. For a transition element, say that these include d-electrons as well as s-electrons.

Why a transition element has a high melting point

Titanium is a transition element. It melts at 1670 °C. Calcium, an s-block metal in the same period, melts at 842.0 °C. Explain why titanium has the higher melting point. [2 marks]

  1. This is the fact the syllabus statement gives you. Use the word “d-electrons”.

    Ca: [Ar] 4s2    Ti: [Ar] 3d2 4s2

    In titanium, 3d electrons are delocalized as well as 4s electrons. In calcium, only the two 4s electrons are delocalized.

  2. More delocalized electrons per atom means more negative charge for each cation to attract.

    Titanium has more delocalized electrons per atom than calcium.

  3. This step is the same for every melting point question in this topic.

    The electrostatic attraction between the cations and the delocalized electrons is stronger in titanium, so more energy is needed to overcome it.

In titanium, 3d electrons as well as 4s electrons are delocalized, so there are more delocalized electrons per atom than in calcium. The electrostatic attraction between the cations and the delocalized electrons is stronger, so more energy is needed to overcome it.

The key phrase is “delocalized d-electrons”, or “d-electrons as well as s-electrons are delocalized”. Then finish in the usual way: stronger electrostatic attraction between the cations and the delocalized electrons.

Variable oxidation states, coloured compounds and catalysis are chemical properties of transition elements. They do not explain a melting point or conductivity. Keep the physical explanation (delocalized d-electrons) separate.

The chemical properties of transition elements are in Structure 3.1 and Reactivity 3.4. On this page you need only the two physical properties: high melting point and electrical conductivity. You are not asked to count how many d-electrons are delocalized, or to explain why the melting points from scandium to copper go up and down. Zinc is not shown in the chart. Structure 3.1 explains why zinc does not behave like a typical transition element.

Linking it up

The IB guide asks these linking questions. Short answers are enough at this stage.

What are the features of metallic bonding that make it possible for metals to form alloys?

The metallic bond is non-directional. The delocalized electrons attract any cation, of any size, in any position. So atoms of another element can take places in the lattice and the metal still holds together. Alloys and their properties are in Structure 2.4.

What experimental data demonstrate the physical properties of metals, and trends in these properties?

Melting points are one example. The values in section 8 of the data booklet show the fall down group 1 and the rise from sodium to aluminium. Measurements of electrical conductivity show that metals conduct as solids and as liquids. Periodic trends are studied in Structure 3.1.

What trends in the reactivity of metals can be predicted from the periodic table?

Metals react by losing electrons. Down group 1 the outer electron is further from the nucleus and is lost more easily, so the metals become more reactive. This is a chemical trend, and it is taught in Structure 3.1 and Reactivity 3.2. Do not mix it up with melting point, which is a physical property with its own explanation.

Why is the trend in melting points of metals across a period less evident across the d-block?

From sodium to aluminium, each step adds one delocalized electron per atom, increases the charge of the ion and makes the ion much smaller. All three changes make the bonding stronger, so the trend is clear. Across the d-block, every element has both d-electrons and s-electrons that can be delocalized. But the number of delocalized electrons does not rise by one at each step. The size of the atoms also changes less from one element to the next. No single factor changes steadily, so there is no simple trend.
