Introduction to the particulate nature of matter

Guiding questionHow can we model the particulate nature of matter?

Structure 1.1.1

Elements, compounds and mixtures

Think about a glass of sea water. You can get salt out of it by leaving it in the sun. You cannot get hydrogen out of the water that way. The salt and the water are only mixed. The hydrogen and oxygen in the water are chemically bonded. That difference is what this section is about.

An element is a substance that cannot be chemically broken down into simpler substances. It contains only one type of atom. Examples: copper, O2, diamond.

A compound is made of atoms of different elements, chemically bonded together in a fixed ratio. Examples: H2O, NaCl, CO2.

A mixture contains more than one element or compound, in no fixed ratio. The parts are not chemically bonded to each other, so they can be separated by physical methods. Examples: air, sea water.

A compound has different properties from the elements in it. A mixture keeps the properties of its parts. That is why a mixture can be separated by physical methods and a compound cannot.

Three kinds of matter
TypeExamplesCompositionPropertiesHow to separate the parts
Elementcopper, O2one type of atomits own fixed propertiescannot be broken down chemically
CompoundNaCl, CO2different elements, bonded, fixed ratiodifferent from its elementschemical reaction only
Mixtureair, sea waterno fixed ratio, not bonded to each othereach part keeps its ownphysical methods

Here is one example of “different properties”. Sodium is a soft metal that reacts violently with water. Chlorine is a toxic yellow-green gas. The compound sodium chloride is the white solid you put on food.

“Pure” in everyday speech means natural or clean. In chemistry a pure substance is a single element or a single compound. Pure orange juice is a mixture.

An element is not always single atoms. O2, N2 and Cl2 are molecules, but each contains only one type of atom, so each is an element.

Homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures

Stir salt into water and it disappears. Stir sand into water and you still see the sand. Both are mixtures, but they are different kinds.

A homogeneous mixture has the same composition and properties throughout. You cannot see separate parts. Solutions are homogeneous. So is air.

A heterogeneous mixture does not have the same composition throughout. It has separate regions with different properties. Sand in water and oil on water are heterogeneous.

A homogeneous mixture is not a compound. Salt water looks like one substance, but the salt and water are not chemically bonded in a fixed ratio. You can add more salt, and you can get the salt back by evaporating the water.

To “distinguish between an element and a compound”, give one clear statement about each. An element contains only one type of atom. A compound contains atoms of different elements chemically bonded in a fixed ratio. If a question asks for a physical property that differs, do not give a chemical property or a molar mass.

You do not need colloids, suspensions or emulsions as separate categories. Homogeneous and heterogeneous are enough. Why particular substances do or do not mix is explained with intermolecular forces later, in Structure 2.2.

Structure 1.1.1

Separating mixtures

Making coffee uses two separation techniques. Hot water dissolves the flavour compounds out of the ground beans. Then a paper filter holds back the grounds and lets the solution through.

Every separation works the same way. You find a physical property that is different for the parts of the mixture, and you pick a technique that uses that difference.

The six techniques named in the syllabus
TechniqueProperty that differsWhat it separatesWords to use
Solvation (dissolving)solubility in a chosen solventa soluble solid from an insoluble solid (first step)solute, solvent, solution
Filtrationparticle size: an undissolved solid cannot pass through filter paperan insoluble solid from a liquid or solutionresidue (on the paper), filtrate (passes through)

Solvation is the chemist’s word for dissolving: solvent particles surround the solute particles and carry them into the liquid. A liquid is volatile if it turns to vapour easily, which means it has a low boiling point.

How to choose a technique

  1. List what is in the mixture and the state of each part: solid, liquid or dissolved.
  2. Decide which part you want to keep.
  3. Find a physical property that is different: solubility, particle size, boiling point, or attraction to paper.
  4. Pick the technique that uses that difference. Use more than one step if you need to.
  5. Finish the job: dry a solid, or evaporate the solvent from a filtrate.

Getting dry sand and dry salt from a mixture of the two

A student has a dry mixture of sand and salt (sodium chloride). Outline how to obtain a dry sample of each solid.

  1. Every separation starts from a difference in a physical property.

    Salt is soluble in water. Sand is insoluble in water.

  2. Solvation moves the salt into the liquid and leaves the sand as a solid.

    Add distilled water to the mixture and stir until no more solid dissolves.

  3. The undissolved sand grains cannot pass through filter paper. The dissolved salt can.

    Pour the mixture through filter paper in a funnel. Sand is the residue. Salt solution is the filtrate.

  4. The sand is wet, and it has salt solution on it.

    Rinse the residue with a little distilled water, then leave it to dry.

  5. The salt is still dissolved. Water is volatile and salt is not.

    Heat the filtrate to evaporate the water. Leave it to crystallize, then dry the crystals.

Add water and stir, filter, rinse and dry the residue (sand), evaporate the water from the filtrate and dry the crystals (salt).

In a separation plan, each workable step in a sensible order earns a mark. The steps students forget are the last ones: drying the residue and evaporating the filtrate. Use any physical property the question gives you. For example, if one solid is magnetic, remove it with a magnet first.

Recrystallization, step by step

Recrystallization purifies a solid that contains small amounts of impurities. It works when the solid is very soluble in the hot solvent and much less soluble in the cold solvent.

  1. Dissolve the impure solid in the minimum volume of hot solvent.
  2. Filter the solution while it is hot. Insoluble impurities stay on the filter paper.
  3. Let the filtrate cool. The product is less soluble in cold solvent, so it forms crystals. Soluble impurities are present in small amounts, so they stay dissolved.
  4. Filter to collect the crystals.
  5. Rinse the crystals with a little cold solvent, then dry them.

Using plenty of hot solvent “to be safe” loses product. The more solvent there is, the more product stays dissolved after cooling, so fewer crystals form. The same idea explains the cold rinse: warm solvent would dissolve some of your crystals again.

Paper chromatography in practice

Draw the baseline in pencil, because pencil does not dissolve in the solvent. Put a small spot of the mixture on the baseline. Stand the paper in a little solvent, with the solvent level below the baseline. If the spot started under the solvent, the dyes would dissolve into the beaker instead of moving up the paper. A pure substance gives one spot. A mixture gives more than one.

You do not need retardation factor (Rf) calculations or the terms stationary phase and mobile phase in this topic. They come later, in Structure 2.2, together with thin layer chromatography. You also do not need the details of fractionating columns, centrifuges or separating funnels here.

Structure 1.1.2

The kinetic molecular theory and states of matter

An ice cube keeps its shape on a plate. When it melts, the water spreads out. When the water boils away, the steam fills the kitchen. It is the same substance, H2O, each time. What changes is how its particles are arranged and how they move.

The kinetic molecular theory is a model. It says that all matter is made of particles that are always moving, and that there are forces of attraction between them. With those two ideas you can explain the physical properties of solids, liquids and gases.

Solids, liquids and gases in the particle model
SolidLiquidGas

Spacingclose togetherclose togetherfar apart
Movementvibrate about fixed positionsmove past each othermove quickly in all directions
Attractions between particlesstrong enough to hold particles in placehold particles close, but not in placevery weak, because the particles are far apart

Link each property to the particles. Solids and liquids are hard to compress because their particles are already close together. Gases are easy to compress because there is a lot of empty space between the particles. Liquids and gases flow because their particles can move past each other.

The particles themselves do not expand, melt or get hot. When a substance is heated, its particles move faster and usually move further apart. Each particle stays the same size.

When you describe a state, cover three things: arrangement, spacing and movement. For example, for a liquid: “particles are close together, randomly arranged, and move past each other”.

You do not need other states such as plasma, or phase diagrams and triple points. Why one substance is a solid and another a gas at room temperature depends on the bonding and the forces between particles. That comes in Structure 2.

Structure 1.1.2

Changes of state and state symbols

A puddle dries up. A cold window mists over. Frost forms on a freezing night without any liquid water appearing first. Each of these is a change of state, and each has a name you need to know.

Names of the changes of state
ChangeNameEnergyExample
solid → liquidmeltingabsorbed (endothermic)ice melting in a drink
liquid → gasvaporization (evaporation or boiling)absorbed (endothermic)a puddle drying, a kettle boiling
solid → gassublimationabsorbed (endothermic)dry ice, CO2(s), turning to gas
gas → liquidcondensationreleased (exothermic)mist on a cold window
liquid → solidfreezingreleased (exothermic)water turning to ice
gas → soliddepositionreleased (exothermic)frost forming on cold glass

Why energy goes in or comes out

There are forces of attraction between particles. To melt, vaporize or sublime a substance, the particles must overcome some or all of those attractions. That needs energy, so these changes are endothermic: they absorb energy from the surroundings.

In the opposite changes, particles come closer together and attractions form between them. That releases energy to the surroundings, so condensation, freezing and deposition are exothermic.

Freezing releases energy. It feels wrong because freezing happens in cold places. But the water must lose energy to its colder surroundings in order to freeze. Energy leaves the water, so the change is exothermic.

Evaporation and boiling are both vaporization

Two ways for a liquid to become a gas
EvaporationBoiling
Where?at the surface onlythroughout the liquid (bubbles of vapour form)
At what temperature?any temperature below the boiling pointat the boiling point
Which particles?the faster particles at the surface escapeparticles all through the liquid

The bubbles in boiling water are not air, and they are not hydrogen and oxygen. They are water vapour, H2O(g). A change of state does not break the bonds inside the molecules. It only separates whole molecules from each other.

State symbols

A state symbol is written in brackets after a formula to show the state of the substance.

The four state symbols
SymbolMeaningExample
(s)solidNaCl(s), salt crystals
(l)pure liquidH2O(l), water; NaCl(l), molten salt
(g)gasCO2(g)
(aq)aqueous: dissolved in waterNaCl(aq), salt solution

Changes of state can be written as equations. The formula stays the same and only the state symbol changes:

H2O(s) -> H2O(l) (melting)    CO2(s) -> CO2(g) (sublimation)

A reaction equation with state symbols looks like this:

Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g)

Read it as: solid zinc reacts with hydrochloric acid solution to give zinc chloride solution and hydrogen gas.

(l) and (aq) are different. NaCl(l) is salt that has been melted, above 800 °C. NaCl(aq) is salt dissolved in water. Water itself is H2O(l), never H2O(aq).

State symbols are marked when the question asks for them, so include them whenever you are asked. Acids, alkalis and dissolved salts are (aq). Water as a product is (l). A solid that forms in a solution (a precipitate) is (s).

Structure 1.1.3

Temperature and the kelvin scale

Put a spoon in hot tea and the handle warms up. The fast-moving particles in the tea hit the particles of the spoon and pass energy on. “Hot” means the particles have, on average, more energy of movement.

Kinetic energy, Ek, is the energy a particle has because it is moving. In any sample some particles move slowly and some move fast, so we talk about the average.

Temperature, T, in kelvin (K) is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles.

The Celsius scale puts its zero at the freezing point of water. The kelvin scale puts its zero at absolute zero, the lowest possible temperature. Because it starts from a true zero, the temperature in kelvin is directly proportional to the average kinetic energy. If you double the temperature in kelvin, you double the average kinetic energy. This does not work in degrees Celsius.

The kelvin is the SI unit of temperature. One kelvin is the same size as one degree Celsius. Only the zero point is different, so you convert by adding or subtracting a fixed number.

T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15
T(K)
temperature on the kelvin scale K
T(°C)
temperature on the Celsius scale °C

The data booklet gives the conversion: temperature (K) = temperature (°C) + 273.15. It also gives STP as 273.15 K and 100 kPa.

Method in words

  1. Decide the direction. Going to kelvin, the number gets bigger: add 273.15. Going to degrees Celsius, the number gets smaller: subtract 273.15.
  2. Keep the sign of a negative Celsius value.
  3. Round to the same number of decimal places as the temperature you were given.
  4. Write the unit as K, with no degree sign.
  5. For a temperature change, do not add 273.15. The change is the same number in °C and in K.

Converting a boiling point to kelvin

Liquid oxygen boils at −182.96 °C. Give this temperature in kelvin.

  1. Starting from the formula shows your method.

    T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15

  2. The Celsius value is negative. Losing the minus sign is the most common slip.

    T = (−182.96) + 273.15 = 90.19 K

  3. When you add, the answer keeps the smallest number of decimal places in the data. Both values have two decimal places, so keep two.

    T = 90.19 K

  4. A kelvin value is always positive, and always bigger than the Celsius value.

    90.19 > −182.96, and 90.19 > 0

90.19 K

Converting kelvin to degrees Celsius

Ethanol boils at 351.52 K. Give this temperature in degrees Celsius.

  1. You want °C on its own, so subtract 273.15 from both sides.

    T(°C) = T(K) − 273.15

  2. Going from kelvin to °C, the number must get smaller.

    T = 351.52 − 273.15 = 78.37 °C

  3. Both values have two decimal places, so keep two.

    T = 78.37 °C

78.37 °C

A temperature change in °C and in K

Water is heated from 21.4 °C to 68.9 °C. Find the temperature change in °C and in K.

  1. A change is always final minus initial.

    ΔT = 68.9 °C − 21.4 °C = 47.5 °C

  2. This is the long way. It shows you what happens to the 273.15.

    initial: 21.4 + 273.15 = 294.55 K
    final: 68.9 + 273.15 = 342.05 K

  3. The 273.15 is added to both values, so it cancels when you subtract.

    ΔT = 342.05 K − 294.55 K = 47.5 K

  4. The scales have the same step size, so a change is the same number on both.

    ΔT = 47.5 °C = 47.5 K

47.5 °C, which is also 47.5 K

Adding 273.15 to a temperature change is wrong. A rise of 47.5 °C is a rise of 47.5 K, not 320.65 K. Add 273.15 only to a single temperature reading.

Write temperatures in kelvin as 298 K, with no degree sign. In gas calculations (Structure 1.5), always convert to kelvin before you substitute.

Normal human body temperature is about 37 °C. What is this in kelvin? Give your answer to 3 significant figures.

  1. Which is bigger, the number in kelvin or the number in °C? Decide before you calculate.
  2. Add 273.15 to the Celsius value: 37 + 273.15.
T = 37 + 273.15 = 310.15 K

To 3 significant figures: 310 K.

Liquid helium boils at 4.22 K. What is this in degrees Celsius? Give your answer to 2 decimal places.

  1. Going from kelvin to °C, the number gets smaller. Here it will be negative.
  2. Subtract 273.15 from the kelvin value: 4.22 − 273.15.
T = 4.22 − 273.15 = −268.93 °C

The answer is negative because 4.22 K is far below the freezing point of water.

A solution warms from −5.0 °C to 21.5 °C. What is the temperature change in kelvin?

  1. This is a change, not a single reading. Do you need 273.15?
  2. Change = final − initial: 21.5 − (−5.0). Subtracting a negative number means adding.
ΔT = 21.5 − (−5.0) = 26.5 °C = 26.5 K

A temperature change is the same number in °C and in K.

You do not need a formula that links kinetic energy to temperature or to particle speed. You need the idea only: temperature in kelvin is a measure of average kinetic energy. The graph of how kinetic energies are spread out among the particles comes later, in Reactivity 2.2.

Structure 1.1.3

What happens to temperature during a change of state

Put a thermometer in a pan of boiling water and keep the heat on. The reading stays at 100 °C until the water has boiled away. Energy is going in all the time, but the temperature is not rising. Where is the energy going?

A heating curve shows the temperature of a substance as energy is supplied to it at a steady rate. It has sloping parts and flat parts.

  • Sloping parts: one state is being heated. The energy supplied increases the average kinetic energy of the particles, so the temperature rises.
  • Flat parts: the state is changing. The energy supplied is used to overcome the forces of attraction between the particles. This increases their potential energy. Their average kinetic energy does not change, so the temperature stays constant.

During a change of state, the temperature stays constant even though energy is still being supplied or removed. The first flat part is at the melting point. The second is at the boiling point.

How to read a heating or cooling curve

  1. Find the flat parts. On a heating curve the lower one is the melting point and the higher one is the boiling point.
  2. Name the state on each sloping part: solid below the melting point, liquid between, gas above the boiling point.
  3. On a flat part, two states are present together.
  4. To find the state at a given temperature, compare it with the melting point and the boiling point.

Finding the state from melting and boiling points

Bromine melts at 266 K and boils at 332 K. What is the state of bromine at 25 °C?

  1. You cannot compare a temperature in °C with temperatures in K.

    T = 25 + 273.15 = 298.15 K

  2. Above the melting point, the substance is not a solid.

    298.15 K > 266 K, so bromine has melted

  3. Below the boiling point, the substance has not boiled.

    298.15 K < 332 K, so bromine has not boiled

  4. Between the melting point and the boiling point, a substance is a liquid.

    Bromine is a liquid at 25 °C: Br2(l)

Liquid

“The temperature is constant, so no energy is being absorbed” is wrong. Energy is still going in. It is increasing the potential energy of the particles as they move apart, not their average kinetic energy.

“Explain why the temperature stays constant during melting” is usually worth 2 marks. Mark 1: the energy is used to overcome the forces of attraction between the particles. Mark 2: the average kinetic energy of the particles does not change. Write both.

You do not need to calculate the energy for any part of a heating curve in this topic. Calculations with specific heat capacity come later, in Reactivity 1.1. The numbers on the graph above are there only to make the scale honest.

Linking it up

The syllabus asks questions that connect this topic to others. Short answers to four of them:

What factors are considered in choosing a method to separate the components of a mixture?

The states of the components, a physical property that differs between them (solubility, particle size, boiling point), and which component you want to keep. For example, evaporation and distillation both use volatility, but only distillation keeps the liquid.

Why are alloys generally considered to be mixtures, even though they often contain metallic bonding?

An alloy does not have a fixed ratio of elements. Brass can be made with different proportions of copper and zinc. A compound always has a fixed ratio. The full picture of bonding in alloys comes in Structure 2.3.

Why are some changes of state endothermic and some exothermic?

Melting, vaporization and sublimation need energy to overcome attractions between particles, so they are endothermic. Freezing, condensation and deposition form attractions between particles, which releases energy, so they are exothermic. Energy changes are studied in Reactivity 1.

What is the graphical distribution of kinetic energy values of particles in a sample at a fixed temperature?

At any one temperature the particles do not all have the same kinetic energy. A few have very little, a few have a lot, and most are in between. Temperature measures the average. The shape of this distribution is covered in Reactivity 2.2.
