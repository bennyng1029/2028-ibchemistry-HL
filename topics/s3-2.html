Structure 3.2

Functional groups: classification of organic compounds

Guiding questionHow does the classification of organic molecules help us to predict their properties?

What you need to be able to do

Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds. There are millions of them. This page shows you how chemists draw them, sort them into families and name them.

You do not need to know any organic chemistry before you start. Tick each item when you can do it without looking at your notes.

  • Identify empirical, molecular, full structural, condensed structural, skeletal and stereochemical formulas.
  • Convert between molecular, skeletal and structural formulas.
  • Build or read a 3-D model of an organic molecule.
  • Identify these functional groups by name and by structure: halogeno, hydroxyl, carbonyl, carboxyl, alkoxy, amino, amido, ester and phenyl.
  • Use the words saturated and unsaturated correctly.
  • Describe a homologous series and use its general formula.
  • Identify alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, halogenoalkanes, alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, ethers, amines, amides and esters.
  • Describe and explain the trend in melting point and boiling point along a homologous series.
  • Name compounds with a parent chain of up to six carbon atoms that contain one type of group: halogeno, hydroxyl, carbonyl or carboxyl. Include branched chains and compounds with one double or triple bond.
  • Recognize structural isomers: straight-chain and branched, position, and functional group isomers.
  • Classify alcohols, halogenoalkanes and amines as primary, secondary or tertiary.

This page sorts, draws and names organic compounds. What the compounds do (their reactions and mechanisms) comes later, in Reactivity 3. Stereoisomers and spectroscopy are Additional HL and have their own page.

Structure 3.2.1

Six ways to write one molecule

Think of a house. You can give its address, show a floor plan, or show a photograph. Each one is correct. Each one tells you something different. Chemists do the same with molecules.

First: how many bonds each atom makes

Organic molecules are held together by covalent bonds. In almost every organic molecule you will meet, each kind of atom makes a fixed number of bonds.

Number of covalent bonds formed in organic molecules
AtomBondsWhat that looks like
carbon, C4four single bonds, or a double bond and two single bonds, or a triple bond and one single bond
nitrogen, N3three single bonds, for example
oxygen, O2two single bonds, or one double bond
hydrogen, H1one single bond
halogens: F, Cl, Br, I1one single bond

Carbon always forms four bonds. A double bond counts as two and a triple bond counts as three. Almost everything on this page follows from that one fact.

The empirical formula and the molecular formula can be different. Ethanoic acid has the molecular formula C2H4O2. Divide every number by 2 and you get its empirical formula, CH2O. You met empirical formulas in Structure 1.4.

If a question asks for a structural formula, a molecular formula such as C3H8O does not earn the mark. Show how the atoms are joined. If it asks for a full structural formula, draw every bond, including each C–H and the O–H.

How to read a condensed structural formula

Read it from left to right. Each carbon is written with the hydrogen atoms that are bonded to it. Then comes the next carbon in the chain.

  • CH3CH2CH3 is a chain of three carbons: CH3, then CH2, then CH3.
  • Brackets show a group that hangs off the chain. In CH3CH(CH3)CH3 the CH3 in brackets is a branch on the second carbon.
  • Brackets with a number mean several copies of the same group on one atom. (CH3)3COH has three CH3 groups on the carbon that carries the OH.
  • Some groups have a standard short form. COOH is a carbon with a double bond to one O and a single bond to an O–H. CHO is a carbon with one H and a double bond to O. CO between two carbons is a carbon with a double bond to O.
  • A double bond between carbons is usually shown: CH2=CHCH3.

How to read a skeletal formula

A skeletal formula is the fastest one to draw, and exam papers use it often. It leaves out the letters C and H. You have to put them back in your head. There are four rules.

  1. Every line is a bond between two atoms. A double line is a double bond.
  2. Every line end and every corner is a carbon atom, unless another symbol is written there.
  3. Each carbon has enough hydrogen atoms to make four bonds in total. These hydrogens are not drawn.
  4. Atoms that are not carbon or hydrogen are always written. A hydrogen bonded to one of them is written too, as in OH or NH2.

butane, CH3CH2CH2CH3: four carbons, so three lines

2-methylpropane: the middle carbon has three lines, so it has one H

but-2-ene: each carbon of the C=C has one H

ethanoic acid, CH3COOH

Structure 3.2.2

Functional groups and classes of compounds

Ethanol and propan-1-ol have different sizes, but they behave in very similar ways. Both mix with water. Both burn easily. The reason is one small part that they share: an O–H joined to a carbon atom.

A functional group is an atom or a group of atoms that gives a compound its characteristic physical and chemical properties. The rest of the molecule, the chain of carbon and hydrogen, changes those properties only a little.

Chemists sort organic compounds into classes according to the functional group in their molecules. In general formulas the letter R stands for the rest of the molecule: any group made of carbon and hydrogen, such as CH3 or CH3CH2.

Three classes have no group from this list. Alkanes have only C–C single bonds and C–H bonds. Alkenes have a carbon–carbon double bond, C=C. Alkynes have a carbon–carbon triple bond, C≡C. That makes twelve classes in total.

The benzene ring is drawn here with alternating single and double lines. The real bonding in benzene is described in Structure 2.2 (Additional HL). For this page, you only need to recognize the ring and call it phenyl.

The name of the group and the name of the class are two different words. The group in ethanol is hydroxyl; the class is alcohol. The group in ethanal is carbonyl; the class is aldehyde. The group in ethanoic acid is carboxyl; the class is carboxylic acid.

Read the question word by word. “State the name of the functional group” wants hydroxyl, carbonyl, carboxyl or phenyl. Answers such as alcohol, aldehyde, carboxylic acid or “benzene ring” have lost this mark. “State the class” or “the homologous series” wants alcohol, aldehyde or carboxylic acid.

Look at the whole group

Some groups contain smaller ones. Always take the largest group you can see.

  • C=O with an O–H on the same carbon is carboxyl. It is not “carbonyl and hydroxyl”.
  • C=O with an O–R on the same carbon is ester. It is not “carbonyl and alkoxy”.
  • C=O with an N on the same carbon is amido. It is not “carbonyl and amino”.

Saturated and unsaturated

A saturated compound has only single bonds between its carbon atoms. An unsaturated compound has at least one carbon–carbon double bond or triple bond.

Alkanes are saturated. Alkenes and alkynes are unsaturated. The words describe the carbon–carbon bonds only: ethanol and propanone are both saturated.

Finding every group in a larger molecule

Paracetamol is a painkiller. Identify the functional groups in its molecule.

  1. Functional groups are built around these atoms, and around rings and multiple bonds.

    There are two oxygen atoms and one nitrogen atom. There is also a six-carbon ring with alternating double bonds.

  2. The neighbours decide which group it is.

    One O carries an H and is bonded to a ring carbon. The other O is double-bonded to a carbon. That same carbon is bonded to the N.

  3. C=O and N on the same carbon are one group, not two.

    O–H is a hydroxyl group. C=O with N on the same carbon is an amido group. The ring is a phenyl group.

  4. The question asked for groups, so do not answer “alcohol” or “amide”.

    Hydroxyl, amido and phenyl.

Hydroxyl, amido and phenyl.

Textbooks and websites list many more groups, such as nitrile, nitro, acyl chloride, acid anhydride and thiol. They are not in the IB guide. Phenols and arenes are not classes you have to learn either. Stop at the nine groups and twelve classes above.

Structure 3.2.3

Homologous series: families of compounds

Methane, ethane, propane and butane are like the steps of a staircase. Each step is one CH2 higher than the one before.

A homologous series is a family of compounds in which each member differs from the next by a common structural unit, usually CH2. Each series can be described by a general formula.

The members of one homologous series:

  • have the same functional group;
  • fit the same general formula, a formula written with n for the number of carbon atoms;
  • differ from the next member by one CH2;
  • show a gradual trend in physical properties, such as boiling point;
  • have similar chemical properties, because they have the same functional group.

In these formulas n is the total number of carbon atoms in the molecule, and X is a halogen atom. The formulas are for molecules with one functional group, no rings and no other double bonds.

Not every series starts at n = 1. An alkene needs two carbons for its C=C, so the first alkene is ethene. A ketone needs a carbon on each side of the C=O, so the first ketone is propanone, with three carbons.

Some general formulas are shared. Aldehydes and ketones are both CnH2nO. Carboxylic acids and esters are both CnH2nO2. Alcohols and ethers are both CnH2n+2O. So a molecular formula alone cannot tell you the class. This matters for isomers, later on this page.

The guide does not list the general formulas, but questions do ask for one, most often for alkanes, alkenes, alkynes or alcohols. You can always rebuild a general formula from two members of the series. Write their molecular formulas and see what each extra carbon adds.

Structure 3.2.4

Trends in boiling point and melting point

Methane is the gas in a kitchen stove. Octane is a liquid in petrol. Candle wax is a mixture of solid alkanes with more than 20 carbon atoms. They are all alkanes. The only thing that changes is the length of the chain.

Along a homologous series, the boiling point rises as the carbon chain gets longer. Melting points rise too.

Why the boiling point rises

When a liquid boils, its molecules move apart. The covalent bonds inside each molecule do not break. Only the weak forces between the molecules are overcome. These are the intermolecular forces from Structure 2.2.

Every molecule attracts its neighbours by London (dispersion) forces. A longer chain has more electrons, and a larger surface that touches its neighbours. So the London forces are stronger. More energy is needed to separate the molecules, and the boiling point is higher.

The functional group is the same in every member, so the forces it causes stay about the same. The part that grows is the carbon chain.

Two points earn the marks. First, name the force: London (dispersion) forces. Second, say why they are stronger: more electrons, or greater molar mass, or a larger surface area, as the chain gets longer. “Stronger bonds” without naming the force earns nothing.

Do not write that the covalent bonds in a larger molecule are harder to break. Boiling does not break covalent bonds. Ethane gas is still made of C2H6 molecules.

Melting points

Melting points also rise overall along a series, for the same reason. The rise is less regular, because melting also depends on how well the molecules pack together in the solid. Choose “Melting point (alkanes)” in the graph above to see this.

Different functional groups, similar size

You can only compare chain length fairly inside one series. Between series, the functional group matters more. The table compares molecules of nearly the same molar mass.

Branching

A branched molecule is more compact than a straight-chain molecule with the same formula. It has less surface in contact with its neighbours. The London forces are weaker, so the boiling point is lower.

Explaining a trend in an exam answer

The boiling points of methanol, ethanol and propan-1-ol are 65 °C, 78 °C and 97 °C. Explain this trend. [2 marks]

  1. This shows you know which force is not the cause of the change.

    All three molecules have one hydroxyl group, so each can form hydrogen bonds in the same way.

  2. Each member has one more CH2 than the one before.

    From methanol to propan-1-ol the molecules have more electrons (a greater molar mass and a larger surface area).

  3. The mark is for the named force, linked to the energy needed.

    So the London (dispersion) forces between the molecules become stronger. More energy is needed to separate the molecules, so the boiling point increases.

London (dispersion) forces become stronger [1] because the molecules have more electrons / greater molar mass / larger surface area as the chain length increases [1].

Butane boils at 0 °C, pentane at 36 °C and hexane at 69 °C. Estimate the boiling point of heptane, the next alkane. An answer within 6 °C of the measured value counts as correct.

  1. Find how much the boiling point rises for each extra CH2.
  2. The rises are 36 °C and then 33 °C. The steps are getting a little smaller, so add about 30 °C to 69 °C.

The steps are +36 °C and +33 °C. A sensible next step is about +30 °C, which gives about 99 °C. The measured boiling point of heptane is 98 °C.

Structure 3.2.5

IUPAC names

A postal address is built from fixed parts in a fixed order, so that it points to exactly one house. A chemical name works in the same way. If you know the rules, you can turn any name into a structure, and any structure into a name.

IUPAC nomenclature is the set of rules for naming compounds. IUPAC is the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry. The rules give each compound a systematic name that chemists everywhere can read.

You name compounds that have: a parent chain of one to six carbon atoms; only single bonds between carbons, or one C=C or C≡C; and one type of group out of halogeno, hydroxyl, carbonyl and carboxyl. The chain can be straight or branched.

That means alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, halogenoalkanes, alcohols, aldehydes, ketones and carboxylic acids. You must recognize ethers, amines, amides and esters as classes, but the guide does not ask you to name them. Their names may be given to you in a question. You also do not name compounds with two different functional groups, or with rings.

The three parts of a name

  • prefix – what is attached to the chain, and where
  • stem – how many carbons are in the parent chain
  • ending – the type of carbon–carbon bonds, and the functional group

For example, in 2-methylbutan-1-ol the prefix is 2-methyl, the stem is but, and the ending is an-1-ol.

The letters before the ending tell you about the carbon–carbon bonds: -an- means all single, -en- means one C=C, and -yn- means one C≡C. So but-2-enoic acid is a four-carbon carboxylic acid with a C=C that starts at carbon 2.

Method in words

  1. Find the parent chain. It is the longest chain of carbon atoms that contains the carbon of the functional group, and the C=C or C≡C if there is one. The chain may bend. Its length gives the stem.
  2. Number the chain. Start from the end that gives the lowest number to the group named by the ending. If there is no such group, give the lowest number to the C=C or C≡C. If there is none of those, give the lowest numbers to the attached groups.
  3. Name what is attached. Each branch or halogen gets a prefix and the number of the carbon it is on. Use di- for two of the same kind and tri- for three. Put different prefixes in alphabetical order (ignore di- and tri- when you sort).
  4. Put the name together. Prefix, stem, ending. Write commas between numbers and hyphens between a number and a letter.

Two more rules save time. The carbon of an aldehyde group or a carboxyl group is always carbon 1, so its number is never written: propanal, not propan-1-al. And a number is left out when only one position is possible: ethanol, propene, propanone, butanone.

The longest chain is not always drawn in a straight line. Trace every path through the carbons before you choose. A “2-ethyl” branch usually means you missed a longer chain. For example, “2-ethylbutane” is really 3-methylpentane.

Drawing a structure from a name

Draw the structural formula of 3-methylbutan-2-ol.

  1. A name is easiest to read from the stem outwards.

    “but” means four carbons. “an” means only single bonds between them. Draw four carbons in a row and number them 1 to 4.

  2. The number before the ending says which carbon carries the group.

    “-2-ol” means an O–H on carbon 2.

  3. Each prefix has its own number.

    “3-methyl” means a CH3 group on carbon 3.

  4. This turns the carbon skeleton into a complete structural formula.

  5. Name your own drawing again. You should get the name you started with.

    Longest chain through the OH carbon: 4. OH on carbon 2, not 3. Branch on carbon 3. The name is 3-methylbutan-2-ol, and the formula is CH3CH(OH)CH(CH3)CH3.

How many carbon atoms are in one molecule of 3-ethyl-2-methylpentane?

  1. Add the carbons of the parent chain and the carbons of each branch.
  2. Pentane has 5. An ethyl group has 2. A methyl group has 1.
C: 5 + 2 + 1 = 8

It is an alkane, so its formula is C8H18. The parent chain still has only five carbons, so the name is inside the limits.

In the IUPAC name of CH3CH2CH(OH)CH2CH2CH3, which number does the carbon with the OH group get?

  1. The chain has six carbons. Number it from each end in turn.
  2. From the left the OH is on carbon 3. From the right it is on carbon 4. Take the lower number.

The lower number is 3, so the compound is hexan-3-ol.

Markschemes have accepted names with a missing hyphen, and prefixes that are not in alphabetical order. A wrong parent chain, a wrong number or a wrong ending is not accepted. Write the name in the standard form anyway, so that it cannot be misread.

Older books write the number in front of the whole name, as in “2-butanol”. Many everyday names are also still used, such as acetone, acetic acid, isopropyl and tert-butyl. You do not need them. You also do not need E/Z or cis–trans labels here: on this page but-2-ene is a complete name.

Structure 3.2.6

Structural isomers

With the same set of building blocks you can build different models. Atoms are the same. Four carbon atoms and ten hydrogen atoms can be joined in two different ways.

butane, boiling point 0 °C

2-methylpropane, boiling point −12 °C

Structural isomers are molecules that have the same molecular formula but different connectivities. Connectivity means which atom is bonded to which. Structural isomers are different compounds, with different names and different properties.

Three types to recognize

Types of structural isomers

Pairs of classes that share a general formula give functional group isomers: alcohols and ethers, aldehydes and ketones, carboxylic acids and esters.

To decide the type, ask three questions in order. Are the functional groups the same? If not, they are functional group isomers. If yes, is the carbon chain the same? If not, they are chain isomers. If yes, only the position of the group is different.

Bending a chain or turning a drawing over does not make a new isomer. The atoms are still joined in the same order. To test two drawings, name them both. Same name means same molecule.

Finding every structural isomer of C4H10O

Deduce the number of structural isomers with the molecular formula C4H10O, and draw them.

  1. A system stops you from missing isomers and from counting one twice.

    The formula fits CnH2n+2O, so there are no double bonds and no rings. One O with only single bonds is either a hydroxyl group (an alcohol) or an alkoxy group (an ether).

  2. Four carbons can be joined in only two ways.

    A straight chain of four carbons, or a chain of three with a one-carbon branch in the middle.

  3. Carbons that are equivalent by symmetry give the same compound.

    The straight chain has two different positions: an end carbon or an inner carbon. The branched chain also has two: one of the three equivalent CH3 carbons, or the central carbon. That makes 4 alcohols.

    butan-1-ol

    butan-2-ol

    2-methylpropan-1-ol

    2-methylpropan-2-ol

  4. The O must sit between two carbons.

    One carbon and three carbons: the three-carbon group can be joined to the O by its end carbon or by its middle carbon, which gives 2 ethers. Two carbons and two carbons gives 1 more. That makes 3 ethers.

    CH3OCH2CH2CH3

    CH3OCH(CH3)CH3

    CH3CH2OCH2CH3

  5. Name each alcohol. If two have the same name, they are the same compound.

    4 alcohols + 3 ethers = 7 structural isomers

7 structural isomers: 4 alcohols and 3 ethers.

How many structural isomers have the molecular formula C4H9Br?

  1. Draw the two possible four-carbon chains first.
  2. On each chain, put the Br on every carbon that is in a different position. Each chain gives two.

1-bromobutane, 2-bromobutane, 1-bromo-2-methylpropane and 2-bromo-2-methylpropane: 4 isomers.

How many structural isomers have the molecular formula C5H12?

  1. Start with the straight chain. Then shorten the longest chain by one carbon and attach that carbon as a branch.
  2. A chain of four with one branch gives one isomer. A chain of three with two branches gives one more.

Pentane, 2-methylbutane and 2,2-dimethylpropane: 3 isomers. “3-methylbutane” is 2-methylbutane numbered from the wrong end.

When you are asked to draw an isomer, a molecular formula does not earn the mark: draw a structural formula. Read any condition in the question, such as “an isomer that is not an ester”. Then check that your answer has the same molecular formula as the compound you were given.

Rings can also be isomers of open-chain compounds. For example, cyclobutane has the same formula as the butenes, C4H8. You may see such answers accepted, but you are not asked to name ring compounds here. Isomers that differ only in their 3-D arrangement (stereoisomers) are Additional HL.

Primary, secondary and tertiary

Position isomers such as propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol have the same functional group, but they do not react in exactly the same way. What matters is how crowded the group is. Chemists describe this with three words.

Alcohols and halogenoalkanes: count on the carbon

Find the carbon atom that is bonded to the OH group or to the halogen. Count how many other carbon atoms are bonded directly to that carbon.

Classifying three compounds

Classify each compound as primary, secondary or tertiary: (a) (CH3)3CBr, (b) CH3CH2CH(OH)CH3, (c) CH3NHCH2CH3.

  1. For a halogenoalkane, you count on the carbon that carries the halogen.

    That carbon is bonded to three CH3 groups. Three carbons: tertiary halogenoalkane.

  2. For an alcohol, you count on the carbon that carries the OH.

    That carbon is bonded to a CH2 on one side and a CH3 on the other. Two carbons: secondary alcohol.

  3. For an amine, you count on the N, not on a carbon.

    The N is bonded to a CH3 carbon, a CH2 carbon and one H. Two carbons: secondary amine.

(a) tertiary, (b) secondary, (c) secondary.

In 2-methylbutan-2-ol, CH3CH2C(CH3)(OH)CH3, how many carbon atoms are bonded directly to the carbon that carries the OH group?

  1. Find the carbon written just before (CH3)(OH). It carries the OH.
  2. Its neighbours are the CH2 before it, the CH3 in brackets and the CH3 after it.

Three carbon atoms, so 2-methylbutan-2-ol is a tertiary alcohol.

Why does this matter? Primary, secondary and tertiary alcohols give different products when they are oxidized (Reactivity 3.2). Primary and tertiary halogenoalkanes react by different mechanisms (Reactivity 3.4). Here you only need to classify them.

Linking it up

What is unique about carbon that enables it to form more compounds than all the other elements together?

A carbon atom forms four covalent bonds. It bonds strongly to other carbon atoms, so it can make long chains, branched chains and rings. It also forms strong bonds to hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and the halogens, and it can form double and triple bonds. Each new arrangement is a new compound. Isomers multiply the number again.

What are the advantages and disadvantages of the different ways of drawing an organic compound?

A full structural formula shows every atom and bond, so nothing can be misread, but it is slow to draw and its 90° angles are not real. A skeletal formula is fast and shows the shape of the carbon chain and the functional groups clearly, but you must work out the hidden carbon and hydrogen atoms. A stereochemical formula shows the 3-D arrangement around one atom, but it becomes crowded for a large molecule. A 3-D model shows the true shape and can be turned, but it is hard to put on paper. No single picture shows everything, so chemists choose the one that fits the question.

How do chain length, branching and the functional group affect intermolecular forces?

A longer chain has more electrons and a larger surface, so London (dispersion) forces are stronger. Branching makes a molecule more compact, so less of its surface touches its neighbours and the London forces are weaker. A polar functional group such as C=O adds dipole–dipole forces. A group with O–H or N–H allows hydrogen bonding. Stronger forces between molecules mean a higher boiling point.

How can functional groups be used to plan a route from one compound to another?

Each functional group has its own typical reactions, and many of them turn one group into another. So a chemist can plan a route step by step, group by group. For example, ethene can be converted into ethanol, and ethanol into ethanoic acid. The reactions themselves come later, in Reactivity 3.2 and 3.4.
