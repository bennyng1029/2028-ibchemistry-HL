The covalent model

Structure 2.2.1

Covalent bonds, the octet rule and Lewis formulas

Two hydrogen atoms meet. Each has one electron and each needs one more. Neither atom is strong enough to take an electron from the other. So they share.

The two shared electrons sit between the two nuclei. Each nucleus is positive. The shared electrons are negative. Both nuclei are attracted to the same pair of electrons, and this holds the atoms together.

A covalent bond is the electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of electrons and the positively charged nuclei.

Covalent bonds usually form between atoms of non-metals. A group of atoms joined by covalent bonds is a molecule.

“State what a covalent bond is” is a one-mark question. The answer needs three parts: electrostatic attraction, a shared pair of electrons, and the (positively charged) nuclei.

“A covalent bond is the sharing of electrons” does not earn the mark. Sharing is how the bond forms. The bond itself is the attraction between the shared pair and the nuclei.

The octet rule

The electrons in the outer shell of an atom are its valence electrons. For the elements in this topic, the number of valence electrons is the last digit of the group number.

The octet rule is the tendency of atoms to gain a valence shell with a total of 8 electrons. Atoms can reach 8 by sharing electrons.

Hydrogen is different. Its first shell is full with 2 electrons, so a hydrogen atom forms one bond only.

The octet rule is a useful guide. It is not a law. Two atoms often have fewer than an octet in their compounds: beryllium (4 electrons) and boron (6 electrons).

Lewis formulas

A Lewis formula shows all the valence electrons in a molecule or ion. It is also called a Lewis structure or an electron dot structure.

There are two kinds of electron pair in a Lewis formula.

  • A bonding pair is shared between two atoms.
  • A non-bonding pair, or lone pair, belongs to one atom only.

You may draw each pair as two dots, two crosses or one line. On this site a line is a bonding pair and two dots are a lone pair.

Method in words

  1. Count all the valence electrons. For a negative ion add one electron for each charge. For a positive ion take one away for each charge.
  2. Choose the central atom. It is usually the atom that there is only one of. Hydrogen is never central.
  3. Join each outer atom to the central atom with a single bond. Each bond uses 2 electrons.
  4. Give each outer atom lone pairs until it has 8 electrons. Hydrogen gets none.
  5. Put any electrons that are left on the central atom.
  6. Check the central atom. If it has fewer than 8, move a lone pair from an outer atom into the bond. This makes a double or triple bond. Do not do this for Be or B.
  7. For an ion, draw square brackets around the formula and write the charge outside.
  8. Count the electrons in your drawing. The total must equal the number from step 1.

A Lewis formula with lone pairs on every atom

Deduce the Lewis formula of nitrogen trifluoride, NF3.

  1. The total tells you how many electrons your drawing must show.

    N: 1 × 5 = 5
    F: 3 × 7 = 21
    total = 5 + 21 = 26 electrons = 13 pairs

  2. There is only one nitrogen atom, so it is the central atom.

    3 bonds use 3 × 2 = 6 electrons. Left: 26 − 6 = 20

  3. Each fluorine has 2 electrons from its bond. It needs 3 lone pairs to reach 8.

    3 F atoms × 3 lone pairs × 2 = 18 electrons. Left: 20 − 18 = 2

  4. The last 2 electrons become one lone pair on nitrogen.

  5. Nitrogen has 3 bonding pairs and 1 lone pair: 8 electrons. Each fluorine has 8. The drawing shows 3 bonds and 10 lone pairs.

    3 × 2 + 10 × 2 = 26 electrons, the same as step 1

N in the centre with three N–F single bonds and one lone pair. Three lone pairs on each F.

A Lewis formula with double bonds

Deduce the Lewis formula of carbon dioxide, CO2.

  1. Carbon is in group 14 and oxygen is in group 16.

    C: 1 × 4 = 4
    O: 2 × 6 = 12
    total = 4 + 12 = 16 electrons = 8 pairs

  2. There is one carbon atom, so carbon is central.

    2 bonds use 4 electrons. Left: 16 − 4 = 12

  3. Each oxygen takes 3 lone pairs. That uses all 12 electrons, so none are left for carbon.

  4. Carbon has only 2 bonding pairs, which is 4 electrons. It needs 8.

    carbon: 2 × 2 = 4 electrons (not enough)

  5. Move one lone pair from each oxygen into its bond. No electrons are added. They are only moved.

  6. Carbon has 4 bonding pairs: 8 electrons. Each oxygen has 2 bonding pairs and 2 lone pairs: 8 electrons.

    4 bonding pairs + 4 lone pairs = 8 pairs = 16 electrons

O=C=O, with two lone pairs on each oxygen atom.

A Lewis formula of an ion

Deduce the Lewis formula of the hydronium ion, H3O+.

  1. The ion has a charge of 1+. It has lost one electron, so take one away.

    O: 1 × 6 = 6
    H: 3 × 1 = 3
    charge 1+: − 1
    total = 6 + 3 − 1 = 8 electrons = 4 pairs

  2. Hydrogen is never central, so oxygen is.

    3 bonds use 6 electrons. Left: 8 − 6 = 2

  3. Hydrogen atoms take no lone pairs. The last 2 electrons go on oxygen as one lone pair.

    oxygen: 3 bonding pairs + 1 lone pair = 8 electrons

  4. The charge belongs to the whole ion. In an exam the charge must be shown.

  5. The ion forms when a water molecule bonds to an H+ ion. An H+ ion has no electrons at all.

    H2O + H+ -> H3O+

    So both electrons in the new O–H bond came from a lone pair on oxygen. A bond like this is a coordination bond. You will meet it in section 2.2.3.

O in the centre with three O–H bonds and one lone pair, in square brackets with the charge +.

Atoms with fewer than an octet

Beryllium has 2 valence electrons and boron has 3. In BeCl2 and BF3 all of these are used in single bonds. No electrons are left for the central atom.

Organic examples

Carbon forms four bonds. It can reach 8 electrons with single, double or triple bonds.

How many valence electrons must the Lewis formula of phosphorus trichloride, PCl3, show?

  1. Phosphorus is in group 15 and chlorine is in group 17.
  2. Add 5 for the one P atom and 7 for each of the three Cl atoms.
P: 1 × 5 = 5
Cl: 3 × 7 = 21
total = 5 + 21 = 26 electrons

How many valence electrons must the Lewis formula of the carbonate ion, CO3^2-, show?

  1. Count the atoms first: C has 4 and each O has 6.
  2. The charge is 2−. A negative ion has gained electrons, so add 2.
C: 1 × 4 = 4
O: 3 × 6 = 18
charge 2−: + 2
total = 4 + 18 + 2 = 24 electrons

The most common lost mark is a missing lone pair on an outer atom, such as the three lone pairs on each F or Cl. The second is a missing charge on an ion. Always finish by counting the electrons in your drawing.

A Lewis formula earns its mark only if it is completely correct: every bonding pair and every lone pair on every atom. Dots, crosses and lines are all accepted, and you may mix them.

Atoms with more than 8 valence electrons (five or six pairs) are Additional HL. They are on the HL page. Molecules with an unpaired electron (radicals) come later, in Reactivity 3.3.

Structure 2.2.2

Single, double and triple bonds

Two atoms can share more than one pair of electrons.

  • A single bond is one shared pair.
  • A double bond is two shared pairs.
  • A triple bond is three shared pairs.

Bond length and bond strength

The bond length is the distance between the two nuclei. The bond strength tells you how much energy is needed to break the bond. It is measured as a bond enthalpy.

Think of the shared electrons as glue between the two nuclei. More shared pairs mean more negative charge between the nuclei. Both nuclei are attracted more strongly to it.

More shared pairs give a stronger attraction between the nuclei and the shared electrons. The nuclei are pulled closer together. So the bond is shorter and stronger: triple, then double, then single.

The triple bond has three shared pairs, so the nuclei are pulled closer.

To explain why a triple bond is shorter or stronger, write about the electrons: “more shared pairs of electrons, so greater attraction between the nuclei and the shared electrons”. Then give the result: shorter bond, stronger bond.

The term “bond order” is not in the IB guide. “One, two or three shared pairs” is enough. Calculations with bond enthalpies come later, in Reactivity 1.2.

Structure 2.2.3

Coordination bonds

In most covalent bonds each atom gives one electron to the shared pair. Sometimes one atom gives both.

A coordination bond is a covalent bond in which both electrons of the shared pair originate from the same atom.

The atom that gives the pair must have a lone pair. The atom that accepts the pair must have room for two more electrons.

Three examples to know

The ammonium ion. Ammonia has a lone pair on nitrogen. An H+ ion has no electrons. Nitrogen shares its lone pair with H+.

NH3 + H+ -> NH4+

The hydronium ion. Water has two lone pairs on oxygen. One of them is shared with an H+ ion.

H2O + H+ -> H3O+

Carbon monoxide. Carbon has 4 valence electrons and oxygen has 6. In CO the two atoms share three pairs, and each atom also keeps one lone pair. Carbon gives one electron to each of two shared pairs. Oxygen does the same. The third shared pair comes entirely from oxygen.

Once it has formed, a coordination bond is the same as any other covalent bond. In NH4+ all four N–H bonds have the same length and strength.

How to identify a coordination bond

  1. Draw the Lewis formula.
  2. For each atom, count the bonds it has formed.
  3. Compare with the number of bonds that atom usually forms: H 1, C 4, N 3, O 2, F and Cl 1.
  4. An atom with one bond more than usual, such as N with four bonds or O with three bonds, has given a lone pair. An atom that started with fewer than an octet, such as B in BF3, can accept a pair.

Another example: ammonia reacts with boron trifluoride. Boron has only 6 electrons and nitrogen has a lone pair. The new N–B bond in H3NBF3 is a coordination bond, and boron now has 8 electrons.

How many of the four N–H bonds in one ammonium ion, NH4+, formed as a coordination bond?

  1. Think about how the ion forms from NH3 and H+.
  2. Three N–H bonds were already in the ammonia molecule. Only the new bond used nitrogen’s lone pair.

One. Ammonia already has three normal N–H bonds. The fourth forms when nitrogen shares its lone pair with H+, so both electrons come from nitrogen.

The definition mark needs “both electrons (of the shared pair) come from the same atom”. If you are asked to label a coordination bond, an arrow or any clear label is accepted. Markschemes also accept the older names “dative bond” and “coordinate bond”.

HL only: coordination bonds also hold molecules and ions (ligands) to a transition element ion in a complex ion. This is covered on the HL page for 2.2 and in Structure 3.1.

Structure 2.2.4

Shapes of molecules: the VSEPR model

Tie four balloons together at their necks. They push each other apart and point to the corners of a pyramid shape. You do not have to arrange them. They do it themselves.

Groups of electrons around an atom behave in the same way. They are all negative, so they repel each other and move as far apart as possible.

This idea is the valence shell electron pair repulsion (VSEPR) model. It predicts the shape of a molecule from the repulsion between the electron domains around a central atom.

Electron domains

An electron domain is a region of electron density around the central atom. Each of these counts as one domain:

  • a lone pair,
  • a single bond,
  • a double bond,
  • a triple bond.

A double bond is two pairs of electrons, but both pairs point in the same direction. So it is one domain.

Two geometries, two names

The electron domain geometry is the arrangement of all the domains, including lone pairs.

The molecular geometry is the arrangement of the atoms only. This is the shape of the molecule.

Lone pairs still take up space. But you cannot “see” them in the shape, so they are not part of the name of the molecular geometry.

Lone pairs change the bond angle

A bonding pair is held between two nuclei. A lone pair is held by one nucleus only, so it spreads out more and stays closer to the central atom.

Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs. They push the bonding pairs closer together, so the bond angle becomes smaller than the ideal angle.

Methane, ammonia and water all have four electron domains. Compare their angles.

Multiple bonds change the bond angle too

A double or triple bond holds more electrons than a single bond. It has greater electron density, so it repels the other domains more strongly.

Methanal, HCHO, has three domains around carbon: two C–H bonds and one C=O bond. The shape is trigonal planar. The C=O bond pushes the two C–H bonds together. The H–C–H angle is about 116°, a little less than 120°.

Method in words

  1. Draw the Lewis formula.
  2. Count the electron domains around the central atom. Each lone pair, single bond, double bond or triple bond counts as one.
  3. Name the electron domain geometry: 2 is linear, 3 is trigonal planar, 4 is tetrahedral.
  4. Now ignore the lone pairs and name the shape made by the atoms. This is the molecular geometry.
  5. State the bond angle. With no lone pairs, give the ideal angle. With lone pairs, write “less than” the ideal angle.

Shape and bond angle with a lone pair

Predict the electron domain geometry, the molecular geometry and the F–N–F bond angle of nitrogen trifluoride, NF3.

  1. You cannot count domains without it. This is the formula from worked example 1.

  2. Count around nitrogen only. The lone pairs on fluorine do not matter here.

    3 single bonds + 1 lone pair = 4 electron domains

  3. Four domains get as far apart as possible by pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron.

    electron domain geometry: tetrahedral

  4. Look at the atoms only. Nitrogen sits above a triangle of three fluorine atoms.

    molecular geometry: trigonal pyramidal

  5. The lone pair repels more than the bonding pairs, so the bonds are pushed together.

    F–N–F angle: less than 109.5°

    The measured angle is about 102°. You are not expected to know that number.

Tetrahedral electron domain geometry, trigonal pyramidal molecular geometry, bond angle less than 109.5°.

Shape and bond angle with a double bond

Predict the shape of methanal, HCHO, and compare the H–C–H angle with 120°.

  1. Carbon is central, with two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom around it.

  2. The double bond is one domain, not two.

    2 single bonds + 1 double bond + 0 lone pairs = 3 electron domains

  3. There are no lone pairs on carbon, so the two geometries are the same.

    electron domain geometry = molecular geometry = trigonal planar

  4. The double bond has greater electron density than a single bond, so it repels more.

    H–C–H angle: slightly less than 120°

Trigonal planar. The H–C–H angle is slightly less than 120° because the C=O double bond repels the C–H bonding pairs more strongly.

How many electron domains are there around the oxygen atom in the hydronium ion, H3O+?

  1. Look at the Lewis formula in worked example 3.
  2. Count the bonds to oxygen and the lone pairs on oxygen.

Three O–H bonds and one lone pair: 4 electron domains. The electron domain geometry is tetrahedral and the molecular geometry is trigonal pyramidal.

How many electron domains are there around the carbon atom in hydrogen cyanide, H–C≡N?

  1. Carbon has one single bond and one triple bond, and no lone pairs.
  2. A triple bond counts as one domain.

One single bond and one triple bond: 2 electron domains. The molecule is linear, with a bond angle of 180°.

Three slips cost marks here. Giving the molecular geometry when the electron domain geometry is asked, or the reverse. Counting a double bond as two domains. Writing the ideal angle (109.5°) for a molecule that has lone pairs.

“State and explain the shape” is two marks. One is for the name of the shape. The other is for the reason: the number of electron domains, or the number of bonding pairs and lone pairs, around the central atom. For a bent or pyramidal molecule an angle in a sensible range below the ideal value is accepted.

Shapes with five or six electron domains are Additional HL and are on the HL page. The guide does not list exact angles to memorize. “Less than 109.5°” with the reason is what you must be able to give.

Structure 2.2.5

Bond polarity

Sharing is not always equal. In a tug of war between a strong team and a weak team, the rope moves towards the strong team. In a bond, the shared electrons move towards the atom that attracts them more.

Electronegativity measures how strongly an atom attracts a shared pair of electrons. It has no unit. Fluorine has the highest value, 4.0.

Electronegativity values are in section 9 of the data booklet. Use those values. Older books give slightly different numbers, such as 2.5 for carbon and 3.5 for oxygen.

Polar bonds

When two different atoms are bonded, the more electronegative atom pulls the shared pair closer. That atom becomes slightly negative. The other atom becomes slightly positive.

These small charges are partial charges. They are written δ+ and δ−. The symbol δ (delta) means “a little”.

A bond with a δ+ end and a δ− end is a polar bond. The separation of charge is a bond dipole.

Method in words

  1. Find the electronegativity of each atom in the data booklet.
  2. Subtract the smaller value from the larger value.
  3. If the difference is zero, the bond is non-polar. The larger the difference, the more polar the bond.
  4. Write δ− on the atom with the larger value and δ+ on the other atom.

Use the table of electronegativity values. Calculate the difference in electronegativity for a C–Cl bond.

  1. Carbon is 2.6 and chlorine is 3.2.
  2. Subtract the smaller value from the larger value.
3.2 − 2.6 = 0.6

Chlorine has the larger value, so chlorine is δ− and carbon is δ+.

Calculate the difference in electronegativity for a C=O bond. Which atom is δ−?

  1. Carbon is 2.6 and oxygen is 3.4.
  2. A double bond is treated in the same way as a single bond here.
3.4 − 2.6 = 0.8

Oxygen has the larger value, so oxygen is δ− and carbon is δ+.

A partial charge is not an ion charge. Write δ+ and δ−, never + and −. And put δ− on the atom with the larger electronegativity, because it pulls the negative electrons towards itself.

If you are asked to show the polarity of a bond, you need both partial charges, δ+ and δ−, on the correct atoms for the mark. To explain why a bond is polar, write “the two atoms have different electronegativities”.

The guide gives no cut-off numbers (such as 0.4 or 1.8) for “non-polar”, “polar” and “ionic”. You do not need to memorize any. To decide the type of bonding from electronegativity, you will use the bonding triangle in Structure 2.4. The trends in electronegativity are explained in Structure 3.1.

Structure 2.2.6

Molecular polarity

Two equal teams pull a rope in opposite directions. Both pull hard, but the rope does not move. Equal pulls in opposite directions cancel.

Bond dipoles work in the same way. Each bond dipole has a size and a direction. If the dipoles are equal and the shape is symmetrical, they cancel.

A polar molecule has a net dipole: its bond dipoles do not cancel. A non-polar molecule has no net dipole.

Molecular polarity depends on two things: the polarity of the bonds and the geometry of the molecule. You always need both.

Same kind of bond, different shape

Carbon dioxide has two polar C=O bonds. The molecule is linear, so the two dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel. CO2 is non-polar.

Water has two polar O–H bonds. The molecule is bent, so the two dipoles do not point in opposite directions. They add up. H2O is polar.

Method in words

  1. Draw the Lewis formula and deduce the molecular geometry.
  2. Decide whether each bond is polar. Use electronegativity values.
  3. Ask: do the bond dipoles cancel? They cancel when the outer atoms are all the same and the shape is linear, trigonal planar or tetrahedral, with no lone pairs on the central atom.
  4. If they cancel, the molecule is non-polar. If they do not, the molecule is polar.

The same reasoning works for ions. In the ammonium ion, NH4+, the four N–H dipoles cancel because the ion is tetrahedral. The ion has a charge, but it has no net dipole.

Is the molecule polar?

Deduce whether boron trifluoride, BF3, and ammonia, NH3, are polar molecules.

  1. A molecule can only be polar if it has polar bonds. Use the booklet values.

    B–F: 4.0 − 2.0 = 2.0, so polar, with F δ−
    N–H: 3.0 − 2.2 = 0.8, so polar, with N δ−

  2. Boron has three bonding domains and no lone pairs.

    BF3: 3 electron domains, 0 lone pairs → trigonal planar, 120°

  3. Nitrogen has three bonding domains and one lone pair.

    NH3: 4 electron domains, 1 lone pair → trigonal pyramidal

  4. Three equal dipoles at 120° in one plane pull equally in all directions.

    BF3: bond dipoles cancel → non-polar

  5. The three N–H dipoles all point partly towards the nitrogen end of the pyramid. Nothing pulls the other way.

    NH3: bond dipoles do not cancel → polar

BF3 is non-polar: its polar bonds are arranged symmetrically (trigonal planar), so the dipoles cancel. NH3 is polar: it is trigonal pyramidal, so the dipoles do not cancel.

How many of these five molecules are polar? CO2, H2O, NH3, CH4, CHCl3

  1. First name the shape of each one: linear, bent, trigonal pyramidal, tetrahedral, tetrahedral.
  2. Symmetrical shapes with identical outer atoms have no net dipole. Check whether the outer atoms of CHCl3 are identical.

Three: H2O (bent), NH3 (trigonal pyramidal) and CHCl3 (tetrahedral, but the outer atoms are not all the same).

CO2 and CH4 are symmetrical, so their bond dipoles cancel.

“It has polar bonds, so the molecule is polar” is wrong reasoning. CO2, BF3 and CCl4 all have polar bonds and are non-polar molecules. Always check the shape.

For “explain why the molecule is polar” write two things. First: the bonds are polar because the atoms differ in electronegativity. Second: the bond dipoles do not cancel because of the shape, so there is a net dipole. An answer that only mentions electronegativity does not earn the second mark.

You do not need to calculate a dipole moment or use its unit (the debye). The question is always a yes or no decision with a reason.

Structure 2.2.7

Covalent network structures

A drop of water contains billions of separate molecules. A diamond is different. Every carbon atom in it is joined by covalent bonds to its neighbours, and those to their neighbours, through the whole crystal.

A covalent network structure is a giant structure of atoms joined by covalent bonds throughout. It has no separate molecules. Carbon and silicon form covalent network structures.

Allotropes are different forms of the same element. Their atoms are bonded and arranged in different patterns, so they have different chemical and physical properties. Diamond, graphite, graphene and fullerenes are allotropes of carbon.

Diamond

Each carbon atom is bonded to four other carbon atoms. The four bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron, with angles of 109.5°. All four valence electrons of each atom are held in bonds.

  • Very hard and a very high melting point: many strong covalent bonds must be broken.
  • Does not conduct electricity: there are no delocalized electrons and no ions.
  • Insoluble in water and in other solvents.

Graphite

Each carbon atom is bonded to three other carbon atoms. The atoms form flat layers of hexagons, with angles of 120°.

Three of the four valence electrons of each atom are in bonds. The fourth is delocalized: it is free to move along the layer.

Between the layers there are only weak London (dispersion) forces. You will meet these forces in section 2.2.8.

  • Conducts electricity: the delocalized electrons can move along the layers.
  • Soft and slippery: the layers slide over each other easily. Graphite is used in pencils and as a lubricant.
  • Very high melting point: the covalent bonds inside the layers are strong.

Graphene

Graphene is a single layer of graphite. It is one atom thick. Each carbon atom is bonded to three others in a flat sheet of hexagons, and one electron per atom is delocalized.

  • A very good conductor of electricity and of heat: delocalized electrons move across the sheet.
  • Very strong for its mass, because of the strong covalent bonds in the sheet.
  • Very thin, light, flexible and almost transparent.

Fullerenes

Fullerenes are closed cages of carbon atoms. The one to know is C60. Its 60 atoms form a ball of 12 pentagons and 20 hexagons, like a football. Each carbon atom is bonded to three others.

C60 is a molecule. It is not a network. Solid C60 is made of separate molecules, held together by weak London (dispersion) forces.

  • It turns into a gas at a much lower temperature than diamond or graphite: only the forces between molecules must be overcome.
  • A poor conductor of electricity: electrons are delocalized inside one molecule but cannot move from molecule to molecule.
  • Dissolves in some non-polar solvents, because it is made of separate non-polar molecules.

Silicon

Silicon is below carbon in group 14, so it also has four valence electrons. Solid silicon has the same structure as diamond: each silicon atom is bonded to four others in a tetrahedral arrangement.

Silicon is hard and has a high melting point, 1414 °C. This is lower than carbon’s. A silicon atom is larger than a carbon atom, so the Si–Si bond is longer and weaker than the C–C bond.

Silicon dioxide

Silicon dioxide (silica) is the main substance in sand and quartz. Each silicon atom is bonded to four oxygen atoms in a tetrahedral arrangement. Each oxygen atom is bonded to two silicon atoms.

The formula SiO2 is a ratio: one silicon atom for every two oxygen atoms. There are no SiO2 molecules. Compare this with CO2, which is made of small separate molecules and is a gas.

  • Hard, with a high melting point (about 1700 °C): strong Si–O covalent bonds run through the whole structure.
  • Does not conduct electricity: no delocalized electrons and no mobile ions.
  • Insoluble in water.

Structure 2.2.8

Intermolecular forces

Water forms drops. Petrol evaporates quickly. Candle wax is a solid. In all three, the molecules attract each other, but with different strengths.

Intermolecular forces are forces of attraction between molecules. They are much weaker than the covalent bonds inside a molecule.

When a molecular substance melts or boils, the molecules move apart. Only the intermolecular forces are overcome. The covalent bonds inside each molecule do not break.

The kind of force between molecules depends on two things: the size of the molecules and their polarity. There are four kinds to know.

London (dispersion) forces

The electrons in a molecule are always moving. At any moment, there may be slightly more electrons on one side than on the other. For that moment the molecule has a small, temporary dipole.

This temporary dipole pushes or pulls the electrons in a neighbouring molecule. It induces (causes) a dipole there. The two temporary dipoles attract each other.

These attractions are London (dispersion) forces. Two facts matter most.

  • London forces act between all molecules and atoms, polar or non-polar, because all of them have electrons.
  • London forces are stronger when a molecule has more electrons, so they increase with molar mass. They are also stronger when molecules can touch over a larger surface area.

Dipole–dipole forces

A polar molecule has a permanent dipole: one end is always δ+ and the other is always δ−. The δ+ end of one molecule attracts the δ− end of the next.

These attractions are dipole–dipole forces. They act between polar molecules only, for example between HCl molecules.

Dipole–induced dipole forces

Now mix a polar substance with a non-polar one. The permanent dipole of the polar molecule shifts the electrons of the non-polar molecule and induces a dipole in it. The two then attract.

This is a dipole–induced dipole force. One example is the attraction between a polar water molecule and a non-polar oxygen molecule. It is why a little oxygen dissolves in water.

Hydrogen bonding

A hydrogen bond occurs when hydrogen, covalently bonded to an electronegative atom, has an attractive interaction with a neighbouring electronegative atom.

In almost all the examples you will meet, the electronegative atoms are nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine. So look for two things.

  1. A hydrogen atom bonded directly to N, O or F. This bond is very polar, so the H atom is strongly δ+.
  2. A lone pair on an N, O or F atom of a neighbouring molecule. The δ+ hydrogen is attracted to it.

Water, ammonia, hydrogen fluoride and ethanol all form hydrogen bonds between their own molecules. Hydrogen bonding is the strongest of the four intermolecular forces. It is still much weaker than a covalent bond.

A hydrogen bond is not the covalent O–H bond inside a molecule. It is the attraction between molecules. Also, a hydrogen atom bonded to carbon does not form hydrogen bonds. So propanone, CH3COCH3, has no hydrogen bonding between its own molecules, although it contains H atoms and an O atom.

“Explain what a hydrogen bond is” has two marks. One: hydrogen is covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom (N, O or F). Two: it is attracted to an electronegative atom (or its lone pair) on a neighbouring molecule. A clearly labelled diagram can earn both.

The term “van der Waals forces”

Van der Waals forces is an inclusive term. It covers three forces together: London (dispersion) forces, dipole–induced dipole forces and dipole–dipole forces. It does not include hydrogen bonding.

Do not write “van der Waals forces” when you mean London forces. Markschemes have refused it. Write “London (dispersion) forces”. “London forces” or “dispersion forces” are also accepted.

“Ion–dipole forces” are not in the guide’s list of intermolecular forces. You do not need the history of the hydrogen bond definition or any calculation of the strength of a force.

Structure 2.2.9

Explaining physical properties

Nail polish remover (propanone) dries on your skin in seconds. Water takes minutes. Cooking oil stays. The difference comes from the strength of the forces between the molecules.

For molecules of comparable molar mass, the strengths are generally: London (dispersion) forces < dipole–dipole forces < hydrogen bonding. All of them are much weaker than covalent bonds.

The words “comparable molar mass” matter. London forces grow with the number of electrons. A large non-polar molecule can have stronger forces in total than a small polar one. Iodine, I2, is non-polar, yet it is a solid at room temperature.

Volatility

Volatility is how easily a substance turns into a vapour. A volatile substance has a low boiling point.

To boil a liquid, you must give its molecules enough energy to separate. Stronger intermolecular forces need more energy. So stronger forces mean a higher boiling point and a lower volatility.

Explaining a boiling-point difference

Butane, CH3CH2CH2CH3, boils at −1 °C. Propanone, CH3COCH3, boils at 56 °C. Their molar masses are 58.14 and 58.09 g mol-1. Explain the difference in boiling point.

  1. The types of force depend on polarity. Butane contains only C–C and C–H bonds. Propanone has a polar C=O bond that nothing cancels.

    butane: non-polar
    propanone: polar (C=O, 3.4 − 2.6 = 0.8)

  2. This is the first mark. Naming the force in only one substance is not enough.

    butane: London (dispersion) forces only
    propanone: London (dispersion) forces and dipole–dipole forces

  3. Propanone contains oxygen, so check. Every H atom in propanone is bonded to carbon.

    no H bonded to N, O or F → no hydrogen bonding in either

  4. This is the second mark. The molar masses are almost equal, so the London forces are similar.

    propanone has dipole–dipole forces as well → stronger intermolecular forces in propanone

  5. Finish by connecting force strength to the property in the question.

    more energy is needed to separate propanone molecules → higher boiling point

Butane has London (dispersion) forces only. Propanone has London (dispersion) forces and dipole–dipole forces. The intermolecular forces in propanone are stronger, so more energy is needed to separate its molecules.

Boiling-point questions are marked in the same way every time. Mark 1: name the intermolecular forces in both substances. Mark 2: state which are stronger. “Stronger bonding” or “stronger forces” without the names earns nothing.

Never write that covalent bonds break when a molecular substance boils. Boiling water gives water vapour, which is still H2O molecules. Only the forces between the molecules are overcome.

Electrical conductivity

An electric current is a flow of charged particles. A substance conducts only if it has charged particles that are free to move: ions or delocalized electrons.

  • Molecular substances do not conduct as solids, liquids or gases. Molecules have no overall charge, and their electrons are held in bonds or lone pairs.
  • Covalent network solids do not conduct either. Diamond and silicon dioxide have no delocalized electrons.
  • Graphite and graphene are the exceptions. They have delocalized electrons, so they conduct.

Solubility

A useful rule is “like dissolves like”. Polar substances tend to dissolve in polar solvents such as water. Non-polar substances tend to dissolve in non-polar solvents such as hexane.

The reason is about forces. A substance dissolves when its molecules can form attractions with the solvent molecules that are about as strong as the attractions that were there before.

  • Water molecules are held to each other by hydrogen bonds. A solute that can form hydrogen bonds with water, such as ethanol, mixes in easily.
  • A non-polar solute, such as hexane, can form only weak London forces with water. These cannot replace the hydrogen bonds between water molecules. So the two liquids stay separate.
  • As the hydrocarbon chain of an alcohol gets longer, more of the molecule is non-polar. Solubility in water falls. Ethanol mixes completely with water. Octan-1-ol hardly dissolves.
  • Covalent network solids are insoluble in all common solvents. Dissolving would mean breaking strong covalent bonds.

Some molecules cannot form hydrogen bonds with themselves but can accept them from water. Propanone has a lone pair on oxygen, so a water H atom can hydrogen-bond to it. This is why propanone mixes with water.

Explaining solubility

Ethanol, CH3CH2OH, mixes completely with water. Hexane, C6H14, is almost insoluble in water. Explain this difference.

  1. Solubility depends on both partners, so start with water.

    water: polar, hydrogen bonding between molecules

  2. Look for an H atom bonded to N, O or F, and for polarity.

    ethanol: has an O–H group, polar
    hexane: only C–C and C–H bonds, non-polar

  3. A solute dissolves well if it can attract water molecules strongly.

    ethanol and water: hydrogen bonds form between them
    hexane and water: only weak London (dispersion) forces

  4. The new attractions must be able to replace the hydrogen bonds between water molecules.

    hexane–water forces are much weaker than water–water hydrogen bonds → hexane does not mix

Ethanol has an O–H group, so it forms hydrogen bonds with water molecules and dissolves. Hexane is non-polar. It forms only London (dispersion) forces with water, which are too weak to replace the hydrogen bonds between water molecules.

Structure 2.2.10

Chromatography

A drop of water falls on a note written in black ink. As the water spreads through the paper, the black mark separates into bands of blue, red and yellow. The ink was a mixture, and the water has separated it.

Chromatography is a technique that separates the components of a mixture. It works because the components have different attractions, involving intermolecular forces, to two phases.

  • The stationary phase stays in place.
  • The mobile phase moves. It is the solvent, and it carries the mixture with it.
The two techniques you need
TechniqueStationary phaseMobile phase
paper chromatographywater held in the fibres of the papera solvent that rises up the paper
thin layer chromatography (TLC)a thin layer of a solid such as silica (SiO2) on a glass or plastic platea solvent that rises up the plate

Why the components separate

Each component is pulled in two ways at once. It is attracted to the stationary phase, which holds it back. It is attracted to the moving solvent, which carries it forward.

  • A component that is attracted strongly to the stationary phase spends more time held still. It moves a short distance.
  • A component that is attracted more to the mobile phase (it is more soluble in the solvent) spends more time moving. It travels far.

In both paper chromatography and TLC with silica, the stationary phase is polar and can form hydrogen bonds. So polar components, and those that can hydrogen-bond, are held back most. Less polar components travel further with a less polar solvent.

How a chromatogram is run

  1. Draw a baseline in pencil near the bottom of the paper. Pencil does not dissolve in the solvent. Ink would dissolve and move.
  2. Put a small spot of the mixture on the baseline. Put spots of known pure substances beside it for comparison.
  3. Stand the paper in a little solvent. The baseline must be above the solvent level. Otherwise the spots dissolve into the solvent in the container.
  4. Cover the container. This stops the solvent evaporating from the paper as it rises.
  5. Take the paper out before the solvent reaches the top. Mark the solvent front with pencil at once, before the solvent dries.

The result is a chromatogram.

The retardation factor, RF

How far a spot moves depends on how far the solvent was allowed to run. So chemists use a ratio instead of a distance.

RF = dspotdfront
RF
retardation factor (a ratio, so it has no unit)
dspot
distance moved by the spot: from the baseline to the centre of the spot cm
dfront
distance moved by the solvent front: from the baseline to the solvent front cm
  • Both distances are measured from the baseline.
  • An RF value has no unit and is always between 0 and 1.
  • A substance has the same RF value every time, if the stationary phase, the solvent and the temperature are the same.

This lets you interpret a chromatogram. If a spot from the mixture has the same RF value as a known substance run in the same way, it is probably that substance. A pure substance gives one spot. A mixture usually gives more than one.

Method in words

  1. Measure from the baseline to the centre of the spot.
  2. Measure from the baseline to the solvent front.
  3. Divide the first distance by the second. Use the same unit for both (cm or mm).
  4. Give the answer to 2 significant figures, with no unit. Check that it is less than 1.

Calculating an RF value

On a chromatogram the solvent front is 8.0 cm above the baseline. The centre of a blue spot is 3.6 cm above the baseline. Calculate the RF value of the blue substance.

  1. Both must start at the baseline. Here they already do.

    distance moved by the spot = 3.6 cm
    distance moved by the solvent front = 8.0 cm

  2. The spot can never travel further than the solvent, so the smaller number goes on top.

    RF = 3.6 cm / 8.0 cm

  3. The units cancel, so the answer has no unit.

    RF = 0.45

  4. The value is between 0 and 1, and the data have 2 significant figures.

    RF = 0.45

RF = 0.45 (no unit)

A spot moves 2.7 cm from the baseline. The solvent front moves 7.5 cm from the baseline. Calculate the RF value.

  1. Which distance goes on top? The answer must be less than 1.
  2. Divide 2.7 cm by 7.5 cm.
RF = 2.7 cm / 7.5 cm = 0.36

A student measures everything from the bottom edge of the paper. The baseline is at 1.5 cm, the centre of a spot is at 6.3 cm, and the solvent front is at 9.5 cm. Calculate the RF value of the spot.

  1. The distances you need start at the baseline, not at the bottom edge. Subtract 1.5 cm from each reading.
  2. The spot moved 6.3 − 1.5 = 4.8 cm. Work out how far the solvent front moved in the same way, then divide.
distance moved by the spot = 6.3 cm − 1.5 cm = 4.8 cm
distance moved by the solvent front = 9.5 cm − 1.5 cm = 8.0 cm
RF = 4.8 cm / 8.0 cm = 0.60

Three errors are common. Measuring from the bottom edge of the paper, not from the baseline. Dividing by the distance to the top of the paper, not to the solvent front. Giving a value greater than 1, or adding a unit.

Do not reverse the polarity logic. With a polar stationary phase, the more polar component is held back more. It moves less and has the smaller RF value.

Because you measure from a diagram, markschemes accept a small range around the RF value. To explain the principle, say: the components have different attractions to the stationary phase and to the mobile phase (or different solubilities in the solvent), so they move at different speeds. To improve a poor separation, suggest a different solvent or a longer paper.

You do not need to know about locating agents (chemicals or lamps used to make colourless spots visible). The technical and operating details of gas chromatography and high-performance liquid chromatography will not be assessed.

Linking it up

These are some of the linking questions from the guide, with short answers. They connect this topic to others.

Why do ionic bonds only form between different elements, while covalent bonds can form between atoms of the same element?

An ionic bond needs one atom to lose electrons and another to gain them. That needs a large difference in electronegativity, so the two atoms must be different elements. Two atoms of the same element attract electrons equally. Neither can take electrons from the other, so they share them in a covalent bond. (Structure 2.1)

Why are silicon–silicon bonds generally weaker than carbon–carbon bonds?

A silicon atom has one more occupied energy level than a carbon atom, so it is larger. In an Si–Si bond the shared pair is further from both nuclei, and the attraction is weaker. The data booklet shows this: Si–Si is 232 pm and 226 kJ mol-1, while C–C is 154 pm and 346 kJ mol-1. (Structure 3.1)

To what extent can intermolecular forces explain the deviation of real gases from ideal behaviour?

The ideal gas model assumes that there are no forces between the particles. Real molecules do attract each other. At low temperature and high pressure the molecules are slow and close together, so these attractions matter and the gas no longer behaves ideally. Gases with stronger intermolecular forces deviate more. (Structure 1.5)

How do the terms “bonds” and “forces” compare?

Both are electrostatic attractions. “Bond” is normally used for the strong attractions that hold atoms or ions together: ionic, covalent and metallic bonds. “Force” is normally used for the much weaker attractions between molecules. The hydrogen bond has “bond” in its name, but it is an intermolecular force.

What features of a molecule make it “infrared (IR) active”?

HL only (Structure 3.2): a molecule absorbs infrared radiation when a vibration changes its dipole. So bond polarity and molecular geometry, the ideas of section 2.2.6, decide whether a molecule such as CO2 or H2O absorbs infrared radiation.
