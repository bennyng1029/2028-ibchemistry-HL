Stereoisomers and cis–trans isomers

Your left hand and your right hand have the same parts, joined in the same order. But they are not the same shape. Some molecules are like this too.

Stereoisomers have the same molecular formula and the same structural formula. Their atoms are joined in the same order, with the same single, double and triple bonds. Only the arrangement of the atoms in space is different.

Compare this with structural isomers on the core page. In structural isomers the atoms are joined in a different order.

You need two kinds of stereoisomer: cis–trans isomers and optical isomers.

What cis–trans isomers are

Look at but-2-ene, CH3CH=CHCH3. Each carbon of the double bond holds one H atom and one CH3 group. There are two ways to arrange them.

In the cis isomer the two matching groups are on the same side of the double bond. In the trans isomer they are on opposite sides.

These are two different compounds. One cannot turn into the other, because atoms cannot rotate around a double bond. To rotate, the π bond would have to break.

Cis–trans isomerism needs two things. First, rotation must be restricted: by a C=C double bond or by a ring. Second, each of the two carbon atoms must have two different groups attached.

The two-step test for an alkene

  1. Find the C=C double bond. Look at the two groups on the left carbon. Are they different from each other?
  2. Look at the two groups on the right carbon. Are they different from each other?

If both answers are yes, the alkene has cis–trans isomers. If one carbon has two identical groups, it does not. The groups on the left carbon do not have to match the groups on the right carbon.

Rings of three and four carbon atoms

A ring also stops rotation. Each ring carbon has one bond pointing above the ring and one pointing below it. If two different ring carbons each have two different groups, there are two isomers.

In these drawings, a solid wedge is a bond coming towards you. A hashed wedge is a bond going away from you.

Students often say that but-1-ene has cis–trans isomers because it has a double bond. It does not. One carbon of its double bond has two H atoms. A double bond alone is not enough.

Does pent-2-ene have cis–trans isomers?

Deduce whether pent-2-ene and 2-methylbut-2-ene show cis–trans isomerism. Draw the isomers where they exist.

  1. You can only compare groups when you can see what is attached to each carbon of the C=C.

    In pent-2-ene the double bond is between carbon 2 and carbon 3.

  2. Each carbon must have two different groups.

    Carbon 2 has an H atom and a CH3 group. These are different.

  3. One carbon with two identical groups would rule it out.

    Carbon 3 has an H atom and a CH2CH3 group. These are different. So pent-2-ene has cis–trans isomers.

  4. Keep the left carbon the same. Swap the two groups on the right carbon.

  5. Use the same two-step test.

    The left carbon of the C=C has two CH3 groups. Swapping them changes nothing. 2-methylbut-2-ene has no cis–trans isomers.

Pent-2-ene has a cis and a trans isomer. 2-methylbut-2-ene does not.

How many of these four alkenes show cis–trans isomerism: but-1-ene, but-2-ene, 2-methylpropene, pent-2-ene?

  1. Write the two groups on each carbon of the C=C for every alkene.
  2. But-1-ene is CH2=CHCH2CH3 and 2-methylpropene is CH2=C(CH3)2. Each has a carbon with two identical groups.

Only but-2-ene and pent-2-ene have two different groups on each carbon of the C=C. The answer is 2.

To explain cis–trans isomerism, give both points: restricted rotation around the C=C (or in the ring), and two different groups on each of the two carbon atoms. In a drawing, show clearly which groups are on the same side.

The E–Z naming system is not assessed. You also do not need cis–trans isomers of rings with more than four carbon atoms, or of rings that contain a double bond.

Chiral carbon atoms and enantiomers

Hold your hands up, palms facing you. One hand is the mirror image of the other. Now try to place one hand exactly on top of the other, both palms down. The thumbs point in opposite directions. Your hands are mirror images that cannot be superimposed.

A molecule can be like this when it has a chiral carbon atom.

A chiral carbon is a carbon atom bonded to four different atoms or groups. It is often marked with an asterisk (*).

A molecule with one chiral carbon exists in two forms. Each form is the mirror image of the other, and the two cannot be superimposed. These two stereoisomers are called enantiomers. They are also called optical isomers.

How to find a chiral carbon

  1. Ignore every carbon with a double or triple bond. It has fewer than four groups.
  2. Ignore every CH3 and CH2 carbon. It has two or three identical H atoms.
  3. For each carbon that is left, write its four groups. Compare whole groups, not only the first atom. CH3 and CH2CH3 are different groups.

In a skeletal formula, remember the H atoms that are not drawn.

Drawing a pair of enantiomers

A flat drawing cannot show a tetrahedral carbon. You need wedge–dash notation.

  • A plain line is a bond in the plane of the paper.
  • A solid wedge is a bond coming out of the paper, towards you.
  • A hashed wedge is a bond going into the paper, away from you.

Draw two plain bonds, one solid wedge and one hashed wedge around the chiral carbon. Then draw a mirror line and reflect the whole drawing.

These are the two enantiomers of butan-2-ol. The dashed line is the mirror.

Enantiomer drawings must look three-dimensional and must be mirror images of each other. Use tapered wedge and hashed bonds. Drawings with all four bonds at 90° do not earn the marks. When asked what a chiral carbon is, write “a carbon atom bonded to four different atoms or groups”. A description of optical properties alone does not earn that mark.

Find the chiral carbon and draw both enantiomers

Identify the chiral carbon atom in 2-chlorobutane, CH3CHClCH2CH3. Draw the two enantiomers.

  1. A carbon with two or three H atoms has identical groups.

    Carbon 1 and carbon 4 are CH3 groups. Carbon 3 is a CH2 group. None of them can be chiral.

  2. A chiral carbon needs four different atoms or groups.

    Carbon 2 is bonded to H, Cl, CH3 and CH2CH3. All four are different, so carbon 2 is chiral.

  3. The four bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron.

    Put the chiral carbon in the centre. Draw two plain bonds with a wide angle between them. Add one solid wedge and one hashed wedge. Attach the four groups. Their order does not matter for the first drawing.

  4. The second enantiomer is the mirror image of the first.

    Each group is the same distance from the mirror line on the other side. A wedge stays a wedge and a hashed bond stays a hashed bond.

Carbon 2 is the chiral carbon. The two mirror-image drawings are the enantiomers.

How many chiral carbon atoms are in a molecule of CH3CH(OH)CH(NH2)COOH?

  1. The CH3 carbon and the COOH carbon cannot be chiral. Test the two carbons in the middle.
  2. Carbon 3 has H, OH, CH3 and the rest of the chain. Carbon 2 has H, NH2, COOH and the rest of the chain.

Both middle carbons have four different groups. The answer is 2.

Optical activity

Ordinary light vibrates in all planes. Plane-polarized light vibrates in one plane only.

A substance is optically active if it rotates the plane of plane-polarized light. Each enantiomer of a chiral compound is optically active.

The two enantiomers rotate the plane by the same angle, but in opposite directions. One rotates it clockwise and the other anticlockwise. This is the “different optical property” of enantiomers. An instrument called a polarimeter measures the rotation.

A racemic mixture contains equal amounts of the two enantiomers. The two rotations cancel, so a racemic mixture does not rotate plane-polarized light. It is optically inactive.

How enantiomers compare

Properties of a pair of enantiomers
PropertyThe two enantiomers
Melting point, boiling point, solubility, densitythe same
Effect on plane-polarized lightsame angle of rotation, opposite directions
Reactions with substances that are not chiralthe same
Behaviour in a chiral environmentdifferent

A chiral environment contains other chiral molecules. The human body is one, because enzymes are chiral. So two enantiomers can have different effects in the body.

You do not need R/S, D/L or (+)/(−) labels. You do not need diastereomers, meso compounds or rules for counting stereoisomers. You do not need to explain how a polarimeter works or to calculate a rotation. For the chemistry of enantiomers, it is enough to know that they behave differently in chiral environments.

A racemic mixture is not “a molecule that is not chiral”. It is a 50 : 50 mixture of two chiral molecules. Each one still rotates the light. The rotations cancel.

Mass spectrometry of organic compounds

The question it answers: how heavy is the molecule, and which pieces can break off it?

Imagine dropping a model made of building bricks. It breaks into pieces. If you weigh the pieces, you can work out how the model was put together. A mass spectrometer does this to molecules.

The molecular ion

In the mass spectrometer, a molecule loses one electron. This makes a positive ion called the molecular ion, written M+. It has the same mass as the whole molecule.

CH3COCH3 -> CH3COCH3+ + e-

The ion has a charge of 1+, so its m/z value equals its mass. The m/z of the molecular ion peak gives the relative molecular mass, Mr, of the compound.

Fragmentation

Many molecular ions break into smaller pieces. This is called fragmentation. One piece keeps the positive charge and is detected. The other piece has no charge and is not detected.

CH3COCH3+ -> CH3CO+ + •CH3

Here the ion CH3CO+ gives a peak at m/z = 43. The piece that was lost, •CH3, has a mass of 15. You find it by subtraction: 58 − 43 = 15.

The set of peaks is the fragmentation pattern. It tells you which groups the molecule contains.

Method in words

  1. Find the molecular ion peak. It is the peak at the highest m/z. Its m/z is Mr.
  2. Choose another peak. Subtract its m/z from Mr. This is the mass lost.
  3. Look up the mass lost in the data booklet. It suggests the piece that was lost.
  4. Write the formula of the ion that was detected. Always include the positive charge.
  5. Check that the piece fits the structure you are testing.

Structure 3.2.9

Infrared spectroscopy

The question it answers: which types of bond does the molecule contain?

Think of two balls joined by a spring. The spring can stretch and shorten at its own natural frequency. A covalent bond behaves in a similar way. It vibrates all the time.

A bond can absorb infrared (IR) radiation that has the right energy for its vibration. Each type of bond absorbs in its own range. So an IR spectrum shows which types of bond a molecule contains.

Reading an IR spectrum

  • The horizontal axis is the wavenumber, in cm-1. It runs from 4000 on the left down to about 400 on the right.
  • The vertical axis is transmittance: the percentage of the radiation that passes through the sample.
  • An absorption shows as a dip. The line goes down where the sample absorbs.
  • Note three things for each dip: where it is, how strong it is, and whether it is broad or sharp.

Section 20 of the data booklet gives the wavenumber range for each bond. You do not need to memorize the ranges. The interactive spectrum below shows the whole table.

Almost every organic compound has C–H bonds. So the C–H absorption rarely helps you to tell compounds apart.

Method in words

  1. Look for a strong absorption at 1700–1750 cm-1. If it is there, the molecule has a C=O bond.
  2. Look for a broad absorption above 2500 cm-1. Broad at 3200–3600 is the O–H of an alcohol. Very broad at 2500–3000 is the O–H of a carboxylic acid.
  3. Match any other strong absorption to a row of the booklet table. Check that the shape fits too.
  4. Note which bonds are absent. “No O–H” and “no C=O” are useful evidence.
  5. Write each result as a bond with its booklet range.

The fingerprint region

Below about 1500 cm-1, a spectrum has many overlapping absorptions. This part is called the fingerprint region, because its pattern is different for every compound.

You are only asked to interpret the functional group region, using the booklet table. You do not need to explain the pattern of small peaks in the fingerprint region. You can still be asked about a strong absorption that matches a booklet row there, such as C–O.

Acid or ester?

A compound has the molecular formula C3H6O2. It is either propanoic acid, CH3CH2COOH, or methyl ethanoate, CH3COOCH3. Use the sketch of its IR spectrum to identify it. The absorptions are labelled A (2500–3000 cm-1, very broad), B (1715 cm-1) and C (1240 cm-1).

  1. Start with the clearest absorption. Find the booklet range that contains it.

    B: 1715 cm−1 is inside 1700–1750 cm−1 → C=O

    Both compounds contain C=O, so B does not decide between them.

  2. The shape matters as much as the position.

    A: very broad, 2500–3000 cm−1 → O–H in a carboxylic acid

  3. Check that the remaining strong absorption also fits.

    C: 1240 cm−1 is inside 1050–1410 cm−1 → C–O

  4. Look for a bond that one compound has and the other does not.

    Methyl ethanoate has no O–H bond. It could not give absorption A.

    The compound is propanoic acid.

Propanoic acid, because the spectrum shows the very broad O–H absorption of a carboxylic acid at 2500–3000 cm−1, which an ester does not have.

How many of these five compounds absorb strongly at 1700–1750 cm-1: propanone, ethanol, ethyl ethanoate, ethanoic acid, hexane?

  1. Find 1700–1750 cm-1 in the booklet table. Which bond is it?
  2. The bond is C=O. Which classes of compound contain C=O?

C=O is in the ketone (propanone), the ester (ethyl ethanoate) and the carboxylic acid (ethanoic acid). The answer is 3.

The two O–H rows are easy to mix up. An alcohol O–H absorbs at 3200–3600 cm-1. A carboxylic acid O–H absorbs lower, at 2500–3000 cm-1, and is much broader. It often covers the C–H absorption.

Give the bond and the wavenumber range together, for example “C=O at 1700–1750 cm-1”. One without the other may not earn the mark. Any value inside the booklet range is accepted. To choose between two compounds, name the compound, the bond that is present or absent, and its range.

Infrared radiation and greenhouse gases

The surface of the Earth gives out infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide, water vapour and methane absorb some of it, because their bonds vibrate at infrared frequencies.

The molecules then give the energy out again in all directions. Some of it returns to the surface. This keeps the lower atmosphere warmer.

Nitrogen and oxygen make up most of the air, but N2 and O2 do not absorb infrared radiation. The reason is in “Linking it up” below.

You do not need to know how an IR spectrometer works, or the physics of why each bond has its own frequency. You do not need to calculate a global warming potential.

Structure 3.2.10

Proton NMR: signals, chemical shifts and areas

The question it answers: how many different kinds of hydrogen atom does the molecule have, how many of each kind, and what is each kind next to?

Think of a class photograph. Students in the front row have different neighbours from students in the back row. In a molecule, hydrogen atoms also sit in different surroundings.

Proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, 1H NMR, detects the hydrogen atoms in a molecule. It gives one signal for each different surrounding.

A chemical environment is the set of atoms and bonds around a hydrogen atom. Hydrogen atoms with exactly the same surroundings are called equivalent. They are in the same environment.

A 1H NMR spectrum gives three pieces of information at this stage. The number of signals is the number of hydrogen environments. The area under each signal shows how many H atoms are in that environment. The chemical shift shows what type of environment it is.

1. Number of signals

Count the different hydrogen environments. Two rules help.

  • The H atoms on the same carbon atom are equivalent. The three H atoms of a CH3 group give one signal.
  • Groups that are mirror images of each other in a symmetrical molecule are equivalent. The two CH3 groups in propanone, CH3COCH3, give one signal.

Ethanol has three environments, labelled a, b and c. Its spectrum has three signals.

2. Area under each signal

The area under a signal is proportional to the number of H atoms in that environment. The spectrometer measures the areas. This is called integration, and it is often printed as a stepped line called an integration trace. A bigger step means a bigger area.

The areas give a ratio, not the actual numbers. Ethanol gives areas in the ratio 3 : 2 : 1. Ethoxyethane, CH3CH2OCH2CH3, has 6 H and 4 H in its two environments, so its ratio is also written 3 : 2.

3. Chemical shift

The position of a signal on the horizontal axis is its chemical shift, symbol δ, in parts per million (ppm). The axis runs from high values on the left to 0 on the right.

The chemical shift depends on what is near the hydrogen atom. An H atom close to an oxygen atom or a C=O group has a higher chemical shift.

Section 21 of the data booklet gives typical chemical shifts for each type of proton. The booklet states that the values are measured relative to tetramethylsilane (TMS), which is the zero of the scale. It also states that values can vary with the solvent and conditions.

Method in words

  1. Draw the structure so that every H atom is shown.
  2. Group the H atoms that have exactly the same surroundings. Look for symmetry. The number of groups is the number of signals.
  3. Count the H atoms in each group. Write the counts as the simplest ratio. This is the ratio of the areas.
  4. For each group, find the row of the booklet table that matches what its carbon is joined to. Quote that range.

To read a spectrum, work the other way: signals → environments, areas → H atoms in each, shifts → type of each environment.

The number of signals is not the number of hydrogen atoms, and it is not the number of carbon atoms. Propanone has six H atoms and three C atoms, but only one signal. Always count environments.

How many signals are in the 1H NMR spectrum of pentan-3-one, CH3CH2COCH2CH3?

  1. Draw the molecule. Is it symmetrical about the C=O group?
  2. The two CH3 groups are equivalent, and the two CH2 groups are equivalent.

There are two environments: the CH3 hydrogens (6 H) and the CH2 hydrogens (4 H). The answer is 2, with areas in the ratio 3 : 2.

How many signals are in the 1H NMR spectrum of ethyl ethanoate, CH3COOCH2CH3?

  1. There are two CH3 groups. Do they have the same surroundings?
  2. One CH3 is next to C=O. The other is next to a CH2 group. They are different environments.

The environments are CH3 next to C=O, CH2 next to O, and CH3 next to CH2. The answer is 3, with areas in the ratio 3 : 2 : 3.

For “number of signals and ratio of areas”, the ratio can be given in any order, but it must match the environments. For a chemical shift, quote the booklet range for that type of proton. A molecule with no hydrogen atoms gives no signals.

You do not need 13C NMR. You do not need to explain how NMR works, why TMS is used, or which solvents are chosen. You do not need to memorize chemical shifts.

Structure 3.2.11

Proton NMR: splitting patterns

The question it answers: how many hydrogen atoms are on the carbon atoms next to each group?

Look closely at a signal and you often see that it is not one peak. It is a small cluster of peaks. The signal has been split.

Splitting is caused by the hydrogen atoms on the neighbouring carbon atoms. So the splitting pattern of a signal tells you what is next to that group.

If there are n hydrogen atoms on the neighbouring carbon atoms, the signal is split into n + 1 peaks. This is often called the n + 1 rule.

The four splitting patterns you need
H atoms on neighbouring carbons (n)Peaks in the signal (n + 1)NameExample of the neighbour
01singletno H on the next atom, for example next to C=O or O
12doubletnext to a CH group
23tripletnext to a CH2 group
34quartetnext to a CH3 group

Three things to remember

  • Count the H atoms on the neighbouring carbon atoms. Do not count the H atoms in the group itself.
  • Equivalent hydrogen atoms do not split each other. The six H atoms of propanone give a singlet.
  • The H atom of an O–H group usually gives a singlet, and it usually does not split its neighbours. This page treats O–H in that way.

Method in words

  1. Choose one group of equivalent H atoms.
  2. Find the carbon atoms bonded directly to its carbon.
  3. Count the H atoms on those neighbouring carbon atoms. This number is n. Do not count the H of an O–H group.
  4. The signal has n + 1 peaks: 1 is a singlet, 2 a doublet, 3 a triplet, 4 a quartet.
  5. To read a spectrum, work the other way: peaks − 1 = H atoms on the neighbouring carbon atoms.

A triplet together with a quartet is the pattern of an ethyl group, CH3CH2–. The CH3 signal is a triplet because it is next to CH2. The CH2 signal is a quartet because it is next to CH3.

Predict the 1H NMR spectrum of propanoic acid

    c: O–H → singlet

Three signals with areas 3 : 2 : 1. A triplet at 0.9–1.0 ppm, a quartet at 2.0–2.5 ppm and a singlet at 9.0–13.0 ppm.

Predict the 1H NMR spectrum of ethyl ethanoate

Predict the number of signals, the ratio of areas, the chemical shift ranges and the splitting patterns in the 1H NMR spectrum of ethyl ethanoate, CH3COOCH2CH3.

  1. The two CH3 groups have different neighbours, so they are different environments.

    a: CH3 next to C=O   b: CH2 next to O   c: CH3 next to CH2 → 3 signals

  2. The areas are in the ratio of these numbers.

    a : b : c = 3 : 2 : 3

  3. The atom next to the carbon decides the row.

    a, CH3 next to the C=O of an ester: 2.0–2.5 ppm
    b, CH2 bonded to the O of an ester: 3.7–4.8 ppm
    c, CH3 on an alkyl chain: 0.9–1.0 ppm

  4. Peaks = number of H on neighbouring carbon atoms + 1.

    a: the next atom is the C of C=O, with no H, n = 0 → singlet
    b: next to CH3, n = 3 → quartet (the O on the other side has no H)
    c: next to CH2, n = 2 → triplet

Three signals with areas 3 : 2 : 3. A singlet at 2.0–2.5 ppm, a quartet at 3.7–4.8 ppm and a triplet at 0.9–1.0 ppm.

In the 1H NMR spectrum of chloroethane, CH3CH2Cl, how many peaks are in the signal of the CH2 group?

  1. Which carbon atom is next to the CH2 group, and how many H atoms does it carry?
  2. The neighbour is CH3, so n = 3. Use n + 1.
peaks = n + 1 = 3 + 1 = 4 (a quartet)

Two slips are common. The first is to use n instead of n + 1: a CH3 next to a CH2 is a triplet, not a doublet. The second is to count the H atoms on the same carbon: the three H atoms of a CH3 group do not split their own signal.

A full prediction has four parts: the number of signals, the chemical shift range of each signal, the ratio of areas, and the splitting of each signal. You can give splitting as a word (“quartet”) or as a number of peaks (“4”).

You only need singlets, doublets, triplets and quartets. You do not need patterns with more than four peaks, the relative heights of the peaks in a cluster, or coupling constants. If a group has H atoms on neighbouring carbons on both sides, its pattern can be more complex. You will not be asked to name such a pattern.

Combining the techniques

The question it answers: what is the full structure of an unknown compound?

A detective does not rely on one clue. Each technique gives a different clue about a molecule. Together they give the structure.

What each piece of data tells you
DataWhat you learn
Percentage composition by massthe empirical formula
Mass spectrum: molecular ionMr, and so the molecular formula
Mass spectrum: fragmentsgroups that break off, such as CH3 or CH2CH3
Infrared spectrumthe bonds present, and so the functional group
1H NMR: number of signalsthe number of hydrogen environments
1H NMR: areasthe ratio of H atoms in the environments
1H NMR: chemical shiftsthe type of each environment
1H NMR: splittingthe number of H atoms on the neighbouring carbon atoms

Method in words

  1. Find the empirical formula from the percentage composition. Divide each percentage by the relative atomic mass. Then divide by the smallest value.
  2. Read Mr from the molecular ion peak. Compare it with the empirical formula mass to get the molecular formula.
  3. Use the IR spectrum to find the functional group. Note what is absent as well as what is present.
  4. Use the mass lost for one or two fragments to find groups in the molecule.
  5. Use the number of NMR signals and their areas to find how many H atoms are in each environment.
  6. Use the chemical shifts and the splitting to see what each group is next to.
  7. Draw a structure. Check it against every piece of data.

A full structure problem

Compound X contains 69.70 % carbon, 11.72 % hydrogen and 18.57 % oxygen by mass. Its simplified mass spectrum, a sketch of its IR spectrum and a sketch of its 1H NMR spectrum are shown. Deduce the structure of X.

  1. Percentages by mass become amounts in mol when you divide by the relative atomic mass.

    C: 69.70 / 12.01 = 5.803 mol   H: 11.72 / 1.01 = 11.60 mol   O: 18.57 / 16.00 = 1.161 mol
    divide by 1.161 → C : H : O = 5.00 : 10.00 : 1.00
    empirical formula = C5H10O

  2. The molecular ion peak gives Mr.

    molecular ion peak at m/z = 86, so Mr = 86
    mass of C5H10O = 5(12.01) + 10(1.01) + 16.00 = 86.15
    molecular formula = C5H10O

  3. The IR spectrum shows the bonds, and so the functional group.

    1715 cm−1 is inside 1700–1750 cm−1 → C=O
    no broad absorption at 3200–3600 or 2500–3000 cm−1 → no O–H

    With one oxygen atom and a C=O bond, X is an aldehyde or a ketone.

  4. The mass lost shows a group that breaks off.

    86 − 57 = 29 → •CH2CH3 or •CHO lost

    The spectrum cannot yet tell you which one. Keep both ideas.

  5. Two signals mean two hydrogen environments. The areas share out the 10 H atoms.

    ratio 3 : 2, total 10 H → 6 H and 4 H

    There is no signal at 9.4–10.0 ppm, so there is no aldehyde H. X is a ketone, and the piece of mass 29 is •CH2CH3.

  6. The splitting shows what each group is next to.

    6 H, triplet, 1.0 ppm → two equivalent CH3 groups, each next to a CH2
    4 H, quartet, 2.4 ppm → two equivalent CH2 groups, each next to a CH3 and next to C=O (2.2–2.7 ppm)
    X is pentan-3-one, CH3CH2COCH2CH3.

    Check: the peak at m/z = 57 is CH3CH2CO+ and the peak at m/z = 29 is CH3CH2+.

Pentan-3-one, CH3CH2COCH2CH3.

A compound has the empirical formula C2H4O. Its molecular ion peak is at m/z = 88. How many carbon atoms are in one molecule?

  1. Find the mass of the empirical formula. Use whole-number masses: C 12, H 1, O 16.
  2. The empirical formula mass is 44. How many times does 44 go into 88?
2(12) + 4(1) + 16 = 44
88 / 44 = 2, so the molecular formula is C4H8O2

There are 4 carbon atoms.

Do not stop at the first structure that fits one spectrum. Several isomers often fit the IR spectrum. Check the number of NMR signals and the splitting for every structure you consider.

These questions are marked in steps. Show each deduction: the bond and its range from the IR spectrum, the molecular ion and the formula, and the NMR evidence. The final structure can be a name or any clear structural formula.

Some books calculate an “index of hydrogen deficiency” at this point. It is not required here. The IR spectrum already shows whether there is a C=O or C=C bond. You also do not need UV–visible spectra or X-ray crystallography for organic structures.

Linking it up

The syllabus asks questions that connect this topic to others. Here are short answers to two of them.

What features of a molecule determine whether it is IR active or not?

A vibration absorbs infrared radiation only if it changes the dipole moment of the molecule. A molecule with polar bonds can do this. Carbon dioxide has no overall dipole, but when it bends or stretches unevenly, a dipole appears, so it absorbs. Water and methane absorb for the same reason.

Molecules such as N2 and O2 have two identical atoms and a non-polar bond. Stretching the bond does not create a dipole. They are not IR active. Bond polarity and molecular polarity are in Structure 2.2.

What properties of a greenhouse gas determine its “global warming potential”?

Two properties matter most. The first is how strongly the gas absorbs infrared radiation, and at which wavenumbers. The second is how long the gas stays in the atmosphere before it is removed. A gas that absorbs strongly and stays for a long time has a high global warming potential. Combustion and its products are in Reactivity 1.3.
