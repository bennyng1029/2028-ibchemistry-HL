Structure 3.1

The periodic table: classification of elements

Guiding questionHow does the periodic table help us to predict patterns and trends in the properties of elements?

What you need to be able to do

Structure 3.1.1

Periods, groups and blocks

A calendar puts the days in rows. Every Monday is in the same column. The periodic table does the same thing with the elements. Elements with similar properties are in the same column.

The elements are in order of atomic number, Z. There are three ways to describe where an element is.

  • A period is a horizontal row. There are 7 periods.
  • A group is a vertical column. Groups are numbered from 1 to 18.
  • A block is a region named after the sublevel that is being filled: s, p, d or f.
The four blocks
BlockWhere it isSublevel being filledColumns wide
s-blockgroups 1 and 2, and heliums (holds 2 electrons)2
p-blockgroups 13 to 18 (not helium)p (holds 6 electrons)6
d-blockgroups 3 to 12d (holds 10 electrons)10
f-blockthe two rows drawn below the main tablef (holds 14 electrons)14

The width of each block matches the number of electrons its sublevel can hold. Sublevels are explained in Structure 1.3.

Metals, metalloids and non-metals

Most elements are metals. They are on the left and in the centre of the table. The non-metals are on the right, plus hydrogen. Between them is a diagonal band of metalloids, such as silicon. Metalloids have properties between those of metals and non-metals.

The periodic table is in section 7 of the data booklet. It shows groups 1 to 18, atomic numbers and relative atomic masses. It does not label the blocks or the metalloids, so learn where they are.

The guide does not list which elements are metalloids, and sources disagree about the edges of the band. You need the general position: a diagonal band between the metals and the non-metals. You do not need to learn a list.

Structure 3.1.2

Position and electron configuration

An element’s address in the table tells you how its electrons are arranged. This works in both directions.

The period number is the outer main energy level that is occupied by electrons. Elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons.

Valence electrons are the electrons in the outer main energy level. They decide most of the chemistry of an element. That is why elements in a group react in similar ways.

From group number to outer electrons
GroupsBlockRuleExample
1 and 2svalence electrons = group numberCa, group 2, period 4: [Ar] 4s2
13 to 18pvalence electrons = group number − 10P, group 15, period 3: [Ne] 3s2 3p3 (5 valence electrons)
3 to 12d4s and 3d electrons together = group numberFe, group 8, period 4: [Ar] 4s2 3d6 (2 + 6 = 8)

Helium is the one odd case. It has 2 valence electrons, but its outer level is full, so it is placed in group 18.

Method in words: position → configuration

  1. Find the noble gas at the end of the period above. It gives the core, for example [Ar].
  2. The period number, n, is the outer main energy level.
  3. Count along the period from group 1 to your element. Fill ns first, then (n − 1)d in period 4, then np.
  4. Check Cr and Cu. They have one 4s electron, not two (see Structure 1.3).

Configuration from position

An element is in period 4 and group 15. Deduce its condensed electron configuration and identify the element.

  1. The noble gas at the end of period 3 is argon. All its 18 electrons are inner electrons here.

    core = [Ar]

  2. Period 4 means the outer main energy level is n = 4.

    outer electrons are in 4s and 4p

  3. Groups 1 and 2 fill 4s. Groups 3 to 12 fill 3d. Groups 13, 14 and 15 put three electrons in 4p.

    4s2 (2 electrons), 3d10 (10 electrons), 4p3 (3 electrons)

  4. Check with the atomic number: 18 + 2 + 10 + 3 = 33. Element 33 is arsenic.

    [Ar] 4s2 3d10 4p3, arsenic, As

[Ar] 4s2 3d10 4p3. The element is arsenic, As. (You may also write 3d10 before 4s2.)

Method in words: configuration → position

  1. The highest main energy level in the configuration gives the period.
  2. The last sublevel being filled gives the block.
  3. Count the outer electrons to get the group, using the table above.

An atom has the configuration 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p4. Which group is the element in?

  1. The outer main energy level is n = 3. Count the electrons in 3s and 3p.
  2. There are 2 + 4 = 6 valence electrons. For the p-block, group number = valence electrons + 10.
valence electrons = 2 + 4 = 6
group = 6 + 10 = 16 (period 3: the element is sulfur)

Bromine is in period 4 and group 17. How many valence electrons does a bromine atom have?

  1. Bromine is in the p-block. Use: valence electrons = group number − 10.
  2. 17 − 10 = ?
valence electrons = 17 − 10 = 7 (4s2 4p5)

You only need to deduce configurations up to Z = 36 (krypton). For heavier elements you only need the position. Other family names, such as “alkaline earth metals” or “chalcogens”, are not required.

Structure 3.1.3

Periodicity: trends in properties

Imagine a magnet pulling on a paper clip. The pull is stronger if the magnet is stronger. It is weaker if the clip is further away, or if something is in between. The nucleus pulls on the outer electrons in the same way.

Periodicity means that the trends in properties repeat across each period and down each group. You explain almost every trend with the same three ideas.

  • Nuclear charge. More protons give a stronger attraction.
  • Shielding. Electrons in inner energy levels repel the outer electrons. This cancels part of the attraction.
  • Distance. Electrons in a higher main energy level are further from the nucleus, so the attraction is weaker.
What changes in each direction
Across a period (left to right)Down a group
Nuclear chargeincreasesincreases
Shielding by inner electronsstays about the sameincreases
Outer electronssame main energy levelhigher main energy level, further away
Attraction of the nucleus for the outer electronsstrongerweaker

Down a group the nuclear charge also increases. But the extra distance and the extra shielding matter more, so the attraction gets weaker.

A negative electron affinity means that energy is released when the atom gains an electron. Chlorine has the value −349 kJ mol−1. Sodium has −53 kJ mol−1. Chlorine attracts an extra electron much more strongly.

First ionization energy, electron affinity and electronegativity are in section 9 of the data booklet. Atomic and ionic radii are in section 10. Radii are given in 10−12 m, which is the picometre, pm. The atomic radii are covalent radii.

Explaining a trend in atomic radius

The atomic radius of sodium is 160 pm and the atomic radius of chlorine is 100 pm. Explain why the atomic radius decreases across period 3. [2]

  1. The first mark is for the cause. Across a period, each element has one more proton.

    Na has 11 protons. Cl has 17 protons. The nuclear charge increases.

  2. The second mark is for showing that nothing cancels the extra attraction.

    The outer electrons are in the same main energy level (n = 3), so the shielding by inner electrons is about the same.

  3. Finish with the effect on the size of the atom.

    The outer electrons are attracted more strongly and pulled closer to the nucleus.

Nuclear charge increases across the period. The outer electrons are in the same energy level with similar shielding, so they are attracted more strongly and the radius decreases.

Ionic radius

Two rules cover most questions.

  • A positive ion is smaller than its atom. Sodium is 160 pm, but Na+ is 102 pm. The atom has lost its whole outer energy level.
  • A negative ion is larger than its atom. Chlorine is 100 pm, but Cl- is 181 pm. The nuclear charge is the same, but there is one more electron. The electrons repel each other more and spread out.

Some ions have exactly the same electron configuration. Then only the nuclear charge is different.

Comparing ionic radii

Ionic radii: K+ 138 pm, Cl- 181 pm. The atomic radius of chlorine is 100 pm. Explain (a) why Cl- is larger than the chlorine atom, and (b) why K+ is smaller than Cl-. [3]

  1. Atom and ion have the same nucleus. Only the number of electrons is different.

    Cl: 17 protons, 17 electrons. Cl−: 17 protons, 18 electrons.

  2. The same nuclear charge now holds one more electron.

    There is more repulsion between the electrons in the outer level, so they spread further from the nucleus.

  3. Check whether the two ions have the same number of electrons.

    K+ and Cl− both have 18 electrons: 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6

  4. Same electrons and same shielding, so the nuclear charge decides the size.

    K+ has 19 protons and Cl− has 17. The greater nuclear charge attracts the same number of electrons more strongly, so K+ is smaller.

(a) Same nuclear charge, one more electron, more repulsion between electrons. (b) Same electron configuration, but K+ has the greater nuclear charge.

Explaining a trend in first ionization energy

First ionization energies: Na 496 kJ mol−1, K 419 kJ mol−1. Explain why the value for potassium is lower. [2]

  1. Ionization energy is about one electron: the outer one.

    Na: [Ne] 3s1, electron removed from n = 3. K: [Ar] 4s1, electron removed from n = 4.

  2. This is the mark-earning comparison. Write about energy levels, not only about “size”.

    In K the outer electron is in a higher main energy level. It is further from the nucleus and shielded by more inner electrons.

  3. Weaker attraction means less energy is needed.

    The outer electron in K is attracted less strongly, so less energy is needed to remove it.

The electron removed from K is in a higher energy level, further from the nucleus and more shielded. The attraction is weaker, even though K has more protons.

“The atomic radius increases” on its own does not earn the first mark for an ionization energy question. Write about the energy level of the electron, its distance from the nucleus and the shielding. Never write that shielding increases across a period.

Explaining a trend in electronegativity

Electronegativity values: S 2.6, Cl 3.2, F 4.0. Explain (a) why chlorine is more electronegative than sulfur, and (b) why fluorine is more electronegative than chlorine. [4]

  1. Electronegativity is about attracting a shared pair of electrons in a bond.

    Ask: how strongly does each nucleus attract a bonding pair?

  2. S and Cl are both in period 3. Use the “across a period” reasons.

    Cl has a greater nuclear charge (17 protons, S has 16). The shielding is about the same.
    The chlorine nucleus attracts the shared pair more strongly.

  3. F and Cl are both in group 17. Use the “down a group” reasons.

    F has fewer occupied energy levels. A shared pair is closer to the nucleus and less shielded.
    The fluorine nucleus attracts the shared pair more strongly.

(a) Greater nuclear charge with similar shielding. (b) The bonding pair is closer to the nucleus and less shielded in fluorine.

Do not write “electron affinity increases”. The values are negative, so “increases” is unclear. Write “becomes more negative” or “more energy is released”. The data are also not smooth: fluorine (−328) is less negative than chlorine (−349).

The term “effective nuclear charge” and rules for calculating it are not in the guide. Melting point trends are not part of this section. The small dips in first ionization energy across a period are Additional HL.

Structure 3.1.4

Trends down group 1 and group 17

Metals react by losing electrons. Non-metals react by gaining electrons. So the trends in the last section let you predict how reactive an element is.

  • Metallic character is the tendency to lose electrons and form positive ions. It increases down group 1.
  • Non-metallic character is the tendency to gain electrons. It decreases down group 17.

Group 1 metals with water

Every group 1 metal reacts with water. The products are a metal hydroxide and hydrogen gas.

2M(s) + 2H2O(l) -> 2MOH(aq) + H2(g)
M
any group 1 metal: Li, Na, K, Rb or Cs

Observations that earn marks: the metal floats, fizzes (bubbles of gas), moves on the surface, and gets smaller or dissolves. The solution becomes alkaline. For the trend, link reactivity to how easily the outer electron is lost.

Group 17 elements with halide ions

A halide ion is the 1− ion of a halogen, such as Cl-. A more reactive halogen takes electrons from the halide ion of a less reactive halogen. This is called a displacement reaction.

Reactivity decreases down group 17: chlorine is more reactive than bromine, and bromine is more reactive than iodine.

Take care with the names. Chlorine, Cl2, is the element. Chloride, Cl-, is the ion. “Chlorine displaces bromide ions” is correct. “Chloride displaces bromine” is wrong.

Only these two sets of reactions are required here. You do not need group 1 metals with oxygen or halogens, tests with silver nitrate, or group 2 trends. Explaining displacement with oxidation and reduction comes later in Reactivity 3.2.

Structure 3.1.5

Oxides: from basic to acidic

There is no sharp line between metals and non-metals. The properties change gradually across a period. You can see this clearly in the oxides.

  • Metal oxides are basic. If they dissolve in water, they form alkaline solutions.
  • Non-metal oxides are acidic. If they dissolve in water, they form acidic solutions.
  • Between them are amphoteric oxides. An amphoteric oxide can react as a base (with an acid) and as an acid (with a base). Aluminium oxide is the usual example.

Amphoteric does not mean neutral. A neutral substance reacts with neither acids nor bases. An amphoteric oxide reacts with both.

Equations you must be able to write

You need equations for the reaction with water of four kinds of oxide. The patterns are short.

Oxides with water

a group 2 metalbasic oxide + water → metal hydroxideCaO(s) + H2O(l) -> Ca(OH)2(aq)
carbonacidic oxide + water → acidCO2(g) + H2O(l) <=> H2CO3(aq)
sulfuracidic oxide + water → acidSO2(g) + H2O(l) <=> H2SO3(aq)

The acids are carbonic acid, H2CO3, sulfurous acid, H2SO3, and sulfuric acid, H2SO4. To get the formula of the acid, add the atoms of the oxide and the water together.

Equations for oxides with water

Deduce the equation, with state symbols, for the reaction with water of (a) potassium oxide, (b) barium oxide and (c) sulfur dioxide.

  1. Potassium is in group 1, so its ion is K+. The oxide is K2O and the hydroxide is KOH.

    K2O + H2O → KOH (not balanced)

  2. There are two K atoms on the left, so you need 2KOH. Then check H and O.

    K2O(s) + H2O(l) → 2KOH(aq)

  3. Barium is in group 2, so its ion is Ba2+. The oxide is BaO and the hydroxide is Ba(OH)2. It is already balanced.

    BaO(s) + H2O(l) → Ba(OH)2(aq)

  4. Add the atoms of SO2 and H2O to get the acid: H2SO3. The reaction is reversible.

    SO2(g) + H2O(l) ⇌ H2SO3(aq)

(a) K2O(s) + H2O(l) → 2KOH(aq). (b) BaO(s) + H2O(l) → Ba(OH)2(aq). (c) SO2(g) + H2O(l) ⇌ H2SO3(aq).

If the question asks for state symbols, they are part of the mark. Check that the equation is balanced. Remember that a group 2 hydroxide has two hydroxide ions: Ca(OH)2.

Acid rain and ocean acidification

You do not need equations for oxides of nitrogen, phosphorus, silicon or chlorine with water, or for amphoteric oxides. You do not need methods for reducing acid rain. What pH means, and how acids react, comes later in Reactivity 3.1.

Structure 3.1.6

Oxidation states

Chemists need a way to keep count of electrons when atoms bond. Think of it as bookkeeping: each atom gets a number that shows how many electrons it has “lost” or “gained”.

The oxidation state is a number assigned to an atom to show the number of electrons transferred in forming a bond. It is the charge that the atom would have if the compound were composed of ions.

In sodium chloride the ions are real: Na+ is +1 and Cl- is −1. In water the bonds are covalent, but you still pretend. Oxygen is more electronegative, so it is given both electrons of each bond. Oxygen is −2 and each hydrogen is +1.

Write the sign first, then the number: +2, −1. Writing “2+” or “2” does not earn the mark. The form 2+ is for the charge on an ion, such as Mg^2+. The terms “oxidation state” and “oxidation number” are both accepted.

The rules

Rules for oxidation states, in order of use
RuleStatementExample
1An atom in an element is 0.Cl2, Na, O2: all 0
2The oxidation states in a neutral compound add up to 0.H2O: 2(+1) + (−2) = 0
3The oxidation states in an ion add up to the charge on the ion.Mg^2+ is +2; in OH-: (−2) + (+1) = −1
4Fluorine is always −1 in compounds.HF: F is −1
5Group 1 metals are always +1 and group 2 metals are always +2 in compounds.NaCl: Na is +1; CaO: Ca is +2
6Hydrogen is +1. Exception: −1 in metal hydrides.HCl: +1; NaH: −1
7Oxygen is −2. Exception: −1 in peroxides.H2O: −2; H2O2: −1

The rules higher in the table win. That is how you find the exceptions without memorizing them. In NaH, sodium must be +1 (rule 5), so hydrogen must be −1.

Why is an element zero?

In an element, all the atoms are the same. They attract the bonding electrons equally, so no electrons are transferred from one atom to another. The oxidation state is 0.

Method in words

  1. Write down what the oxidation states must add up to: 0 for a compound, the charge for an ion.
  2. Fill in the atoms you know from the rules.
  3. Call the unknown oxidation state x. Multiply each value by the number of atoms.
  4. Solve for x. Write the answer with its sign first.

A simple compound

Deduce the oxidation state of iron in Fe2O3, and name the compound.

  1. It is a neutral compound.

    sum of oxidation states = 0

  2. Oxygen is −2, and there are three oxygen atoms.

    3 × (−2) = −6

  3. There are two iron atoms, so use 2x.

    2x + (−6) = 0
    2x = +6, so x = +3

  4. Iron can have more than one oxidation state, so the name shows which one with a Roman numeral.

    iron(III) oxide

The oxidation state of iron is +3. The compound is iron(III) oxide.

A polyatomic ion

Deduce the oxidation state of chromium in the dichromate ion, Cr2O7^2-.

  1. It is an ion, so the oxidation states add up to its charge.

    sum of oxidation states = −2

  2. Oxygen is −2, and there are seven oxygen atoms.

    7 × (−2) = −14

  3. There are two chromium atoms, so use 2x. Do not forget the charge on the right.

    2x + (−14) = −2
    2x = +12, so x = +6

The oxidation state of chromium is +6. The systematic name of the ion is dichromate(VI).

The two exceptions

Deduce the oxidation state of (a) oxygen in barium peroxide, BaO2, and (b) hydrogen in lithium hydride, LiH.

  1. Barium is a group 2 metal. Rule 5 is higher in the table than the oxygen rule, so use it first.

    Ba = +2

  2. The compound is neutral and has two oxygen atoms.

    (+2) + 2x = 0
    2x = −2, so x = −1

  3. Lithium is a group 1 metal, so it is +1. This is a metal hydride.

    Li = +1

  4. The compound is neutral.

    (+1) + x = 0, so x = −1

(a) Oxygen is −1 (a peroxide). (b) Hydrogen is −1 (a metal hydride).

Deduce the oxidation state of nitrogen in nitric acid, HNO3.

  1. Hydrogen is +1 and each oxygen is −2. The total is 0.
  2. (+1) + x + 3(−2) = 0. Solve for x.
(+1) + x + 3(−2) = 0
x = +6 − 1 = +5

Deduce the oxidation state of oxygen in sodium peroxide, Na2O2.

  1. Sodium is a group 1 metal, so it is always +1. Start there, not with oxygen.
  2. 2(+1) + 2x = 0. Solve for x.
2(+1) + 2x = 0
2x = −2, so x = −1

Deduce the oxidation state of manganese in the manganate(VII) ion, MnO4-. Do the calculation, then check it against the name.

  1. The total equals the charge on the ion: −1. Each oxygen is −2.
  2. x + 4(−2) = −1. Solve for x.
x + 4(−2) = −1
x = −1 + 8 = +7, which matches the Roman numeral VII

Do not give the charge of the whole ion as the answer. In SO4^2- the ion has a charge of 2−, but sulfur is +6. Also remember to divide by the number of atoms: in Cr2O7^2- the two chromium atoms total +12, so each one is +6.

Structure 3.1.7

Discontinuities in first ionization energy

First ionization energy increases across a period. But the increase is not smooth. A discontinuity is a place where the value falls when you expect it to rise. Each of periods 2 and 3 has two.

The two discontinuities (data booklet values in kJ mol−1)
WherePeriod 2Period 3Reason
group 2 → group 13Be 900, B 801Mg 738, Al 578The electron removed from the group 13 atom is in a p sublevel. The p sublevel is higher in energy than the s sublevel of the same main energy level. An electron that is higher in energy needs less energy to remove.
group 15 → group 16N 1402, O 1314P 1012, S 1000In the group 16 atom, the electron removed is in a p orbital that holds two electrons. The two electrons in the same orbital repel each other. This extra repulsion makes one of them easier to remove.

P 3p ↑↑↑ S 3p ↑↓↑↑

Why this is evidence for sublevels. If each main energy level were one single level, first ionization energy would rise steadily across a period. The fall from group 2 to group 13 shows that the electrons in one main energy level do not all have the same energy. The level is divided into sublevels: s, then p at a higher energy.

You can explore every value from hydrogen to krypton in the first ionization energy explorer in Structure 1.3. That page also has the general trends in more detail.

Explaining a discontinuity

First ionization energies: Mg 738 kJ mol−1, Al 578 kJ mol−1. Aluminium has the greater nuclear charge. Explain why its first ionization energy is lower. [2]

  1. You need to know which sublevel each electron comes from.

    Mg: [Ne] 3s2 Al: [Ne] 3s2 3p1

  2. The first mark is for naming the two sublevels.

    Mg loses a 3s electron. Al loses a 3p electron.

  3. The second mark is for the energy of the electron removed.

    The 3p sublevel is higher in energy than the 3s sublevel, so less energy is needed to remove the 3p electron.

The electron removed from Al is in the 3p sublevel, which is higher in energy than the 3s sublevel that Mg loses an electron from. Less energy is needed to remove it.

Base your answer on the energy of the electron that is removed. Do not write that a full or half-full sublevel is “specially stable”, or that removing the electron “leaves a complete sublevel”. The guide rules this out and it does not earn marks.

Structure 3.1.8

Properties of transition elements

Iron, copper, nickel and chromium are metals you see every day. They are strong, they melt at high temperatures, and their compounds have bright colours. They are all in the d-block.

A transition element has an incomplete d sublevel. This gives it characteristic properties.

Six properties to recognize
PropertyWhat it meansWhere it is explained
Variable oxidation stateOne element forms ions with different charges, such as Fe^2+ and Fe^3+.next section
High melting pointsCompared with the metals of groups 1 and 2.Structure 2.3
Magnetic propertiesMany transition elements and their compounds are attracted by a magnet.no explanation needed
Catalytic propertiesThe elements and their compounds can act as catalysts.Reactivity 2.2
Coloured compoundsFor example, copper(II) sulfate solution is blue.last section of this page
Complex ions with ligandsThe metal ion bonds to molecules or ions around it.below, and Reactivity 3.4

The command term here is “recognize”. A typical question gives a list of properties and asks which ones are characteristic of transition elements. “Low melting point” and “colourless compounds” are common wrong options.

Complex ions and ligands

A ligand is a molecule or ion that donates a pair of electrons to a transition element ion. The bond that forms is a coordination bond. Water, ammonia and the chloride ion can all be ligands, because each has a lone pair.

A complex ion is a central metal ion with ligands bonded to it. An example is [Cu(H2O)6]2+: one Cu^2+ ion with six water ligands.

Scandium and zinc

Scandium and zinc are in the d-block, but they do not behave like typical transition elements.

The d sublevel in scandium and zinc
ElementAtomCommon ionResult
Sc[Ar] 4s2 3d1Sc3+: [Ar]The ion has no d electrons. Its compounds are colourless and it has one oxidation state in compounds.
Zn[Ar] 4s2 3d10Zn2+: [Ar] 3d10The d sublevel is full in the atom and in the ion. Its compounds are colourless and it has one oxidation state in compounds.

The guide asks you to think about scandium. An argument for calling it a transition element: the atom has an incomplete d sublevel (3d1), and it is a d-block metal with a high melting point. An argument against: its only common ion, Sc^3+, has no d electrons, so it does not show the characteristic properties.

Zinc has a full d sublevel as an atom and as an ion. It does not fit the definition, so it is usually not counted as a transition element. The guide does not mention zinc by name.

Types of magnetism (paramagnetism, diamagnetism) are not assessed. You do not need to name complexes, know their shapes, classify ligands, or learn specific catalysts.

Structure 3.1.9

Variable oxidation states and ion configurations

Calcium always forms Ca^2+. Iron forms Fe^2+ and Fe^3+. Why can iron choose and calcium cannot? The answer is in the successive ionization energies.

Successive ionization energies are the energies needed to remove the first electron, then the second, then the third, and so on. They are introduced in Structure 1.3.

In a transition element, the 4s and 3d electrons are close in energy. So the successive ionization energies are close in value, with no large jump between them. Different numbers of electrons can be lost for a similar energy cost. This is why transition elements have variable oxidation states.

For calcium, the third ionization energy (4912 kJ mol−1) is about 4.3 times the second (1145 kJ mol−1). The third electron comes from an inner energy level. For iron, the third value (2957 kJ mol−1) is about 1.9 times the second (1562 kJ mol−1).

The mark-earning phrase is: “successive ionization energies are close in value”. You may add that the 4s and 3d sublevels are close in energy. “The d electrons are delocalized” is not the reason.

Electron configurations of the ions

When a first-row transition element atom forms a positive ion, the 4s electrons are removed before any 3d electrons. Structure 1.3 explains why: once 3d contains electrons, 4s is the higher of the two sublevels. See Ions of the d-block metals.

Method in words

  1. Write the configuration of the atom. Remember Cr is [Ar] 4s1 3d5 and Cu is [Ar] 4s1 3d10.
  2. Remove the 4s electrons first.
  3. If more electrons must go, remove them from 3d.
  4. Check: the number of electrons removed equals the charge.

Configurations of transition element ions

Deduce the condensed electron configurations of (a) Mn^2+, (b) Cr^3+ and (c) Cu^2+.

  1. Start from the atom every time. Cr and Cu are the two exceptions with one 4s electron.

    Mn: [Ar] 4s2 3d5 Cr: [Ar] 4s1 3d5 Cu: [Ar] 4s1 3d10

  2. Both come from 4s.

    Mn2+: [Ar] 3d5

  3. Chromium has only one 4s electron. It goes first. The other two come from 3d.

    Cr3+: [Ar] 3d3

  4. Copper has one 4s electron. It goes first. The second comes from 3d.

    Cu2+: [Ar] 3d9

(a) [Ar] 3d5. (b) [Ar] 3d3. (c) [Ar] 3d9.

How many 3d electrons does a Co^3+ ion have? (Cobalt is Z = 27.)

  1. The cobalt atom is [Ar] 4s2 3d7. Three electrons must be removed.
  2. Two come from 4s. The third comes from 3d.
Co: [Ar] 4s2 3d7 → Co3+: [Ar] 3d6

How many 3d electrons does a V^3+ ion have? (Vanadium is Z = 23.)

  1. The vanadium atom is [Ar] 4s2 3d3.
  2. Remove two electrons from 4s, then one from 3d.
V: [Ar] 4s2 3d3 → V3+: [Ar] 3d2

The common error is to remove 3d electrons first, which gives [Ar] 4s2 3d4 for Fe^2+. The correct answer is [Ar] 3d6. The 4s sublevel fills first, and it also empties first.

You do not need to memorize which oxidation states each transition element has, or the colours of particular ions.

Structure 3.1.10

Why transition element complexes are coloured

A red filter on a torch does not add red light. It removes the other colours and lets red through. A coloured solution works in the same way: it removes part of the white light, and you see what is left.

The explanation, step by step

  1. The transition element ion has a partially filled d sublevel.
  2. In a complex ion, the ligands cause the d sublevel to split into two sets of orbitals with different energies.
  3. An electron in the lower set absorbs light and is promoted to the higher set.
  4. The energy gap between the two sets matches the energy of light in the visible region. So one colour of visible light is absorbed.
  5. The colour you observe is complementary to the colour absorbed. It is the light that passes through.

Complementary colours are opposite each other on the colour wheel.

The colour wheel is in section 15 of the data booklet. The equation c = λf is in section 1 and the speed of light is in section 2. You do not need to memorize any of them.

The complex does not emit the colour you see. It absorbs the complementary colour, and the rest of the white light passes through. Writing “the electron falls back and emits blue light” is wrong.

Coloured and colourless ions

A solution containing the complex ion [Cu(H2O)6]2+ is blue. Solutions of zinc compounds are colourless. Explain both observations. [5]

  1. Colour depends on the d electrons of the ion, not of the atom.

    Cu2+: [Ar] 3d9 Zn2+: [Ar] 3d10

  2. Start with the feature that makes colour possible.

    Cu2+ has a partially filled d sublevel.

  3. Say what the ligands do.

    The water ligands cause the d sublevel to split into two sets of orbitals with different energies.

  4. You must mention that light is absorbed. Without this, the complementary colour point does not score.

    An electron absorbs visible light (orange) and is promoted from the lower set to the higher set.
    The colour observed, blue, is complementary to the colour absorbed.

  5. Compare with the ion that has no space in its d sublevel.

    Zn2+ has a full d sublevel (3d10), so no electron can be promoted between d orbitals. No visible light is absorbed.

Cu2+ (3d9) has a partially filled d sublevel, split by the ligands. An electron is promoted by absorbing orange light, and the complementary colour, blue, is seen. Zn2+ (3d10) has a full d sublevel, so no promotion is possible.

The same reasoning explains Sc^3+. It has no d electrons at all, so there is no electron to promote. Its compounds are colourless.

Calculations with c = λf

c = λf
c
speed of light, 3.00 × 108 m s-1
λ
wavelength m
f
frequency Hz (the same as s-1)

Method in words

  1. Decide whether you were given the light absorbed or the colour observed.
  2. If you have a wavelength in nm, convert it to metres: multiply by 10−9.
  3. Rearrange: f = c / λ or λ = c / f.
  4. To use the colour wheel, convert a wavelength in metres back to nm: divide by 10−9.
  5. Find the colour on the wheel. The complementary colour is directly opposite.

Colour from the frequency absorbed

A complex ion absorbs light of frequency 5.00 × 1014 Hz. Calculate the wavelength of this light in nm. Then use the colour wheel to deduce the colour absorbed and the colour of the complex.

  1. You know c and f, and you want λ.

    λ = c / f

  2. Hz is the same as s−1, so the seconds cancel and the answer is in metres.

    λ = (3.00 × 108 m s−1) / (5.00 × 1014 s−1) = 6.00 × 10−7 m

  3. The colour wheel is in nm. 1 nm = 10−9 m, so divide by 10−9.

    λ = 6.00 × 10−7 m / 10−9 m nm−1 = 600 nm

  4. Find the sector that contains 600 nm, then go straight across the wheel.

    600 nm is between 585 nm and 647 nm: orange is absorbed.
    Opposite orange is blue: the complex is blue.

λ = 600 nm (3 significant figures, like the data). Orange light is absorbed, so the complex appears blue.

A complex ion absorbs light of wavelength 450 nm. Calculate the frequency of this light.

  1. Convert the wavelength to metres first: 450 nm = 450 × 10−9 m = 4.50 × 10−7 m.
  2. Use f = c / λ with c = 3.00 × 108 m s−1.
λ = 450 nm × 10−9 m nm−1 = 4.50 × 10−7 m
f = (3.00 × 108 m s−1) / (4.50 × 10−7 m) = 6.67 × 1014 Hz
450 nm is blue light, so this complex appears orange.

A complex ion absorbs light of frequency 7.25 × 1014 Hz. Calculate the wavelength absorbed, in nm. Then decide what colour the complex is.

  1. Use λ = c / f. The answer comes out in metres.
  2. λ = (3.00 × 108) / (7.25 × 1014) m. Then divide by 10−9 to get nm.
λ = (3.00 × 108 m s−1) / (7.25 × 1014 s−1) = 4.138 × 10−7 m
λ = 4.138 × 10−7 m / 10−9 m nm−1 = 414 nm
414 nm is violet (400 to 424 nm). Opposite violet is yellow: the complex is yellow.

The most common slip is to put a wavelength in nm straight into c = λf. The speed of light is in metres per second, so the wavelength must be in metres. Your frequency should be close to 1014 Hz to 1015 Hz for visible light. If it is not, check the units.

For “what colour is absorbed?” examiners accept the colour name, the wavelength range from the wheel, or a wavelength inside the range. The complementary colour point only scores if you also say that light is absorbed.

You do not need d-orbital splitting diagrams for different shapes, crystal field theory, or the spectrochemical series. The data booklet has no spectrochemical series. If a question compares two complexes, it will give you the information you need.

Linking it up

The syllabus asks questions that connect this topic to others. Here are short answers to some of them.

How has the organization of elements in the periodic table helped the discovery of new elements?

When the elements are arranged by their properties, gaps appear. A gap predicts an element that nobody has found yet. Its position also predicts its properties, so chemists know what to look for. Gallium and germanium were found in this way. Today, new elements are made in laboratories to continue period 7.

Why are simulations often used to explore the reactivity of group 1 and group 17 elements?

Some of the reactions are too dangerous for a school laboratory. Rubidium and caesium react explosively with water, and the halogens are toxic. A simulation or a video lets you compare all the elements safely and repeat the comparison as often as you like.

How do differences in bonding explain the properties of metal and non-metal oxides?

Metal oxides are ionic. They contain oxide ions, O^2-, which react with water to form hydroxide ions. So the solution is alkaline. Non-metal oxides are covalent molecules. They react with water to form acids, which release hydrogen ions. Ionic oxides also have high melting points, and many molecular oxides are gases.

How can oxidation states be used to analyse redox reactions?

If the oxidation state of an atom increases during a reaction, that atom has been oxidized. If it decreases, the atom has been reduced. You will use this in Reactivity 3.2.

What is the nature of the reaction between transition element ions and ligands?

The ligand donates a pair of electrons and the metal ion accepts it. A coordination bond forms. You will describe this as a Lewis acid–base reaction in Reactivity 3.4.

How can colorimetry be used to find the concentration of a solution of coloured ions?

A colorimeter measures how much light a solution absorbs. A more concentrated solution absorbs more light. You measure the absorbance of several solutions of known concentration and plot a calibration curve. Then you read the unknown concentration from the curve. You choose light of the complementary colour, because that is the light the solution absorbs most strongly.
