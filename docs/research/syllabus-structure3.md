## 2. Understandings, outcomes, guidance and linking questions

### Structure 3.1 — The periodic table: Classification of elements

**Guiding question:** How does the periodic table help us to predict patterns and trends in the properties
of elements?
SL+HL 7 hours. AHL 4 hours.

#### Structure 3.1.1 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** The periodic table consists of periods, groups and blocks.
- **Outcome:** Identify the positions of metals, metalloids and non-metals in the periodic table.
- **Guidance:** The four blocks associated with the sublevels s, p, d, f should be recognized. A copy of the
  periodic table is available in the data booklet.
- **Linking questions:** none listed.

#### Structure 3.1.2 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** The period number shows the outer energy level that is occupied by electrons. Elements
  in a group have a common number of valence electrons.
- **Outcome:** Deduce the electron configuration of an atom **up to Z = 36** from the element's position in
  the periodic table, and vice versa.
- **Guidance:** Groups are numbered from 1 to 18. The classifications "alkali metals", "halogens",
  "transition elements" and "noble gases" should be known.
- **Linking question:**
  - Nature of science, Structure 1.2 — How has the organization of elements in the periodic table
    facilitated the discovery of new elements?

#### Structure 3.1.3 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Periodicity refers to trends in properties of elements across a period and down a
  group.
- **Outcome:** Explain the periodicity of **atomic radius, ionic radius, ionization energy, electron
  affinity and electronegativity**.
- **Guidance:** none.
- **Linking questions:** none listed.

#### Structure 3.1.4 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Trends in properties of elements down a group include the increasing metallic character
  of group 1 elements and the decreasing non-metallic character of group 17 elements.
- **Outcome:** Describe and explain the reactions of **group 1 metals with water**, and of **group 17
  elements with halide ions**.
- **Guidance:** none.
- **Linking question:**
  - Inquiry 2, Tool 2 — Why are simulations often used in exploring the trends in chemical reactivity of
    group 1 and group 17 elements?

#### Structure 3.1.5 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Metallic and non-metallic properties show a continuum. This includes the trend from
  basic metal oxides, through amphoteric, to acidic non-metal oxides.
- **Outcome:** Deduce equations for the reactions with water of the oxides of **group 1 and group 2 metals,
  carbon and sulfur**.
- **Guidance:** Include acid rain caused by gaseous non-metal oxides, and ocean acidification caused by
  increasing CO₂ levels.
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 2.1, 2.2 — How do differences in bonding explain the differences in the properties of metal
    and non-metal oxides?

#### Structure 3.1.6 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** The oxidation state is a number assigned to an atom to show the number of electrons
  transferred in forming a bond. It is the charge that atom would have if the compound were composed of
  ions.
- **Outcomes:** Deduce the oxidation states of an atom in an ion or a compound. Explain why the oxidation
  state of an element is zero.
- **Guidance:** Oxidation states are shown with a + or – sign followed by the Arabic numeral, e.g. +2, –1.
  Examples should include hydrogen in metal hydrides (–1) and oxygen in peroxides (–1). The terms
  "oxidation number" and "oxidation state" are often used interchangeably, and either is acceptable in
  assessment. Naming conventions for oxyanions use oxidation numbers shown with Roman numerals, but generic
  names persist and are acceptable; examples include NO₃⁻ nitrate, NO₂⁻ nitrite, SO₄²⁻ sulfate, SO₃²⁻
  sulfite.
- **Linking question:**
  - Reactivity 3.2 — How can oxidation states be used to analyse redox reactions?

#### Structure 3.1.7 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Discontinuities occur in the trend of increasing first ionization energy across a
  period.
- **Outcome:** Explain how these discontinuities provide evidence for the existence of energy sublevels.
- **Guidance:** Explanations should be based on the energy of the electron removed, rather than on the
  "special stability" of filled and half-filled sublevels.
- **Linking questions:** none listed.

#### Structure 3.1.8 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Transition elements have incomplete d-sublevels that give them characteristic
  properties.
- **Outcome:** Recognize properties, including: variable oxidation state, high melting points, magnetic
  properties, catalytic properties, formation of coloured compounds, and formation of complex ions with
  ligands.
- **Guidance:** Knowledge of different types of magnetism will not be assessed.
- **Linking question:**
  - Nature of science, Structure 2.3 — What are the arguments for and against including scandium as a
    transition element?

#### Structure 3.1.9 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** The formation of variable oxidation states in transition elements can be explained by
  the fact that their successive ionization energies are close in value.
- **Outcome:** Deduce the electron configurations of ions of the first-row transition elements.
- **Guidance / linking questions:** none listed.

#### Structure 3.1.10 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Transition element complexes are coloured due to the absorption of light when an
  electron is promoted between the orbitals in the split d-sublevels. The colour absorbed is complementary
  to the colour observed.
- **Outcome:** Apply the colour wheel to deduce the wavelengths and frequencies of light absorbed and/or
  observed.
- **Guidance:** Students are **not expected to know the different splitting patterns and their relation to
  the coordination number**. The colour wheel and the equation c = λf are given in the data booklet.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Reactivity 3.4 — What is the nature of the reaction between transition element ions and ligands in
    forming complex ions?
  - Tool 1, Inquiry 2 — How can colorimetry or spectrophotometry be used to calculate the concentration of a
    solution of coloured ions?

Boundary notes for 3.1:

- **Ionization energy appears three times.** General trend across a period and down a group: **3.1.3
  (SL+HL)** — "explain the periodicity of … ionization energy". Discontinuities (group 2 → 13 and
  15 → 16): **AHL**, both in 3.1.7 and in Structure 1.3.6. Successive IEs → group: Structure 1.3.7 (AHL).
  So an SL+HL periodicity section explains only the *general* IE trends; the dips are HL.
- **Explaining trends.** The guide does not prescribe the explanatory vocabulary. The words "shielding" and
  "effective nuclear charge" do not occur in the guide; the standard explanation uses nuclear charge,
  number of occupied energy levels (distance) and shielding by inner electrons **[UNVERIFIED as required
  wording]**.
- **Melting point is not in the 3.1.3 list.** Melting-point trends belong to Structure 2.3.2 (s- and p-block
  metals), 2.3.3 (AHL, transition elements) and linking questions about period 3 chlorides and oxides.
  **Metallic character** trends are in 3.1.4 and 3.1.5.
- **Oxides in 3.1.5.** Equations are required only for oxides of group 1 and group 2 metals, carbon and
  sulfur reacting with water. "Amphoteric" is in the understanding but no amphoteric oxide is named and no
  equation for one is required; Al₂O₃ is the standard example **[UNVERIFIED as named example]**. Oxides of
  nitrogen enter only through "acid rain caused by gaseous non-metal oxides" and a Reactivity 3.1 linking
  question. Period 3 oxides as a full set (Na₂O … Cl₂O₇) appear only in a Structure 2.4 linking question.
- **Oxidation states are SL+HL** (3.1.6), including the hydride and peroxide exceptions. Only the
  *explanation* of variable oxidation states in transition elements (3.1.9) is AHL. SL+HL students still
  meet transition element ions of different charge (Structure 2.1.1) and variable oxidation states as redox
  examples (Reactivity 3.2.1).
- **3.1.8 is "recognize" only.** Explanations sit elsewhere: high melting point and conductivity — Structure
  2.3.3; variable oxidation states — 3.1.9; colour — 3.1.10; complex ions — Structure 2.2.3 (AHL rider) and
  Reactivity 3.4.8; catalysis — a linking question from Reactivity 2.2.5 only. Magnetism has no explanation
  outcome and types of magnetism are not assessed.
- **Group 17 "with halide ions"** means displacement reactions (a more reactive halogen oxidizes the halide
  of a less reactive one). The same chemistry appears as "relative ease of reduction of halogens" in
  Reactivity 3.2.3.

### Structure 3.2 — Functional groups: Classification of organic compounds

**Guiding question:** How does the classification of organic molecules help us to predict their properties?
SL+HL 9 hours. AHL 11 hours.

#### Structure 3.2.1 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Organic compounds can be represented by different types of formulas. These include
  empirical, molecular, structural (full and condensed), stereochemical and skeletal.
- **Outcomes:** Identify different formulas and interconvert molecular, skeletal and structural formulas.
  Construct 3D models (real or virtual) of organic molecules.
- **Guidance:** Stereochemical formulas are not expected to be drawn, except where specifically indicated.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Structure 2.2 — What is unique about carbon that enables it to form more compounds than the sum of all
    the other elements' compounds?
  - Nature of science, Structure 2.2 — What are the advantages and disadvantages of different depictions of
    an organic compound (e.g. structural formula, stereochemical formula, skeletal formula, 3D models)?

#### Structure 3.2.2 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Functional groups give characteristic physical and chemical properties to a compound.
  Organic compounds are divided into classes according to the functional groups present in their molecules.
- **Outcome:** Identify the following functional groups by name and structure: **halogeno, hydroxyl,
  carbonyl, carboxyl, alkoxy, amino, amido, ester, phenyl**.
- **Guidance:** The terms "saturated" and "unsaturated" should be included.
- **Linking questions:**
  - AHL Structure 2.4 — What is the nature of the reaction that occurs when two amino acids form a
    dipeptide?
  - Nature of science, Reactivity 3.2, 3.4 — How can functional group reactivity be used to determine a
    reaction pathway between compounds, e.g. converting ethene into ethanoic acid?

#### Structure 3.2.3 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** A homologous series is a family of compounds in which successive members differ by a
  common structural unit, typically CH₂. Each homologous series can be described by a general formula.
- **Outcome:** Identify the following homologous series: **alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, halogenoalkanes,
  alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, ethers, amines, amides and esters**.
- **Guidance:** none.
- **Linking question:**
  - Nature of science, Tool 2 — How useful are 3D models (real or virtual) to visualize the invisible?

#### Structure 3.2.4 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Successive members of a homologous series show a trend in physical properties.
- **Outcome:** Describe and explain the trend in melting and boiling points of members of a homologous
  series.
- **Guidance:** none.
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 2.2 — What is the influence of the carbon chain length, branching and the nature of the
    functional groups on intermolecular forces?

#### Structure 3.2.5 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** "IUPAC nomenclature" refers to a set of rules used by the International Union of Pure
  and Applied Chemistry to apply systematic names to organic and inorganic compounds.
- **Outcome:** Apply IUPAC nomenclature to **saturated or mono-unsaturated** compounds that have **up to six
  carbon atoms in the parent chain** and contain **one type** of the following functional groups:
  **halogeno, hydroxyl, carbonyl, carboxyl**.
- **Guidance:** Include straight-chain and branched-chain isomers.
- **Linking questions:** none listed.

#### Structure 3.2.6 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Structural isomers are molecules that have the same molecular formula but different
  connectivities.
- **Outcome:** Recognize isomers, including branched, straight-chain, position and functional group isomers.
- **Guidance:** Primary, secondary and tertiary alcohols, halogenoalkanes and amines should be included.
- **Linking question:**
  - AHL Structure 2.2 — How does the fact that there are only 3 isomers of dibromobenzene support the
    current model of benzene's structure?

#### Structure 3.2.7 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Stereoisomers have the same constitution (atom identities, connectivities and bond
  multiplicities) but different spatial arrangements of atoms.
- **Outcomes:**
  - Describe and explain the features that give rise to *cis–trans* isomerism; recognize it in **non-cyclic
    alkenes and C3 and C4 cycloalkanes**.
  - Draw stereochemical formulas showing the tetrahedral arrangement around a chiral carbon.
  - Describe and explain a chiral carbon atom giving rise to stereoisomers with different optical
    properties.
  - Recognize a pair of enantiomers as non-superimposable mirror images from 3D modelling (real or virtual).
- **Guidance:** Nomenclature using the **E–Z system will not be assessed**. The terms "chiral", "optical
  activity", "enantiomer" and "racemic" mixture should be understood. Knowledge of the different chemical
  properties of enantiomers can be limited to the fact that they behave differently in chiral environments.
  Wedge-dash type representations involving tapered bonds should be used for the representation of
  enantiomers.
- **Linking questions:** none listed.

#### Structure 3.2.8 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Mass spectrometry (MS) of organic compounds can cause fragmentation of molecules.
- **Outcome:** Deduce information about the structural features of a compound from specific MS fragmentation
  patterns.
- **Guidance:** Include reference to the molecular ion. Data on specific MS fragments are provided in the
  data booklet.
- **Linking questions:** none listed.

#### Structure 3.2.9 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Infrared (IR) spectra can be used to identify the type of bond present in a molecule.
- **Outcome:** Interpret the **functional group region** of an IR spectrum, using a table of characteristic
  frequencies (wavenumber / cm⁻¹).
- **Guidance:** Include reference to the absorption of IR radiation by greenhouse gases. Data for
  interpretation of IR spectra are given in the data booklet.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Structure 2.2 — What features of a molecule determine whether it is IR active or not?
  - Reactivity 1.3 — What properties of a greenhouse gas determine its "global warming potential"?

#### Structure 3.2.10 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (¹H NMR) gives information on the
  different chemical environments of hydrogen atoms in a molecule.
- **Outcome:** Interpret ¹H NMR spectra to deduce the structures of organic molecules from the **number of
  signals, the chemical shifts, and the relative areas under signals (integration traces)**.
- **Guidance / linking questions:** none listed.

#### Structure 3.2.11 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Individual signals can be split into clusters of peaks.
- **Outcome:** Interpret ¹H NMR spectra from splitting patterns showing **singlets, doublets, triplets and
  quartets** to deduce greater structural detail.
- **Guidance:** Data for interpretation of ¹H NMR spectra are given in the data booklet.
- **Linking questions:** none listed.

#### Structure 3.2.12 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Data from different techniques are often combined in structural analysis.
- **Outcome:** Interpret a variety of data, including analytical spectra, to determine the structure of a
  molecule.
- **Guidance / linking questions:** none listed.

Boundary notes for 3.2:

- **Identify vs name.** Nine functional groups (3.2.2) and twelve homologous series (3.2.3) must be
  *identified*. Systematic *naming* (3.2.5) is required only for compounds containing halogeno, hydroxyl,
  carbonyl or carboxyl groups — i.e. halogenoalkanes, alcohols, aldehydes, ketones and carboxylic acids —
  plus the hydrocarbon skeletons they are built on. Naming of **ethers, amines, amides and esters is not in
  the 3.2.5 outcome** **[INFERENCE]**. **[UNVERIFIED]** whether exam questions nevertheless use ester or
  amine names as given information; a site may show such names but should not test naming them.
- **All of structural isomerism is SL+HL; all of stereoisomerism is AHL.** Primary/secondary/tertiary
  classification (alcohols, halogenoalkanes, amines) is SL+HL, under 3.2.6.
- **Skeletal formulas are SL+HL** (interconvert). **Stereochemical (wedge-dash) formulas are drawn only at
  AHL** (3.2.7); at SL+HL they need only be identified as a type (3.2.1).
- **All spectroscopy is AHL**: MS fragmentation, IR, ¹H NMR and combined analysis. Isotope mass spectra of
  elements are also AHL (Structure 1.2.3). There is no SL spectroscopy in this guide.
- **Empirical formula** as an organic formula type links back to Structure 1.4.4.
- **Reactions are not here.** Structure 3.2 classifies, names, draws and identifies. What the functional
  groups *do* is Reactivity 3.2–3.4 (see section 3c).

### 4b. Colour wheel (section 15, from the mirror)

Six sectors with boundary wavelengths: 400 nm (violet/red junction, i.e. the two ends of the visible
range) – Violet – 424 nm – Blue – 491 nm – Green – 575 nm – Yellow – 585 nm – Orange – 647 nm – Red –
700 nm. Opposite pairs (complementary colours): **red ↔ green, orange ↔ blue, yellow ↔ violet**. A complex
that absorbs orange light (585–647 nm) appears blue. To get frequency use c = λf with c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹
and λ in metres.

- "Mono-unsaturated" most naturally means one C=C; whether one C≡C is included is not stated. Alkynes must
  be *identified* (3.2.3); treat naming simple alkynes (ethyne, propyne, but-1-yne) as low-risk to include
  but do not build hard naming questions on them **[INFERENCE]**.
- A compound that is mono-unsaturated *and* has one of the four groups (e.g. but-3-en-1-ol) fits the literal
  wording; keep such names rare and simple **[INFERENCE]**.
- Cyclic compounds are not mentioned in 3.2.5; cycloalkanes C3 and C4 appear only for *cis–trans*
  recognition at AHL.
- The understanding mentions systematic names for "organic and inorganic compounds"; the inorganic naming
  actually specified by the guide is in Structure 2.1.2 and 3.1.6 / Reactivity 3.2.1.
- Locant style (but-2-ene, propan-2-ol rather than 2-butene, 2-propanol) follows current IUPAC
  recommendations; the guide's own names (e.g. "ethanoic acid", "halogenoalkanes") use IUPAC style
  **[UNVERIFIED]** which variants markschemes accept.

| Type | Level | Scope |

Homologous series trend (3.2.4): boiling and melting points rise with chain length because London
(dispersion) forces increase with molecular size / number of electrons; branching and functional-group

### 7f. Spectroscopy (all AHL)

| Technique | Required depth | Data supplied | Not required |

## 9. Scope rules for page authors and the auditor

1. A statement is "required" only if it maps to an understanding, outcome or guidance line in section 2.
2. Content in 3a or 3b must not be presented as required. If included at all, label it "Beyond the
   syllabus".
3. Content in 3c may be mentioned as a signpost ("you will meet this in Reactivity 3.4") but not taught or
   tested as Structure 3 content. In particular, Structure 3.2 pages must not teach organic reactions or
   mechanisms, and Structure 3.1 pages must not teach redox equations, electrode potentials or ligand
   substitution.
4. Label as HL-only: 3.1.7, 3.1.8, 3.1.9, 3.1.10, 3.2.7, 3.2.8, 3.2.9, 3.2.10, 3.2.11, 3.2.12. Everything
   else in Structure 3 is common to SL and HL.
5. On SL+HL periodicity content, explain only the general ionization-energy trends; put the Be/B and N/O
   discontinuities in an HL-labelled block, and explain them by the energy of the electron removed, never by
   "special stability" of filled or half-filled sublevels.
6. Keep the closed lists closed: four named families (3.1.2); five periodic properties (3.1.3); two group
   reaction types (3.1.4); four kinds of oxide for equations (3.1.5); nine functional groups (3.2.2); twelve
   homologous series (3.2.3); four nameable functional groups and six parent-chain carbons (3.2.5); four
   splitting patterns (3.2.11).
7. Do not test naming of ethers, amines, amides or esters, E/Z or R/S descriptors, or any compound outside
   the 3.2.5 limits. *Cis*/*trans* is the only stereo-descriptor used.
8. Spectroscopy questions must be answerable from the booklet tables in section 4 (IR ranges, ¹H NMR
   shifts, MS fragments lost). Do not require memorized values, the fingerprint region, ¹³C NMR or coupling
   constants. Section 4 is from a mirror: do not present it as officially confirmed.
9. Use the guide's vocabulary: "oxidation state" (sign first, e.g. +2), "transition elements",
   "halogenoalkanes", "hydroxyl", "carboxyl", "alkoxy", "amido", "structural (full and condensed) formula",
   "skeletal formula", "stereochemical formula", "*cis–trans*", "chiral carbon", "racemic mixture",
   "molecular ion", "integration trace", "wavenumber".
10. Anything marked [UNVERIFIED] must not be presented as official IB wording; phrase it as common practice
    or check it against a past markscheme first. This includes trend explanations, oxidation-state rule
    lists, the pairing table in 7e, general formulas and the n + 1 rule.
11. Linking questions are connections, not extra syllabus statements.
