= Paper 1A (40 multiple-choice questions, 40 marks, on SL and AHL material, no marks deducted for wrong

## 2. Understandings, outcomes, guidance and linking questions

### Structure 1.1 — Introduction to the particulate nature of matter

**Guiding question:** How can we model the particulate nature of matter?
SL+HL 2 hours. **AHL: none for Structure 1.1.**

#### Structure 1.1.1 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Elements are the primary constituents of matter, which cannot be chemically broken down
  into simpler substances. Compounds consist of atoms of different elements chemically bonded together in a
  fixed ratio. Mixtures contain more than one element or compound in no fixed ratio, which are not
  chemically bonded and so can be separated by physical methods.
- **Outcome:** Distinguish between the properties of elements, compounds and mixtures.
- **Guidance:** Solvation, filtration, recrystallization, evaporation, distillation and **paper
  chromatography** should be covered. The differences between homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures should
  be understood.
- **Edition note:** "paper chromatography" is the official Feb 2023 wording. A mirrored "Updated May 2023"
  copy drops the word "paper" **[UNVERIFIED]**. Site rule: teach paper chromatography as the required
  method and mention thin layer chromatography alongside it, since Tool 1 lists "paper or thin layer
  chromatography" and Structure 2.2.10 covers both. This satisfies either wording.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Tool 1 — What factors are considered in choosing a method to separate the components of a mixture?
  - Tool 1 — How can the products of a reaction be purified?
  - Structure 2.2 — How do intermolecular forces influence the type of mixture that forms between two
    substances?
  - Structure 2.3 — Why are alloys generally considered to be mixtures, even though they often contain
    metallic bonding?

#### Structure 1.1.2 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** The kinetic molecular theory is a model to explain physical properties of matter
  (solids, liquids and gases) and changes of state.
- **Outcomes:** Distinguish the different states of matter. Use state symbols (s, l, g and aq) in chemical
  equations.
- **Guidance:** Names of the changes of state should be covered: melting, freezing, vaporization
  (evaporation and boiling), condensation, sublimation and deposition.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Structure 2.4 — Why are some substances solid while others are fluid under standard conditions?
  - Structure 2 (all), Reactivity 1.2 — Why are some changes of state endothermic and some exothermic?

#### Structure 1.1.3 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** The temperature, T, in Kelvin (K) is a measure of average kinetic energy Ek of particles.
- **Outcomes:** Interpret observable changes in physical properties and temperature during changes of state.
  Convert between values in the Celsius and Kelvin scales.
- **Guidance:** The kelvin (K) is the SI unit of temperature and has the same incremental value as the
  Celsius degree (°C).
- **Linking questions:**
  - Reactivity 2.2 — What is the graphical distribution of kinetic energy values of particles in a sample at
    a fixed temperature?
  - Reactivity 2.2 — What must happen to particles for a chemical reaction to occur?

### Structure 1.2 — The nuclear atom

**Guiding question:** How do the nuclei of atoms differ?
SL+HL 2 hours. AHL 1 hour.

#### Structure 1.2.1 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Atoms contain a positively charged, dense nucleus composed of protons and neutrons
  (nucleons). Negatively charged electrons occupy the space outside the nucleus.
- **Outcome:** Use the nuclear symbol (mass number A as superscript, atomic number Z as subscript, before
  the element symbol X) to deduce the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in atoms and ions.
- **Guidance:** Relative masses and charges of the subatomic particles should be known; actual values are
  given in the data booklet. The mass of the electron can be considered negligible.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Structure 1.3 — What determines the different chemical properties of atoms?
  - Structure 3.1 — How does the atomic number relate to the position of an element in the periodic table?

#### Structure 1.2.2 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons.
- **Outcome:** Perform calculations involving non-integer relative atomic masses and abundance of isotopes
  from given data.
- **Guidance:** Differences in the physical properties of isotopes should be understood. Specific examples
  of isotopes need not be learned.
- **Linking question:**
  - Nature of science, Reactivity 3.4 — How can isotope tracers provide evidence for a reaction mechanism?

#### Structure 1.2.3 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Mass spectra are used to determine the relative atomic masses of elements from their
  isotopic composition.
- **Outcome:** Interpret mass spectra in terms of identity and relative abundance of isotopes.
- **Guidance:** The operational details of the mass spectrometer will not be assessed.
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 3.2 — How does the fragmentation pattern of a compound in the mass spectrometer help in the
    determination of its structure?

Boundary note: isotope/Ar calculations "from given data" (1.2.2) are SL+HL. Reading those data off a **mass
spectrum** (1.2.3) is AHL. Fragmentation of organic molecules is Structure 3.2.8, not this strand.

### Structure 1.3 — Electron configurations

**Guiding question:** How can we model the energy states of electrons in atoms?
SL+HL 3 hours. AHL 3 hours.

#### Structure 1.3.1 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Emission spectra are produced by atoms emitting photons when electrons in excited
  states return to lower energy levels.
- **Outcomes:** Qualitatively describe the relationship between colour, wavelength, frequency and energy
  across the electromagnetic spectrum. Distinguish between a continuous and a line spectrum.
- **Guidance:** Details of the electromagnetic spectrum are given in the data booklet.
- **Linking questions:** none listed.

#### Structure 1.3.2 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** The line emission spectrum of hydrogen provides evidence for the existence of electrons
  in discrete energy levels, which converge at higher energies.
- **Outcome:** Describe the emission spectrum of the hydrogen atom, including the relationships between the
  lines and energy transitions to the first, second and third energy levels.
- **Guidance:** The names of the different series in the hydrogen emission spectrum will not be assessed.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Inquiry 2 — In the study of emission spectra from gaseous elements and of light, what qualitative and
    quantitative data can be collected from instruments such as gas discharge tubes and prisms?
  - Nature of science, Structure 1.2 — How do emission spectra provide evidence for the existence of
    different elements?

#### Structure 1.3.3 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** The main energy level is given an integer number, n, and can hold a maximum of 2n²
  electrons.
- **Outcome:** Deduce the maximum number of electrons that can occupy each energy level.
- **Guidance:** none.
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 3.1 — How does an element's highest main energy level relate to its period number in the
    periodic table?

#### Structure 1.3.4 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** A more detailed model of the atom describes the division of the main energy level into
  s, p, d and f sublevels of successively higher energies.
- **Outcome:** Recognize the shape and orientation of an s atomic orbital and the three p atomic orbitals.
- **Guidance:** none.
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 3.1 — What is the relationship between energy sublevels and the block nature of the periodic
    table?

#### Structure 1.3.5 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Each orbital has a defined energy state for a given electron configuration and chemical
  environment, and can hold two electrons of opposite spin. Sublevels contain a fixed number of orbitals,
  regions of space where there is a high probability of finding an electron.
- **Outcome:** Apply the Aufbau principle, Hund's rule and the Pauli exclusion principle to deduce electron
  configurations for atoms and ions up to Z = 36.
- **Guidance:** Full electron configurations and condensed electron configurations using the noble gas core
  should be covered. Orbital diagrams, i.e. arrow-in-box diagrams, should be used to represent the filling
  and relative energy of orbitals. The electron configurations of Cr and Cu as exceptions should be covered.
- **Linking questions:** none listed.

#### Structure 1.3.6 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** In an emission spectrum, the limit of convergence at higher frequency corresponds to
  ionization.
- **Outcomes:** Explain the trends and discontinuities in first ionization energy (IE) across a period and
  down a group. Calculate the value of the first IE from spectral data that gives the wavelength or
  frequency of the convergence limit.
- **Guidance:** The value of the Planck constant h and the equations E = hf and c = λf are given in the data
  booklet.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Structure 3.1 — How does the trend in IE values across a period and down a group explain the trends in
    properties of metals and non-metals?
  - Nature of science, Tool 3, Reactivity 3.1 — Why are log scales useful when discussing [H+] and IEs?

#### Structure 1.3.7 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Successive ionization energy (IE) data for an element give information about its
  electron configuration.
- **Outcome:** Deduce the group of an element from its successive ionization data.
- **Guidance:** Databases are useful for compiling graphs of trends in IEs.
- **Linking question:**
  - AHL Structure 3.1 — How do patterns of successive IEs of transition elements help to explain the
    variable oxidation states of these elements?

Boundary notes for 1.3:

- **E = hf and c = λf calculations are AHL in this strand.** At SL+HL level (1.3.1) the relationship between
  colour, wavelength, frequency and energy is *qualitative* only.
- Ionization energy also appears outside this strand: **Structure 3.1.3 (SL+HL)** "Explain the periodicity of
  atomic radius, ionic radius, ionization energy, electron affinity and electronegativity", and **Structure
  3.1.7 (AHL)** "Discontinuities occur in the trend of increasing first ionization energy across a period…
  Explain how these discontinuities provide evidence for the existence of energy sublevels", with the
  guidance: *"Explanations should be based on the energy of the electron removed, rather than on the
  'special stability' of filled and half-filled sublevels."* That guidance governs how this site must explain
  the Be/B and N/O discontinuities in 1.3.6 too **[INFERENCE]** (it is written under 3.1.7, not 1.3.6).

### Structure 1.4 — Counting particles by mass: The mole

**Guiding question:** How do we quantify matter on the atomic scale?
SL+HL 7 hours. **AHL: none for Structure 1.4.**

#### Structure 1.4.1 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** The mole (mol) is the SI unit of amount of substance. One mole contains exactly the
  number of elementary entities given by the Avogadro constant.
- **Outcome:** Convert the amount of substance, n, to the number of specified elementary entities.
- **Guidance:** An elementary entity may be an atom, a molecule, an ion, an electron, any other particle or a
  specified group of particles. The Avogadro constant NA is given in the data booklet. It has the units
  mol⁻¹.
- **Linking questions:** none listed.

#### Structure 1.4.2 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Masses of atoms are compared on a scale relative to ¹²C and are expressed as relative
  atomic mass Ar and relative formula mass Mr.
- **Outcome:** Determine relative formula masses Mr from relative atomic masses Ar.
- **Guidance:** Relative atomic mass and relative formula mass have no units. The values of relative atomic
  masses given to two decimal places in the data booklet should be used in calculations.
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 3.1 — Atoms increase in mass as their position descends in the periodic table. What properties
    might be related to this trend?

#### Structure 1.4.3 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Molar mass M has the units g mol⁻¹.
- **Outcome:** Solve problems involving the relationships between the number of particles, the amount of
  substance in moles and the mass in grams.
- **Guidance:** The relationship n = m/M is given in the data booklet.
- **Linking question:**
  - Reactivity 2.1 — How can molar masses be used with chemical equations to determine the masses of the
    products of a reaction?

#### Structure 1.4.4 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** The empirical formula of a compound gives the simplest ratio of atoms of each element
  present in that compound. The molecular formula gives the actual number of atoms of each element present
  in a molecule.
- **Outcomes:** Interconvert the percentage composition by mass and the empirical formula. Determine the
  molecular formula of a compound from its empirical formula and molar mass.
- **Guidance:** none.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Tool 1 — How can experimental data on mass changes in combustion reactions be used to derive empirical
    formulas?
  - Nature of science, Tool 3, Structure 3.2 — What is the importance of approximation in the determination
    of an empirical formula?

#### Structure 1.4.5 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** The molar concentration is determined by the amount of solute and the volume of
  solution.
- **Outcome:** Solve problems involving the molar concentration, amount of solute and volume of solution.
- **Guidance:** The use of square brackets to represent molar concentration is required. Units of
  concentration should include g dm⁻³ and mol dm⁻³ and conversion between these. The relationship n = CV is
  given in the data booklet.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Tool 1 — What are the considerations in the choice of glassware used in preparing a standard solution and
    a serial dilution?
  - Tool 1, Inquiry 2 — How can a calibration curve be used to determine the concentration of a solution?

#### Structure 1.4.6 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Avogadro's law states that equal volumes of all gases measured under the same
  conditions of temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules.
- **Outcome:** Solve problems involving the mole ratio of reactants and/or products and the volume of gases.
- **Guidance:** none.
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 1.5 — Avogadro's law applies to ideal gases. Under what conditions might the behaviour of a
    real gas deviate most from an ideal gas?

### Structure 1.5 — Ideal gases

**Guiding question:** How does the model of ideal gas behaviour help us to predict the behaviour of real
gases?
SL+HL 3 hours. **AHL: none for Structure 1.5.**

#### Structure 1.5.1 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** An ideal gas consists of moving particles with negligible volume and no intermolecular
  forces. All collisions between particles are considered elastic.
- **Outcome:** Recognize the key assumptions in the ideal gas model.
- **Guidance / linking questions:** none.

#### Structure 1.5.2 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Real gases deviate from the ideal gas model, particularly at low temperature and high
  pressure.
- **Outcome:** Explain the limitations of the ideal gas model.
- **Guidance:** No mathematical coverage is required.
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 2.2 — Under comparable conditions, why do some gases deviate more from ideal behaviour than
    others?

#### Structure 1.5.3 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** The molar volume of an ideal gas is a constant at a specific temperature and pressure.
- **Outcome:** Investigate the relationship between temperature, pressure and volume for a fixed mass of an
  ideal gas and analyse graphs relating these variables.
- **Guidance:** The names of specific gas laws will not be assessed. The value for the molar volume of an
  ideal gas under standard temperature and pressure (STP) is given in the data booklet.
- **Linking question:**
  - Nature of science, Tools 2 and 3, Reactivity 2.2 — Graphs can be presented as sketches or as accurately
    plotted data points. What are the advantages and limitations of each representation?

#### Structure 1.5.4 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** The relationship between the pressure, volume, temperature and amount of an ideal gas is
  shown in the ideal gas equation PV = nRT and the combined gas law P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂.
- **Outcome:** Solve problems relating to the ideal gas equation.
- **Guidance:** Units of volume and pressure should be SI only. The value of the gas constant R, the ideal gas
  equation, and the combined gas law, are given in the data booklet.
- **Linking question:**
  - Tool 1, Inquiry 2 — How can the ideal gas law be used to calculate the molar mass of a gas from
    experimental data?

### 4c. Unit conversions and standard conditions (booklet section 4, verbatim)

- Temperature (K) = temperature (°C) + 273.15
- 1 dm³ = 1 litre = 1 × 10⁻³ m³ = 1 × 10³ cm³
- STP conditions: 273.15 K and 100 kPa
- SATP conditions: 298.15 K and 100 kPa

The booklet gives **no molar volume at SATP**. If needed it must be calculated from PV = nRT (about
24.8 dm³ mol⁻¹ with R = 8.31) **[INFERENCE: computed, not printed]**. Do not quote 24.0 or 24.5 dm³ mol⁻¹.
Booklet section 8 quotes melting and boiling points "at 101.325 kPa"; that is a data condition, not STP.

### 4d. Other booklet sections relevant to this strand

- Section 3: SI multipliers (peta to femto), needed for nm → m and pm conversions.
- Section 5: electromagnetic spectrum diagram (γ-rays, X-rays, UV, IR, microwaves, radio waves on a
  wavelength axis from 10⁻¹⁶ to 10⁸ m, with "increasing energy" arrow; visible band V I B G Y O R,
  400–700 nm). This is the "details … given in the data booklet" referred to in 1.3.1.
- Section 7: periodic table with atomic number and relative atomic mass. **Ar values are printed to two
  decimal places** (H 1.01, C 12.01, N 14.01, O 16.00, Na 22.99, S 32.07, Cl 35.45, Cr 52.00, Fe 55.85,
  Cu 63.55, Br 79.90). Guide 1.4.2 and Reactivity 2.1.2 both require these two-decimal values in calculations.
- Section 9: first ionization energy, electron affinity and electronegativity of the elements (data for
  1.3.6 / 3.1.3).
- Section 15: colour wheel with wavelengths of the visible spectrum.
- Section 23: uncertainty propagation formulas (added February 2024): for y = a ± b, Δy = Δa + Δb; for
  y = ab/c, Δy/y = Δa/a + Δb/b + Δc/c; for y = aⁿ, Δy/y = |n| Δa/a (the last is HL per Tool 3).

### 4e. Unit pitfalls for PV = nRT (the guide says "SI only")

- With R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: P in **Pa**, V in **m³**, T in **K**, n in mol.
- Numerically equivalent shortcut: P in **kPa** with V in **dm³** (kPa × dm³ = J). Mixing kPa with m³, or Pa
  with dm³, gives an answer wrong by a factor of 1000.
- cm³ → m³: divide by 10⁶. dm³ → m³: divide by 10³.
- T must be in kelvin in PV = nRT and in the combined gas law. Add 273.15 (booklet); **[UNVERIFIED]** whether
  markschemes accept 273 — standard practice is that they do.
- Molar volume 22.7 dm³ mol⁻¹ is valid only at STP (273.15 K, 100 kPa); at other conditions use PV = nRT.
- Concentration: mol dm⁻³ and g dm⁻³ (g dm⁻³ = mol dm⁻³ × M). Convert cm³ to dm³ before using n = CV.
  Square brackets mean molar concentration, e.g. [NaCl]. The unit symbol "M" is not used in the guide.
- Molar mass M has units g mol⁻¹; Ar and Mr have no units (1.4.2 guidance). Marks are lost for units on Mr.
- IB unit style is index notation (mol dm⁻³, g mol⁻¹, J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹), not solidus (mol/dm³).

## 6. Key definitions (mark-earning wording)

The guide's own wording is quoted where it exists. The guide contains no formal glossary of chemical
definitions, so anything not quoted from an understanding is **[UNVERIFIED]** standard wording.

| Term | Wording | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Element | A primary constituent of matter which cannot be chemically broken down into simpler substances. | Guide 1.1.1 |
| Compound | Atoms of different elements chemically bonded together in a fixed ratio. | Guide 1.1.1 |
| Mixture | More than one element or compound in no fixed ratio, not chemically bonded, so separable by physical methods. | Guide 1.1.1 |
| Homogeneous / heterogeneous mixture | Uniform composition and properties throughout (one phase) / non-uniform composition (distinct phases). | [UNVERIFIED] wording; the distinction is required by 1.1.1 guidance |
| Temperature | A measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles (T in kelvin). | Guide 1.1.3 |
| Nucleons | Protons and neutrons. | Guide 1.2.1 |
| Atomic number, Z | Number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. | [UNVERIFIED] wording |
| Mass number, A | Total number of protons and neutrons (nucleons) in the nucleus. | [UNVERIFIED] wording |
| Isotopes | Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. (Equivalent: same atomic number, different mass number.) | Guide 1.2.2 |
| Relative atomic mass, Ar | The weighted average mass of the atoms of an element, taking isotopic abundance into account, relative to 1/12 of the mass of an atom of carbon-12. No units. | Scale and "no units" from guide 1.4.2; full sentence [UNVERIFIED] |
| Relative formula mass, Mr | Sum of the relative atomic masses of the atoms in the formula. No units. | Guide 1.4.2 outcome; wording [UNVERIFIED] |
| Mole | The SI unit of amount of substance. One mole contains exactly the number of elementary entities given by the Avogadro constant (6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ in the booklet). | Guide 1.4.1 |
| Elementary entity | An atom, a molecule, an ion, an electron, any other particle or a specified group of particles. | Guide 1.4.1 |
| Molar mass, M | Mass of one mole of a substance; units g mol⁻¹. | Units from guide 1.4.3; wording [UNVERIFIED] |
| Empirical formula | The simplest ratio of atoms of each element present in a compound. | Guide 1.4.4 |
| Molecular formula | The actual number of atoms of each element present in a molecule. | Guide 1.4.4 |
| Molar concentration | Amount of solute (mol) per volume of solution (dm³); shown with square brackets. Note: volume of *solution*, not of solvent. | Guide 1.4.5 |
| Avogadro's law | Equal volumes of all gases measured under the same conditions of temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules. | Guide 1.4.6 |
| Ideal gas | Moving particles with negligible volume and no intermolecular forces; all collisions between particles are considered elastic. | Guide 1.5.1 |
| Molar volume | The volume of one mole of an ideal gas; a constant at a specific temperature and pressure (22.7 dm³ mol⁻¹ at STP). | Guide 1.5.3 + booklet |
| Emission spectrum | Produced by atoms emitting photons when electrons in excited states return to lower energy levels. | Guide 1.3.1 |
| Continuous vs line spectrum | Continuous: all wavelengths/frequencies in a range. Line: only certain discrete wavelengths/frequencies. | [UNVERIFIED] wording; the distinction is required by 1.3.1 |
| Orbital | A region of space where there is a high probability of finding an electron; holds two electrons of opposite spin. | Guide 1.3.5 |
| Aufbau principle | Electrons fill the lowest-energy available orbitals/sublevels first. | Named in guide 1.3.5; wording [UNVERIFIED] |
| Hund's rule | Orbitals of the same sublevel (equal energy) are each occupied singly, with parallel spins, before any is doubly occupied. | Named in guide 1.3.5; wording [UNVERIFIED] |
| Pauli exclusion principle | An orbital can hold at most two electrons, and they must have opposite spins. | Named in guide 1.3.5; "two electrons of opposite spin" is guide wording |
| First ionization energy (AHL here) | The minimum energy required to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms in their ground state, forming one mole of gaseous 1+ ions: X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻. | [UNVERIFIED] wording — not defined in the guide. "Gaseous" and "one mole" are the usual marking points |
| Limit of convergence (AHL) | The point at higher frequency where the spectral lines merge; it corresponds to ionization (electron removed to n = ∞). | Guide 1.3.6; "n = ∞" [UNVERIFIED] wording |
| Successive ionization energies (AHL) | Energies to remove the 1st, 2nd, 3rd… electrons in turn. A large jump shows the next electron comes from a main energy level closer to the nucleus, so the number of electrons removed before the first large jump gives the group. | Outcome from guide 1.3.7; explanation [UNVERIFIED] wording |

Ideal gas assumptions required by the guide (1.5.1), stated precisely — exactly these four ideas:

1. The gas consists of **moving** particles.
2. The particles have **negligible volume** (compared with the volume of the container).
3. There are **no intermolecular forces** between the particles.
4. All collisions between particles are **elastic**.

Commonly added statements — "random, straight-line motion" and "average kinetic energy is proportional to
absolute temperature" — are not in 1.5.1. The second is consistent with 1.1.3 but the guide says only that
temperature "is a measure of" average kinetic energy. **[UNVERIFIED]** whether markschemes credit them.

Real gas deviation (1.5.2): greatest at **low temperature and high pressure**. Qualitative reasons only:
at high pressure the particles' own volume is no longer negligible; at low temperature intermolecular
forces become significant because the particles move more slowly **[UNVERIFIED wording]**.

## 7. Electron configuration specifics

| Question | Answer | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Range of elements | Atoms and ions **up to Z = 36** (H to Kr) | 1.3.5 outcome; repeated in 3.1.2 |
| Principles to apply by name | Aufbau principle, Hund's rule, Pauli exclusion principle | 1.3.5 outcome |
| Notations required | (a) Full configuration, e.g. 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d⁶; (b) condensed configuration with noble gas core, e.g. [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶; (c) orbital diagrams = arrow-in-box diagrams showing filling **and relative energy** of orbitals | 1.3.5 guidance |
| Exceptions | **Cr and Cu only**: Cr is [Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵, Cu is [Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰ | 1.3.5 guidance names Cr and Cu; the configurations themselves are standard chemistry |
| Capacity of main levels | 2n² (2, 8, 18, 32) | 1.3.3 |
| Sublevels | s, p, d, f "of successively higher energies" within a main level; fixed number of orbitals per sublevel (1, 3, 5, 7) | 1.3.4, 1.3.5; the numbers 1/3/5/7 are standard, the guide says only "a fixed number" |
| Orbital shapes | **Recognize** shape and orientation of an s orbital (spherical) and the three p orbitals (dumbbell, along x, y, z). d and f shapes not required. | 1.3.4 outcome |
| Depth of quantum model | Orbital = region of high probability of finding an electron; two electrons of opposite spin per orbital. No quantum numbers, no wave functions, no nodes. | 1.3.5; absences checked in guide text |
| Level | All of the above is **SL+HL** | 1.3.1–1.3.5 are in the SL+HL section |

Points needing care:

- **Ions of transition elements — a genuine boundary ambiguity.** 1.3.5 (SL+HL) says "atoms and ions up to
  Z = 36", which read literally includes ions such as Fe²⁺ and Cu²⁺. But "Deduce the electron configurations
  of ions of the first-row transition elements" is a separate outcome under **Structure 3.1.9, which is
  AHL**. Reading the two together, transition-metal ion configurations are explicitly required only at HL;
  s- and p-block ions (Na⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺, O²⁻) are clearly SL+HL **[INFERENCE]**. For an HL student both are
  required, so the site should teach them and label transition-metal ions as HL (cross-reference 3.1.9).
- **4s before 3d when ionizing.** First-row transition-element ions lose 4s electrons before 3d (Fe²⁺ is
  [Ar] 3d⁶, not [Ar] 4s² 3d⁴). This is the content of 3.1.9; the guide does not spell the rule out
  **[UNVERIFIED as guide wording; standard chemistry and standard markscheme expectation]**.
- **Order of writing 4s and 3d.** The guide does not prescribe whether to write [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶ or
  [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s². **[UNVERIFIED]** IB markschemes are generally understood to accept either order. Pick one
  order site-wide and state that both are accepted.
- **Explaining Cr and Cu.** The guide requires that the exceptions "be covered"; it does not require an
  explanation. Given the 3.1.7 guidance against "special stability" arguments for IE, avoid presenting
  "half-filled and filled sublevels are extra stable" as a required explanation **[INFERENCE]**.
- **Orbital diagrams** must show relative energy (4s box below 3d when filling), one box per orbital, single
  arrows with parallel spins before pairing, paired arrows opposite. Half-arrows or full arrows:
  **[UNVERIFIED]** either is understood to be accepted.
- **Hydrogen emission spectrum depth (1.3.2):** lines arise from transitions *down to* n = 1, n = 2 and n = 3;
  lines converge at higher energy/frequency; discrete lines are evidence of discrete energy levels. Higher
  energy transitions end on lower levels. The regions (to n = 1 ultraviolet, to n = 2 visible, to n = 3
  infrared) are standard and consistent with the booklet's spectrum diagram, but the guide does not list them
  **[UNVERIFIED as required recall]**. Series names are not assessed.
- **AHL calculation (1.3.6):** from the wavelength or frequency of the convergence limit, E = hf (and
  f = c/λ) gives the energy per atom in J; × NA and ÷ 1000 gives kJ mol⁻¹. The convergence limit used must
  be that of the series ending on the ground state for a ground-state first IE **[INFERENCE]**.
- **AHL trends (1.3.6):** explain the general increase in first IE across a period, the decrease down a
  group, and the discontinuities (e.g. group 2 → 13 and group 15 → 16) using nuclear charge, distance,
  shielding and the energy of the electron removed (p sublevel higher in energy than s; a paired p electron
  experiences repulsion). The specific element pairs are standard examples, not guide wording **[UNVERIFIED
  as guide wording]**.
