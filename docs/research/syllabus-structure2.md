## 2. Understandings, outcomes, guidance and linking questions

### Structure 2.1 — The ionic model

**Guiding question:** What determines the ionic nature and properties of a compound?
SL+HL 4 hours. **AHL: none for Structure 2.1.**

#### Structure 2.1.1 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Metal atoms that lose electrons form positive ions, called cations. Non-metal atoms
  that gain electrons form negative ions, called anions.
- **Outcome:** Predict the charge of an ion from the electron configuration of the atom.
- **Guidance:** The formation of ions with different charges from a transition element should be included.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Structure 3.1 — How does the position of an element in the periodic table relate to the charge of its
    ion(s)?
  - AHL Structure 1.3 — How does the trend in successive ionization energies of transition elements explain
    their variable oxidation states?

#### Structure 2.1.2 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** The ionic bond is formed by electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions.
  Binary ionic compounds are named with the cation first, then the anion; the anion takes the suffix "ide".
- **Outcomes:** Deduce the formula and name of an ionic compound from its component ions, including
  polyatomic ions. Interconvert names and formulas of binary ionic compounds.
- **Guidance:** These polyatomic ions should be known by name and formula: ammonium NH₄⁺, hydroxide OH⁻,
  nitrate NO₃⁻, hydrogencarbonate HCO₃⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, sulfate SO₄²⁻, phosphate PO₄³⁻.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Reactivity 3.2 — Why is the formation of an ionic compound from its elements a redox reaction?
  - AHL Structure 2.2 — How is formal charge used to predict the preferred structure of sulfate?
  - AHL Reactivity 3.1 — Polyatomic anions are conjugate bases of common acids. What is the relationship
    between their stability and the conjugate acid's dissociation constant, Ka?

#### Structure 2.1.3 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Ionic compounds exist as three-dimensional lattice structures, represented by empirical
  formulas.
- **Outcome:** Explain the physical properties of ionic compounds, to include volatility, electrical
  conductivity and solubility.
- **Guidance:** Include **lattice enthalpy** as a measure of the strength of the ionic bond in different
  compounds, influenced by ion radius and charge.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Tool 1, Inquiry 2 — What experimental data demonstrate the physical properties of ionic compounds?
  - Structure 3.1 — How can lattice enthalpies and the bonding continuum explain the trend in melting points
    of metal chlorides across period 3?

Boundary notes for 2.1:

- **Lattice enthalpy is SL+HL here, qualitatively.** It appears as guidance to 2.1.3: a *measure* of ionic
  bond strength that depends on ion radius and ion charge. Comparing two compounds (e.g. smaller ions or
  higher charges give a larger lattice enthalpy, hence higher melting point) is in scope at SL+HL.
  **Born–Haber cycles** — where lattice enthalpy is *calculated* — are **Reactivity 1.2.5 (AHL)**.
- **Transition element ions at SL.** 2.1.1 requires that students know a transition element can form ions
  of different charge (e.g. Fe²⁺ / Fe³⁺, Cu⁺ / Cu²⁺ **[INFERENCE: examples are not named in the guide]**).
  *Explaining* this from successive ionization energies and deducing d-block ion configurations is AHL
  (Structure 3.1.9).
- **The seven polyatomic ions are a closed list for recall.** Others (nitrite, sulfite — named in 3.1.6
  guidance as naming examples; ethanoate, etc.) may appear but need not be memorized from 2.1.2.
- Naming with Roman numerals for variable-charge metals (iron(III) chloride) comes from Structure 3.1.6 /
  Reactivity 3.2.1 ("use of oxidation numbers in the naming of compounds"); it is not stated in 2.1.2.
  Teach it where 2.1.1's transition-element ions meet 2.1.2's naming, and cross-reference **[INFERENCE]**.

### Structure 2.2 — The covalent model

**Guiding question:** What determines the covalent nature and properties of a substance?
SL+HL 10 hours. AHL 8 hours.

#### Structure 2.2.1 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** A covalent bond is formed by the electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of
  electrons and the positively charged nuclei. The octet rule refers to the tendency of atoms to gain a
  valence shell with a total of 8 electrons.
- **Outcome:** Deduce the Lewis formula of molecules and ions for **up to four electron pairs on each atom**.
- **Guidance:** Lewis formulas (also called electron dot or Lewis structures) show all the valence electrons
  — bonding and non-bonding pairs — in a covalently bonded species. Electron pairs may be shown as dots,
  crosses or dashes. Molecules containing atoms with **fewer than an octet** should be covered. Organic and
  inorganic examples should be used.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Nature of science — What are some of the limitations of the octet rule?
  - Structure 1.3 — Why do noble gases form covalent bonds less readily than other elements?
  - Structure 2.1 — Why do ionic bonds only form between different elements while covalent bonds can form
    between atoms of the same element?

#### Structure 2.2.2 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Single, double and triple bonds involve one, two and three shared pairs of electrons
  respectively.
- **Outcome:** Explain the relationship between the number of bonds, bond length and bond strength.
- **Guidance:** none.
- **Linking question:**
  - Reactivity 2.2 — How does the presence of double and triple bonds in molecules influence their
    reactivity?

#### Structure 2.2.3 (SL+HL, with an AHL extension)

- **Understanding:** A coordination bond is a covalent bond in which both electrons of the shared pair
  originate from the same atom.
- **Outcome:** Identify coordination bonds in compounds.
- **Guidance:** "**AHL** — Include coverage of transition element complexes."
- **Linking question:**
  - AHL Reactivity 3.4 — Why do Lewis acid–base reactions lead to the formation of coordination bonds?

#### Structure 2.2.4 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** The valence shell electron pair repulsion (VSEPR) model enables the shapes of molecules
  to be predicted from the repulsion of electron domains around a central atom.
- **Outcome:** Predict the electron domain geometry and the molecular geometry for species with **up to four
  electron domains**.
- **Guidance:** Include predicting how non-bonding pairs and multiple bonds affect bond angles.
- **Linking question:**
  - Nature of science — How useful is the VSEPR model at predicting molecular geometry?

#### Structure 2.2.5 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Bond polarity results from the difference in electronegativities of the bonded atoms.
- **Outcome:** Deduce the polar nature of a covalent bond from electronegativity values.
- **Guidance:** Bond dipoles can be shown either with partial charges or with vectors. Electronegativity
  values are given in the data booklet.
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 2.1 — What properties of ionic compounds might be expected in compounds with polar covalent
    bonding?

#### Structure 2.2.6 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Molecular polarity depends on both bond polarity and molecular geometry.
- **Outcome:** Deduce the net dipole moment of a molecule or ion by considering bond polarity and molecular
  geometry.
- **Guidance:** Examples should include species in which bond dipoles do and do not cancel each other.
- **Linking question:**
  - AHL Structure 3.2 — What features of a molecule make it "infrared (IR) active"?

#### Structure 2.2.7 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Carbon and silicon form covalent network structures.
- **Outcome:** Describe the structures and explain the properties of **silicon, silicon dioxide** and
  carbon's allotropes: **diamond, graphite, fullerenes and graphene**.
- **Guidance:** Allotropes of the same element have different bonding and structural patterns, and so have
  different chemical and physical properties.
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 3.1 — Why are silicon–silicon bonds generally weaker than carbon–carbon bonds?

#### Structure 2.2.8 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** The nature of the force that exists between molecules is determined by the size and
  polarity of the molecules. Intermolecular forces include London (dispersion), dipole–induced dipole,
  dipole–dipole and hydrogen bonding.
- **Outcome:** Deduce the types of intermolecular force present from the structural features of covalent
  molecules.
- **Guidance:** The term "**van der Waals forces**" should be used as an inclusive term covering
  dipole–dipole, dipole–induced dipole and London (dispersion) forces. Hydrogen bonds occur when hydrogen,
  covalently bonded to an electronegative atom, has an attractive interaction with a neighbouring
  electronegative atom.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Structure 1.5 — To what extent can intermolecular forces explain the deviation of real gases from ideal
    behaviour?
  - Nature of science, Structure 1.1, 2.1, 2.3 — How do the terms "bonds" and "forces" compare?
  - Nature of science — How can advances in technology lead to changes in scientific definitions, e.g. the
    updated IUPAC definition of the hydrogen bond?

#### Structure 2.2.9 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Given comparable molar mass, the relative strengths of intermolecular forces are
  generally: London (dispersion) forces < dipole–dipole forces < hydrogen bonding.
- **Outcome:** Explain the physical properties of covalent substances, to include volatility, electrical
  conductivity and solubility, in terms of their structure.
- **Guidance:** none.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Tool 1, Inquiry 2 — What experimental data demonstrate the physical properties of covalent substances?
  - Structure 3.2 — To what extent does a functional group determine the nature of the intermolecular
    forces?

#### Structure 2.2.10 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Chromatography is a technique used to separate the components of a mixture based on
  their relative attractions, involving intermolecular forces, to mobile and stationary phases.
- **Outcome:** Explain, calculate and interpret the retardation factor values, RF.
- **Guidance:** Knowledge of the use of locating agents in chromatography is not required. The technical and
  operational details of a gas chromatograph or high-performance liquid chromatograph will not be assessed.
- **Linking question:**
  - Tool 1 — How can a mixture be separated using paper chromatography or thin layer chromatography (TLC)?

#### Structure 2.2.11 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Resonance structures occur when there is more than one possible position for a double
  bond in a molecule.
- **Outcome:** Deduce resonance structures of molecules and ions.
- **Guidance:** Include the term "delocalization".
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 1.3 — Why are oxygen and ozone dissociated by different wavelengths of light?

#### Structure 2.2.12 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Benzene, C₆H₆, is an important example of a molecule that has resonance.
- **Outcome:** Discuss the structure of benzene from physical and chemical evidence.
- **Guidance:** none.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Reactivity 2.1, 2.2 — How does the resonance energy in benzene explain its relative unreactivity?
  - Reactivity 3.4 — What are the structural features of benzene that favour it undergoing electrophilic
    substitution reactions?

#### Structure 2.2.13 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Some atoms can form molecules in which they have an expanded octet of electrons.
- **Outcomes:** Visually represent Lewis formulas for species with **five and six electron domains** around
  the central atom. Deduce the electron domain geometry and the molecular geometry for these species using
  the VSEPR model.
- **Guidance:** none.
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 3.1 — How does the ability of some atoms to expand their octet relate to their position in the
    periodic table?

#### Structure 2.2.14 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Formal charge values can be calculated for each atom in a species and used to determine
  which of several possible Lewis formulas is preferred.
- **Outcome:** Apply formal charge to determine a preferred Lewis formula from different Lewis formulas for
  a species.
- **Guidance:** none.
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 3.1, Reactivity 3.2 — What are the different assumptions made in the calculation of formal
    charge and of oxidation states for atoms in a species?

#### Structure 2.2.15 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Sigma bonds (σ) form by the head-on combination of atomic orbitals, with the electron
  density concentrated along the bond axis. Pi bonds (π) form by the lateral combination of p-orbitals, with
  the electron density concentrated on opposite sides of the bond axis.
- **Outcome:** Deduce the presence of sigma bonds and pi bonds in molecules and ions.
- **Guidance:** Include both organic and inorganic examples.
- **Linking questions:** none listed.

#### Structure 2.2.16 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Hybridization is the concept of mixing atomic orbitals to form new hybrid orbitals for
  bonding.
- **Outcomes:** Analyse the hybridization and bond formation in molecules and ions. Identify the
  relationships between Lewis formulas, electron domains, molecular geometry and type of hybridization.
  Predict the geometry around an atom from its hybridization, and vice versa.
- **Guidance:** Include both organic and inorganic examples. **Only sp, sp² and sp³ hybridization need to be
  covered.**
- **Linking questions:** none listed.

Boundary notes for 2.2:

- **SL+HL Lewis formulas stop at four electron pairs per atom** (octet or fewer, e.g. BF₃, BeCl₂ as
  "fewer than an octet"). Five and six pairs (PCl₅, SF₆, XeF₄, etc.) are AHL (2.2.13).
- **SL+HL VSEPR stops at four electron domains**; five and six domains are AHL. See section 7 for the
  resulting shape lists.
- **Resonance is entirely AHL**, including the word "delocalization" in the covalent context. At SL+HL a
  species such as CO₃²⁻, NO₃⁻ or O₃ can be given one valid Lewis formula; the equal-bond-length /
  delocalization discussion is HL **[INFERENCE]**. (Delocalized electrons in *metals* and in graphite are
  SL+HL via 2.3.1 and 2.2.7.)
- **Formal charge is AHL.** The SL+HL Lewis outcome does not require choosing between alternative formulas
  by formal charge.
- **Sigma/pi and hybridization are AHL.** At SL+HL, single/double/triple bonds are described only as one,
  two or three shared pairs (2.2.2), and graphite/graphene/diamond are explained without hybridization
  language **[INFERENCE]**; an HL page may add it, labelled HL.
- **Hybridization for expanded octets is excluded** by the "only sp, sp² and sp³" guidance: no sp³d or
  sp³d². So for a 5- or 6-domain central atom, students deduce geometry by VSEPR but are not asked for a
  hybridization label.
- **Bond polarity by electronegativity difference.** The guide gives no numeric cut-offs (such as 0.4 and
  1.8) for non-polar / polar / ionic. Any threshold on the site is **[UNVERIFIED]** convention; the bonding
  triangle (2.4) is the guide's own tool for this.
- **Intermolecular force names.** The guide's four names are London (dispersion), dipole–induced dipole,
  dipole–dipole and hydrogen bonding; "van der Waals" is the umbrella for the first three and **excludes
  hydrogen bonding**. Do not use "van der Waals" as a synonym for London forces only.
- **Hydrogen bond definition.** The guide says "an electronegative atom" and does **not** restrict it to
  N, O and F. The N/O/F rule is the usual teaching simplification **[UNVERIFIED as a markscheme
  requirement]**; present it as "typically N, O or F".
- **Chromatography (2.2.10)**: paper and TLC, phases, relative attractions, RF. Calculation is
  RF = distance moved by the component ÷ distance moved by the solvent front **[UNVERIFIED wording — the
  formula is not printed in the guide or the booklet]**.

### Structure 2.3 — The metallic model

**Guiding question:** What determines the metallic nature and properties of an element?
SL+HL 2 hours. AHL 1 hour.

#### Structure 2.3.1 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** A metallic bond is the electrostatic attraction between a lattice of cations and
  delocalized electrons.
- **Outcome:** Explain the electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity and malleability of metals.
- **Guidance:** Relate characteristic properties of metals to their uses.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Tool 1, Inquiry 2, Structure 3.1 — What experimental data demonstrate the physical properties of metals,
    and trends in these properties, in the periodic table?
  - Reactivity 3.2 — What trends in reactivity of metals can be predicted from the periodic table?

#### Structure 2.3.2 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** The strength of a metallic bond depends on the charge of the ions and the radius of the
  metal ion.
- **Outcome:** Explain trends in melting points of **s and p block metals**.
- **Guidance:** A simple treatment in terms of charge of cations and electron density is required.
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 2.4 — What are the features of metallic bonding that make it possible for metals to form
    alloys?

#### Structure 2.3.3 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Transition elements have delocalized d-electrons.
- **Outcome:** Explain the high melting point and electrical conductivity of transition elements.
- **Guidance:** Chemical properties of transition elements are covered in Reactivity 3.4.
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 3.1 — Why is the trend in melting points of metals across a period less evident across the
    d-block?

Boundary notes for 2.3:

- The 2.3.3 guidance points to Reactivity 3.4 (complex-ion formation, 3.4.8). The characteristic properties
  of transition elements (variable oxidation state, colour, catalysis, magnetism, complex ions) are in fact
  listed in **Structure 3.1.8–3.1.10 (AHL)**; their redox chemistry is Reactivity 3.2 **[INFERENCE: the
  guide's cross-reference is narrower than its own content]**.
- 2.3.2 is restricted to **s- and p-block metals** (e.g. Na < Mg < Al across period 3; decreasing down
  group 1). d-block melting points are only the AHL 2.3.3.

### Structure 2.4 — From models to materials

**Guiding question:** What role do bonding and structure have in the design of materials?
SL+HL 4 hours. AHL 1 hour.

#### Structure 2.4.1 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Bonding is best described as a continuum between the ionic, covalent and metallic
  models, and can be represented by a bonding triangle.
- **Outcome:** Use bonding models to explain the properties of a material.
- **Guidance:** A triangular bonding diagram is provided in the data booklet.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Structure 3.1 — How do the trends in properties of period 3 oxides reflect the trend in their bonding?
  - Nature of science, Structures 2.1, 2.2 — What are the limitations of discrete bonding categories?

#### Structure 2.4.2 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** The position of a compound in the bonding triangle is determined by the relative
  contributions of the three bonding types to the overall bond.
- **Outcomes:** Determine the position of a compound in the bonding triangle from electronegativity data.
  Predict the properties of a compound based on its position in the bonding triangle.
- **Guidance:** To illustrate the relationship between bonding type and properties, include example
  materials of varying percentage bonding character. **Only binary compounds** need to be considered.
  **Calculations of percentage ionic character are not required.** Electronegativity data are given in the
  data booklet.
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 2.1, 2.2, 2.3 — Why do composites like reinforced concretes, which are made from ionic and
    covalently bonded components and steel bars, have unique properties?

#### Structure 2.4.3 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Alloys are mixtures of a metal and other metals or non-metals. They have enhanced
  properties.
- **Outcome:** Explain the properties of alloys in terms of non-directional bonding.
- **Guidance:** Illustrate with common examples such as bronze, brass and stainless steel. **Specific
  examples of alloys do not have to be learned.**
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 1.1 — Why are alloys more correctly described as mixtures rather than as compounds?

#### Structure 2.4.4 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Polymers are large molecules, or macromolecules, made from repeating subunits called
  monomers.
- **Outcome:** Describe the common properties of plastics in terms of their structure.
- **Guidance:** Examples of natural and synthetic polymers should be discussed.
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 3.2 — What are the structural features of some plastics that make them biodegradable?

#### Structure 2.4.5 (SL+HL)

- **Understanding:** Addition polymers form by the breaking of a double bond in each monomer.
- **Outcome:** Represent the repeating unit of an addition polymer from given monomer structures.
- **Guidance:** Examples should include polymerization reactions of alkenes. Structures of monomers do not
  have to be learned; they will be provided or will need to be deduced from the polymer.
- **Linking questions:**
  - Structure 3.2 — What functional groups in molecules can enable them to act as monomers for addition
    reactions?
  - Reactivity 2.1 — Why is the atom economy 100% for an addition polymerization reaction?

#### Structure 2.4.6 — **Additional HL (AHL)**

- **Understanding:** Condensation polymers form by the reaction between functional groups in each monomer,
  with the release of a small molecule.
- **Outcome:** Represent the repeating unit of **polyamides and polyesters** from given monomer structures.
- **Guidance:** All biological macromolecules form by condensation reactions and break down by hydrolysis.
- **Linking question:**
  - Structure 3.2 — What functional groups in molecules can enable them to act as monomers for condensation
    reactions?

Boundary notes for 2.4:

- **Addition polymers are SL+HL; condensation polymers are AHL.** In both, the monomer structures are
  *given* (or deduced from the polymer); the skill is drawing the repeating unit (and, by the 2.4.5
  guidance, going from polymer back to monomer).
- The bonding triangle needs two numbers from booklet electronegativities: the **difference** Δχ (vertical
  axis) and the **average** (horizontal axis) — see section 4c. Binary compounds only; elements sit on the
  base (Δχ = 0) **[INFERENCE]**.
- Composites and reinforced concrete appear only in a linking question; they are not a required material
  class.

Standard explanation patterns the outcomes ask for **[UNVERIFIED as markscheme wording; content follows the
guide's models]**:

- **Ionic properties (2.1.3):** low volatility / high melting point — strong electrostatic attraction
  between oppositely charged ions throughout the lattice; conduct only when molten or in aqueous solution —
  ions are free to move (ions, not electrons); often soluble in polar solvents such as water, insoluble in
  non-polar solvents.
- **Covalent properties (2.2.9):** molecular substances — volatility set by intermolecular forces, not by
  covalent bonds; do not conduct (no mobile charged particles); "like dissolves like". Network substances —
  very high melting points because covalent bonds must be broken; insoluble; non-conductors except graphite
  and graphene (delocalized electrons).
- **Metals (2.3.1):** electrical conductivity — delocalized electrons are free to move; thermal
  conductivity — delocalized electrons (and closely packed cations) transfer kinetic energy; malleability —
  layers of cations can slide without breaking the bond because the bonding is non-directional.
- **Metallic bond strength (2.3.2):** higher cation charge and more delocalized electrons per atom, and
  smaller ionic radius (higher charge density), give stronger attraction and a higher melting point.
- **Alloys (2.4.3):** bonding is non-directional, so atoms of other elements can be accommodated in the
  lattice; different-sized atoms disrupt the regular layers, so layers slide less easily (harder, stronger).

- Required reasoning (2.2.4 guidance): non-bonding pairs repel more than bonding pairs, so bond angles

### 7c. Benzene (AHL, 2.2.12) — evidence expected for "discuss"

- Physical: all six C–C bonds are the same length, intermediate between C–C (154 pm) and C=C (134 pm);

- Model: six sp² carbons, each with a p-orbital; six π electrons delocalized above and below the ring.

- Using old electronegativity values instead of the 2025 booklet values.
- Quoting lattice enthalpy as exothermic (ion-formation convention). The 2025 booklet defines it as the
  endothermic separation of the lattice into gaseous ions.

## 9. Scope rules for page authors and the auditor

1. A statement is "required" only if it maps to an understanding, outcome or guidance line in section 2.
2. Content in 3a or 3b must not be presented as required. If included at all, label it "Beyond the
   syllabus".
3. Content in 3c may be mentioned as a signpost ("you will meet this in Reactivity 1.2") but not taught or
   tested as Structure 2 content.
4. Label as HL-only: 2.2.11, 2.2.12, 2.2.13, 2.2.14, 2.2.15, 2.2.16, 2.3.3, 2.4.6, and transition element
   complexes within 2.2.3. Everything else in Structure 2 is common to SL and HL. **Nothing in 2.1 is
   HL-only**; lattice enthalpy on a 2.1 page is qualitative and unlabelled, with a signpost to Born–Haber
   (Reactivity 1.2.5, HL).
5. On SL+HL pages or sections, keep Lewis formulas to at most four electron pairs per atom and VSEPR to at
   most four domains; do not use formal charge, resonance, σ/π or hybridization to explain SL+HL content.
6. Use the guide's vocabulary: "Lewis formula", "coordination bond", "electron domain", "electron domain
   geometry", "molecular geometry", "non-bonding pair", "London (dispersion) forces", "retardation factor",
   "covalent network structure", "repeating unit".
7. Use booklet electronegativity, bond length, bond enthalpy and lattice enthalpy values as in section 4,
   and read the bonding triangle from the booklet diagram. Section 4 is from a mirror: do not present it as
   officially confirmed.
8. Named examples (specific alloys, polymers, fullerenes, complexes, VSEPR example molecules) are
   illustrations, not required recall, unless the guide names them: the seven polyatomic ions (2.1.2), the
   six network/allotrope substances (2.2.7), benzene (2.2.12).
9. Anything marked [UNVERIFIED] must not be presented as official IB wording; phrase it as common practice
   or check it against a past markscheme first. This includes VSEPR shape names and angles, the formal
   charge formula and the RF formula.
10. Linking questions are connections, not extra syllabus statements.
